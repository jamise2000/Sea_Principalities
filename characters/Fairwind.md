# Prince Fairwind

Prince of Fairwind, one of the five rulers of the Hold — and, at the story's opening, the traitor at the heart of the Helm Island crisis. A childhood friend of Prince Jeon turned self-serving schemer, styled both "Lord Fairwind" and "Prince Fairwind," he is the man who sold the Sea Principalities' deepest secret for gold and grievance, then tried to talk his way back out of the noose. An NPC and the story's immediate antagonist.

## Physical Description and Bearing

Fairwind is a fairly big man, broad enough that Paul weighs whether he can even be carried, and he wears his size the way he wears his title — as a matter of station. He carries himself as an aristocrat of the outer isles born to command, and even bound to a chair in a stormed keep he does not lose the posture. His first words to his captor drip with condescension — "you were below my station, young man" — and he speaks throughout with the smooth, unhurried assurance of a politician who has never doubted his right to be heard.

That composure is his most striking feature. Where a lesser man would panic, Fairwind stays cordial, arrogant, and talkative under the wand and the bow, arguing his case, issuing veiled threats, and offering deals in the same even voice. It is a bearing built for the council chamber, and he deploys it just as readily in a cavern full of enemies — the practiced ease of a man who believes he can negotiate his way out of anything.

That bearing works on people like a charm in its own right. Plain soldiers and open-hearted men — Tyrus, Folsom, and even the wary Merrick among them — instinctively grant him the deference owed a countryman and a lord, softening toward him almost against their own judgment; they hear a besieged nobleman where they should hear a traitor. It takes something specific to resist it: Gouge's flat mistrust, Paul's certain knowledge of the treason, Jude's sheer indifference. Fairwind is **no spellcaster** — this is the weight of station and personality, not sorcery — but its effect on a room can be much the same, and it should be written that way: those three men see through him; the rest are half-won before he has finished his first sentence.

## Station and Responsibilities

Fairwind rules the island that bears his name, one of the three great Outer Islands of the Hold — Fairwind, Flotsam, and Jetsom — that lie off the mainland's east coast, home to the rich trade families of the Sea Princes and their sprawling manor estates. Unlike the Prince of Monmurg or the Prince of Toli, the princes of the outer isles do not inherit their thrones by blood; they are elected by the commodores of their island and, once chosen, serve for life. In that office Fairwind holds a full voice in the council of five that governs the Hold, and serves as both military leader and chief magistrate of his island.

Crucially, he also held command of Helm Island at the moment of its fall. Command of that fortified rock in the center of the Strait of Monmurg — the traditional seat where the princes and commodores plan their naval campaigns, and the chief storehouse of the anti-sahaugin powder — rotates among Monmurg and the three outer isles, and it was Fairwind's turn to hold the Helm. That posting placed the Hold's most closely guarded secret and its largest stockpile of the powder directly in his hands, and it is exactly that trust he betrayed.

## Personality

Fairwind is, above all, a politician — glib, self-assured, and endlessly persuasive, a man who reaches for a bargain before a blade. Even as a prisoner he negotiates: offering to order his men to cooperate, promising to argue his case before the princes, insisting he serves Jamis and Jeon "same as you." He is arrogant and conscious of rank, quick to remind others of his station and to threaten that they'll regret crossing him, yet he can turn that arrogance off in an instant when charm serves him better.

His cunning is real but shallow, and shot through with self-deception. Captured, he had already tied himself up to fake being a coerced hostage, spinning his willing dealings with the Toli as duress. Escorted out under Folsom's charm, he worked the gentle bard masterfully — flattering him as a "very nice young man," separating the "good, honest, loyal men" from the "cutthroats," and planting an elaborate counter-accusation that the true traitor is Lord Jamis, through his wife Lady Azane and her brother Zafar Azane of Gradsul's Office of Forbidden Magic. He cast himself throughout as the protector of the innocent, begging Folsom to carry word to his family and to Paul should he die. Whether he believes his own version is genuinely unclear; by Jamis's estimation he does, at least in part — Fairwind thinks he is merely "playing politics," never grasping that he is playing it with people who play for keeps.

That is the paradox others see in him. Jamis judges him a "witless fool," a man out of his depth who has no real understanding of the Scarlet Brotherhood or the danger Cain Toli represents, and who therefore should not be killed but perhaps someday won back. Merrick, who knows and is respected by him, reads his defensive bluster and his attack on Paul as the marks of a guilty man rather than a mastermind. He is slippery to the last: in the escape on the narrow bridge he shoved Folsom, pitched Paul into the water, and went over the side himself into the shark-filled chasm — and his fate remains genuinely uncertain, some reporting his body taken by a shark, others finding only soft footprints leading east and no trace of a drowned man, leaving open whether he perished or slipped away yet again.

## Motivations

Fairwind's treason is, at bottom, a matter of gold and grievance rather than grand ideology. His arrangement with the Toli was transactional: he siphoned the powder from the Helm for months, kept a private stash on his own island, and let Cain Toli's Sea Ghost carry off the rest, in exchange for the Toli vouching for him on the council and pressuring Prince Jeon to grant him a greater share of the profits from Monmurg's lucrative trade. Jamis reads his true motive plainly as personal enrichment and leverage — the self-interest of a man looking out for his own island above the Hold.

Beneath the greed runs an older resentment: a long, simmering jealousy of Monmurg's success, its wealth and taxes and prosperity so far outstripping the outer isles despite their shared piratical roots. In his own mind Fairwind may not see himself as a traitor to the Principalities at all, but as aligned with his fellow princes against a Monmurg that is really the one working against everyone else. He genuinely opposes Jeon's campaign to end the Amedio slave trade, having argued many times in council that provoking the Duke of Berghof over it is reckless — a position that, Jamis concedes, is not without its point.

And threaded through all of it is the plainest motive of all: self-preservation. Once cornered, everything Fairwind does — the faked bonds, the counter-accusations against Jamis, the promises to keep the "innocent" alive, the leap into the sea — bends toward one end, which is to survive the night and live to argue his case another day. Whether he drowned in that chasm or swam free, surviving was the only cause he ever truly served.
