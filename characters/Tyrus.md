# Tyrus Leon Mare

A giant of a Marine, beloved across the Monmurg fleet — boisterous, charismatic, and shrewder than his size suggests. Others see only a very large, enthusiastic soldier loyal to the prince's cause; beneath that is a paladin whose true oath is sworn not to any prince but to the city of Monmurg and its people.

## Physical Description

Tyrus is a big man, large even for a human — six foot four and around two hundred and forty pounds, an imposing wall of muscle who tends to be the first thing a room notices. That size has shaped his life: he is forever the man some smaller fellow in a tavern wants to test himself against, and he has learned to carry the bulk with an easy, good-humored confidence rather than menace.

He is armed and armored as a Marine — breastplate and shield, a longsword at his side — and fights from behind that shield, holding ground and choke points with his body. But what people remember is not the gear; it is the presence. He is boisterous and charismatic, the sort everyone in the fleet seems to love, and he wears that warmth openly. To most eyes he reads as simply a great, enthusiastic fighter; the deeper current of conviction beneath it is not something he advertises.

## History

Tyrus was born to a very young mother who worked as a servant in the household of a Monmurg elite family. She had a gift for storytelling, and after long days serving at the endless parties and dinners of the wealthy, she would come home and spin elaborate tales for her son, stitched together from fragments she overheard from prominent guests and embellished with whatever she didn't know — Tyrus slaying mythical beasts, assassination plots, perilous voyages, each with a thread of truth buried inside. She told the same kind of stories about his father: that he was a great soldier who fought in famous battles, a pirate of wealth and fame. But she ended every one of them the same way — "one day, when you're old enough, I will tell you a real story about him" — and she died before that day came, leaving the truth of his father a mystery he still carries.

They lived poor, in the loft of his great-uncle Gaius's house. Gaius charged no rent but expected work, and he was a hard man — not cruel exactly, but intimidating, ruling by sheer will and presence; his word was law, and men learned there was a price for crossing "Gaius's Law." Gaius ran a small shipping and freight co-op — two little ships and a warehouse off the docks, moving cargo between the islands — and Tyrus grew up in it, first as a dock runner, then, thanks to his size, sailing the ships by the age of eight. When Gaius landed a contract with Jeon II to move military supplies and troops out to the islands, Tyrus fell in love with the weapons and armor that passed through his hands, pestering soldiers to teach him their use. As he grew larger he became a target for men wanting to prove something, and earned his lessons in more than a few scrapes. He never passed up a chance to learn — most of all battle tactics, land and sea, coaxing old soldiers and sailors into recounting how great battles were won and lost, sketching mock engagements with rocks and buttons for armies, and picking up the unglamorous art of how you actually supply and move a fighting force.

At around thirteen, he came home from a trip near Fairwind to find that Gaius and his mother had died together when the house burned down around them. Days later, one of Gaius's partners was stabbed coming home from the pub, leaving the last partner, Kakuros, in sole possession of both ships and the warehouse — which he built into a large shipping company. A new captain and crew pushed Tyrus off the ships he'd grown up on. He spent years as a deckhand running to Port Toli and other ports, and more recently has kept mostly to land, working the Monmurg docks and befriending the city's soldiers and guards. Now a well-known and well-liked figure in the Marine fleet, he is sent out with his unit on the Helm Island expedition, where he holds the line in the bridge fighting, helps expose Fairwind's treachery, and comes home already looking for the next way to help the starving city — drawn to Owen Black's smuggling runs to Red Shore as a chance to bring food home.

## Personality Traits

Tyrus is warm, loud, and magnetic — a natural morale-lifter whose favorite thing in the world is listening to his companion Folsom play, and who is fiercely protective of the bard, quietly running damage control when Folsom's songs land wrong. For all his bulk, his first instinct is not violence. Having spent a lifetime as the big man everyone wants to fight, he has concluded that the best way to win a fight is not to be in one, and he leans on persuasion, redirection, and sheer intimidating presence to settle trouble — though he is not above throwing hands when it comes to that.

He is far shrewder than the "big friendly soldier" impression suggests. Raised on overheard intrigue and self-taught in tactics and logistics, he thinks in terms of strategy, supply, and the movements of powers, and he reads people and politics carefully. His views are pragmatic to the point of coldness in places: he despises the Port Toli slave trade, but frames his objection in ledgers rather than morals, arguing that men led by motivation and loyalty simply produce better results than the enslaved, and that the trade squanders what could otherwise be a great city.

Underneath the good cheer runs the steel of a paladin. When Fairwind's account throws suspicion toward Lord Jamis himself, Tyrus does not flinch or bluster — he states plainly where he stands: his loyalty runs *through* Jamis, but his true loyalty is to Monmurg itself, and no chain of command outranks that. He is measured in the face of unsettling news, unwilling to condemn on rumor, but immovable on the one point that matters to him. His conviction is his spine, even when he keeps it wrapped in a joke and a round of drinks.

## Motivations

Tyrus's deepest allegiance is to Monmurg — the city, its common people, and the three islands he grew up sailing between. He holds the outer isles especially dear, having spent so much of his life among them, and he understands their strategic weight: Monmurg sits on a finger of land that could be cut off from the mainland, and the islands are the shield that guards it. That devotion is more than sentiment; it functions as his sacred oath, the cause his paladin's power answers to, ranking above any single prince or patron. In the present crisis it drives him toward anything that keeps the city alive, from retaking the Helm to smuggling food past the blockade.

Running quietly beneath that is the unfinished business of his own past — the "real story" of his father that his mother never lived to tell him, and a sense that the thread of truth in all her tales is still out there to be found. The fire that took her and Gaius, and the convenient way their deaths handed the family business to another man, are wounds that have never fully closed.

And there is the simpler, sturdier motive of a man who was pushed off the ships he grew up on and has been making his own way ever since: to build something, to matter, to be valued for more than his size. He seeks camaraderie and loyalty — the bonds with Folsom, with his fellow Marines, with the city that raised him — and he wants, in the end, to be the kind of man the true stories are told about, rather than the invented ones.
