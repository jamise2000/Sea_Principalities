# Horace Baldpate

A sword-for-hire out of Onwal who served as Jude's paid companion on the long road from Idee to Monmurg — a broad, unhurried mercenary hired as much for his knowledge of the western ports as for his blade. He is the man at Jude's shoulder through the whole of Part Zero, and the man Jude parts from — honestly, in full, and on good terms — once the journey is done. A minor figure who never learns the half of what he is escorting, and who steps out of the story not into thin air but toward the Harbor District, with a standing offer to come back if Jude ever needs a blade.

## Physical Description

Horace is a big man, broad through the chest, built along the lines of someone who has made his living with a sword and expects to go on doing so. The manuscript spends little on his face; what it fixes on instead is his manner — a physical unhurriedness, a way of holding still and letting a moment pass before he offers an opinion on it. His surname, Baldpate, is the plainest thing about him and the most literal.

He carries himself as the sort of hired man who blends into a crowd rather than commands one — an ordinary, forgettable, capable presence, the kind of man a crowded, hungry city absorbs without a ripple, easy to overlook and easier still to lose track of.

## History

Horace is a mercenary out of Onwal, a seasoned sword-for-hire — an experienced, mid-ranked fighting man rather than a green one or a famed one. He had been at Jude's shoulder for the better part of a year by the time the story opens, hired for the journey west out of Scant and prized less for his swordsmanship than for a rarer virtue: he knows the ports of the western world, and he had never once asked why an elf wizard wanted to reach a city under siege.

He booked and shared the passage on the Onwalian trader that carried Jude toward Monmurg deep in the month of Flocktime, trading the geography and politics of the Azure Sea for wine and company across the crossing. When two Keoland men-of-war intercepted the trader and diverted it to Redshore under the Duke of Gradsul's embargo, it was Horace who read the deck and brought the news back. At the Earl of Redshore's castle he was fed in the kitchens while Jude was received in the hall — the line between principal and hireling drawn plainly by where each man ate. He hauled a crate of wine on poles beside Jude like a common porter through the two miles into Ensar, waited patiently on the Bailey steps in Monmurg while Jude was taken in to Lord Jamis, and carried that same much-traveled crate to a corner table at Ferd's Inn.

There his service ended — amicably, and without ceremony. Horace carried the wine as far as Ferd's and then made plain he had no wish to stay: the inn's listening walls and back-room schemes were Jude's trade, not his. Jude squared his wages honestly and in full, and Horace took a room down nearer the Harbor District, where a man who knew ships could find honest work along the wharves while the war lasted. They parted on good terms, Horace leaving Jude a standing invitation — "come find me if you ever want a blade at your back" — that closed no doors between them, though the manuscript does not follow him past it.

## Personality Traits

Horace is unhurried in the truest sense — slow to speak, slow to alarm, economical with his opinions and comfortable withholding them. He delivers bad news flatly, "with the particular blankness of a man delivering news he didn't like," neither dramatizing a setback nor pretending it away. That steadiness is his defining quality; where Jude runs hot with schemes and second-guesses, Horace simply reports the weather and waits.

He is worldly to the point of quiet cynicism, but without bitterness. His view of the room he is about to walk into — a bar full of men who would sell what they hear to whichever side pays first — is answered with an unbothered "I generally assume that's true everywhere," a line dry enough to draw a rare laugh from Jude. He observes his employer more closely than he lets on ("You're thinking again. You do that face."), and reads a situation shrewdly, but keeps his conclusions light and offhand, as with his one verdict on the Earl of Redshore: "An interesting man."

His central professional virtue is discretion, and it is deliberate rather than incidental. He does not ask why. He is content to be told "more of the same conversation we had on the ship" and to leave the rest unexamined, and this incuriosity is precisely what makes him useful — and what keeps him alive, since he never learns enough of Jude's real business to be worth silencing. He is loyal in the plain, transactional way of a good hireling: reliable while paid, sober and watching the door when told to be, and untroubled when the contract ends.

## Motivations

Horace's motivations are honest and uncomplicated, which in this story sets him apart. He works for pay, does the job in front of him, and expects the arrangement to end when the job does — no grander loyalty, no hidden agenda, no ambition beyond the next berth. He takes his wages, takes Jude's counsel about bettering himself in a hungry city, and moves on without resentment or attachment.

Beneath the paycheck runs a simpler want: to keep his own counsel and stay out of other men's schemes. He chose a trade that asks no questions and rewards a shut mouth, and Ferd's back rooms held no appeal for him — he would rather earn an honest wage along the Harbor District wharves than be drawn into whatever Jude was building. He wants, in the end, the freedom of the unremarkable man: to work, mind his business, and keep clear of trouble that isn't his. But he leaves on good terms rather than slipping away, a blade Jude can call on again if the need is plain enough.
