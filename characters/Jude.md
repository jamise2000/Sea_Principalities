# Jude

An elf mage newly returned to Monmurg, working in the shadows as a covert agent of Lord Jamis. Where others in the sea principalities carry a title or a rank, Jude carries a wand, a familiar, and a long memory for who owes whom. He is not Jamis's knife — that is Gouge's part; Jude's charge is subtler, to see that Prince Jeon's nephew comes home alive.

## Physical Description

Jude is an elf, though the fact reveals itself in glimpses rather than announcements — shadowed, shaded features, the point of an ear, an accent hard to place. A frightened prisoner, studying him in poor light, can tell only that this is an elf and little more.

His face carries a contradiction that others notice before they can name it. Paul Revero, looking at him plainly, tells him he seems "morally young, maybe younger than me, and more world-weary than anyone I've ever seen." The youth is elven and unearned; the weariness is not. It sits in the eyes and in the way he holds himself, a man who has already spent a great deal and expects to spend more. The strangeness in those eyes — the slit pupil that opens snake-wide in poor light — runs older than any of his history. The Scarlet Brotherhood's transmuter did leave its mark on him (a temple raided with Karmirg, a season trapped in a serpent's shape; Ch. 4, Ch. 11), but the Brotherhood only found and worried at something already in him — it did not make him. Jude was never an ordinary elf; whatever lies under that face is older than the grudge, and he turns the question aside with a joke — "a dragon in the family tree" — and lets no one nearer than the joke allows.

He favors anonymity and the tools of it. He is drawn to the intimidation a mask affords — the shrouded, unreadable face that makes a mark sweat — and he keeps to disguise, invisibility, and the dim corners of the foreign district where a face is a liability. In a fight he prefers not to be on the ground at all: his instinct is to levitate above the melee and work his magic from the air while others hold the earth below him.

## History

Jude has walked Monmurg's streets before, but he is, at the story's opening, freshly returned — back perhaps a week, no more than a month and a half, after roughly a year abroad. The city he has come back to is a city under a noose: Keoland's blockade has strangled the trade that fed it, Port Torvin's granaries are ash, and hunger has made the foreign district a tinderbox.

He is not new to Lord Jamis. He worked as Jamis's agent before this return, and that old patronage is the thread he has picked back up. His current business began with the trail of Lord Phranck — an inquiry that led him to an alchemist's shop in the foreign district, where he was followed by a pursuer he could not see. Only by turning invisible himself did he shake the tail, and the encounter left him certain that whoever hunts in Monmurg's alleys now has access to mixed magic.

He lives rough by choice, keeping a thumb on the pulse of the foreign district. He had lodged at Ferd's Inn — "Ferd's" — a place he once ran out on after humiliating and nearly killing a ruffian to win back his room. Abroad, in Idee, he had been given a tower by Lord Anlauf Ogmotton, already half destroyed by the time it came to him (Book 2 Ch. 2); in Monmurg he has only his room at Ferd's. In reward for his service on Helm Island, Jamis offers him a tower overlooking the sea and access to the Royal Library — patronage Jude accepts without hesitation.

Sent to Helm Island as the hidden blade beside Prince Jeon's nephew Paul, Jude scaled the citadel's seaward wall by levitating his companions up through the dark, cut down two Toli agents in Lord Fairwind's inner sanctum, and confirmed with his own eyes that the barrel room had been emptied — the stores of the yellow anti-sahaugin substance siphoned off for months and shipped aboard Cain Toli's Sea Ghost. He pocketed some of the powder to study. He came home on a corsair with twenty surviving marines out of two hundred, and with a new set of standing orders from Jamis: research the substance and what negated it, learn the nature of the Toli artifact that drowns Monmurg's ships, and take the reluctant Paul Revero on as a pupil in the arcane arts.

## Personality Traits

Jude is a planner before he is a fighter. Handed a captured thug, his mind runs immediately to leverage and contingency — send the homunculus ahead in case traitors silence the prisoner first, arrange the meeting place before any deal is struck, follow the man home afterward to see whether he sells them out or hides, hold his family if it comes to that. He thinks in surveillance and second moves, and he plays what he openly calls "the long game": his scheme to buy a still, brew liquor from the noble houses' cast-off peels and rot, and turn Ferd's Inn into a place where loose, drunken tongues do his listening for him.

He is ruthless, but coldly and deliberately so rather than out of appetite. He will kill, take hostages, and trade in people's fear, yet he is quick to distinguish his own calculation from Paul's impulsiveness, and he argues that terror is a poor motivator because a frightened man will simply sell your secret to the next buyer. When Jamis warns him to suspect everyone, Jude finishes the sentence for him — it is already how he lives.

Socially he is a needler and a talker, endlessly probing. He circles back again and again to the mystery of Paul's mask, prying at the man's past with a mix of genuine curiosity and sport. His humor is dry and sardonic — deflecting the foreign district's misery with a joke about onions and vampires, or picturing himself as a badly disguised hobgoblin floating over a wall. Beneath the banter runs an old, guarded wound he keeps entirely off the page — the weariness Paul reads in his eyes (Book 2 Ch. 6). His needling can be cruel: on the dungeon stair he mocks the "thing" *Paul* carries — "Mother's dead. Father never loved me" — and Paul's flat "You don't know that" is the answer (Book 2 Ch. 6; corrected from an earlier draft that had these roles reversed, per the cleaned `Jude_and_Paul_process_the_day` transcript).

He is vigilant to the edge of paranoia, walking the city on high alert for a knife in a side street, alive to the possibility that any encounter is a test. And he has settled, almost against his own grain, into the role of teacher — a demanding, tough-love mentor who promises to "poke and prod and kick" Paul into something better, half-brutal in his methods (a brass bowl to the shins, chairs broken and mended) but plainly invested in the man he is charged with keeping alive.

## Motivations

Jude's clearest hunger is for arcane knowledge. He lights up at the promise of a proper library, pockets unknown powder to break down "for his own edification," and takes evident satisfaction in the puzzle Jamis sets him — reverse-engineering the yellow substance the Duke of Berghof once supplied, and unraveling the artifact by which the Toli command turtle dragons to swallow Monmurg's fleet. The work is a duty, but it is also plainly a pleasure.

Running alongside that is naked, unembarrassed self-advancement. He knows what patronage is worth in desperate times, and he trades on it without shame — angling for a magic ring, accepting the seaside tower and library access, understanding that being Jamis's man is the surest ladder available to him. Jamis is candid that this is not trust but mutual use, and Jude is comfortable on those terms.

He also holds a private theory of what makes a wizard: *hunger*, not for bread but for knowledge, power, and more than one has been allowed to be. Without it, talent stalls, like the hedge-wizard who sells light for coppers. He sees that hunger in Paul (a good mind, a probable gift, a lifetime shut out, and the blood and position to open doors) and weighs Paul both as a future patron for when Jamis dies and as a wizard of his own making. He doubts the hunger will win against Paul's conscience, which he blames on Paul's mother, and he finds himself curious which way it will go (Book 2 Ch. 5).

His third motive is simple survival in a game where, as he puts it, the other players are "playing for keeps." He wants to stay a step ahead of the Toli, the Scarlet Brotherhood, and whatever unseen hand turns invisible in the alleys behind him.

Beneath all of it lies something he will not name — the world-weariness Paul saw at a glance, and whatever buried grief lies behind it, which Jude has never once put into words. It is the quiet counterweight to his ambition, and the likeliest source of the restlessness that keeps him moving, planning, and refusing to look too long at his own past.

## Jude's Theory of Human Servitude

Over his long years among humans (longer than Paul has been alive, as he tells him), Jude has spent a good deal of his time simply watching what people do and asking himself why. Out of that watching he has built a working theory of why one person serves another. It is an elf's view of humankind, and an unflattering one, so he offers it to Paul "straight" only after asking whether Paul would rather have it dressed up (Book 2 Ch. 3). He lays it out on the morning of 4 Wealsun as the foundation of the network Paul must build for himself (Book 2 Ch. 4).

The theory rests on one cold observation. Every servant serves himself first. Most of what divides one servant from another is what binds him, what he wants, and how well he hides his wanting. Jude counts seven kinds. His rule for the whole system is to know which kind you are dealing with, because then you know what to give him and what to watch for.

**The order of fear and love.** Underneath the seven types lies Jude's account of what a human being fears and loves, and in what order: **god, father, mother, lord.** For a young child, the first three are one, because to a child both father and mother are god. Only by growing up with a father and a mother does a human come to see how flawed they are. That disillusionment, in Jude's view, is what makes an adult. The grown human carries the old devotion elsewhere, usually by committing to a lord or to a god. Most of the seven types are a version of that transfer, or a failure of it.

### 1. Servants of Circumstance

**Definition.** A Servant of Circumstance is bound to you by his situation and by some act that he either feels guilty about or that gives you a legal right to end him. His survival depends on your good graces. He does not, however, see you as a leader. With care, the bond can later be turned into patronage. Such a servant is easily made but hard to keep, and in some cases, handled by someone who knows what he is doing, he can be made into a slave.

**How Jude explains it.** Such a man "never chose to serve anyone." He is a desperate man who tried his knife on the wrong purse, and he has caught the eye of someone powerful enough to hang him or to lift him out of the gutter. For a while that makes him useful in a way he would never be otherwise. The bond needs two things: a trespass, and guilt for it. Guilt is perishable, so it must be tended. The man will work it off only for as long as it is kept fresh. The obligation also runs to the person he wronged and nobody else, so the injured party has to be the face of the arrangement. A go-between can be the one the man reports to, but at first he has to know that real power stands behind the go-between.

**What to watch for.** Fear is not the only tool; aloofness and superiority matter just as much. A Servant of Circumstance should never see weakness, or even emotion, from his patron. When Paul proposes leaving word that Gregory's family will hang if Gregory betrays him, Jude closes the subject at once: threats are Jude's department, and the patron must stay above them. Gregory "should never think you would have to order such a thing. It would simply happen." Jude's patience extends only to the man who stayed. A partner who ran and left his friend in the street "would leave you just as quickly." Such a man is worth having, if at all, only later and more cheaply, once he sees that his friend has a patron and he does not.

**In the story.** Gregory, the cutpurse who attacked Paul in the foreign district and was spared, is the first. Jobs, his partner who ran, is held in reserve.

### 2. Servants of Obligation

**Definition.** A Servant of Obligation is bound to a lord or a government by some heroic act of that lord that benefited him or saved his life. Servants of Obligation are generally free men, and their loyalty rests on a belief or ideal that their society accepts, such as gratitude, duty, or honor owed to a benefactor.

**How Jude explains it.** He calls them "the best kind there is, and the hardest to come by." They are not won with a purse alone. They are won "with an act": something done for a man at a moment when it matters to him more than anything, with nothing asked in return. "Then you wait. Some of them forget. The good ones don't." Jude also sees that human obligation crosses generations. A man may owe a lord what his father owed that lord's father, and much of the respect a lord receives is really what was owed to his parents and his house. On the Helm, Tyrus and Folsom (and for a while Merrick) still gave the bound traitor Fairwind the deference due his name and house (Book 2 Ch. 4). The debt is the servant's own to carry. It must never be named or held over him, and it never needs to be.

**What to watch for.** Obligation is personal and does not transfer. A man who owes his life to one master cannot be borrowed by another, so Jude will not reach for "fruit Gouge has already taken a bite of." It does not die with its holder, though. If the man it is owed to falls, his followers are left with a loyalty that has nowhere to go, and they look for the nearest worthy man to give it to. Jude advises Paul to be known and decent to Gouge's men so that he is that man if Gouge ever falls (Book 2 Ch. 4). Obligation can also be renewed. Jude's advice about the twenty Marines is to follow the life-saving act with steady care, coin passed through their sergeant to put bread on the tables of the men with families in a hungry season.

**In the story.** As Jude reads them, Gouge and Merrick are bound to Lord Jamis this way. Gouge's debt is one Jude cannot name. Merrick's is plainer, because Jamis made his career. Paul already holds the same kind of claim on the twenty Marines who held the shield wall on the Helm quays and are alive because he and Jude brought them ashore.

**Bastards: the special case.** Jude files bastards under Servants of Obligation, and he counts them the easiest of all servants to convert and the most excessively loyal. A bastard has had no father to grow out of. He has never watched the man fail, so he idealizes his absent father far beyond any reality there is, and he never makes the transfer that turns a child into an adult. That unfinished devotion is the opening. Replace the father and the bastard is bound to you just as a child is bound to his. A child raised without a father's hand on his shoulder "grows up without a mooring," and goes through life hungry for someone to tell him he is worth keeping. Whoever answers that hunger, he will follow. Jude's method is to find him young, in his early teens if possible, while he still holds some fealty to the father he imagines and has not yet learned how little he stands to inherit. Then become "the parent they didn't have." Jude insists it is no lie as long as the patron truly looks after him (Book 2 Ch. 4). The lesson lands on Paul, a bastard of the prince's line, while Jude seems unaware of it. That is the chapter's central irony.

### 3. Servants of Coin

**Definition.** A Servant of Coin serves only for payment. He is usually loyal, and he will fight for his master, but he will not give his life for money.

**How Jude explains it.** Mercenaries, sellswords, caravan guards, the men who drink at Ferd's while they wait for work: they serve whoever pays, for as long as he pays, "and they don't pretend otherwise. There's an honesty in that you can use." All men work for coin one way or another. The Servant of Coin is the one who is loyal to nothing else.

**What to watch for.** His loyalty ends where a better offer begins. It also ends where the danger outweighs the wage, because no purse buys a man's death. The paradox is that most servants of coin are drawn to violence and adventure for their own sake, and will often risk far more than the pay is worth. That appetite is their own, though. It serves the thrill, not the master, and it cannot be commanded (Book 2 Ch. 4). Such men are not brought to the palace or seen at one's door. A cheap, discreet set of rooms near Ferd's is the place to receive them. Coin is also a poor binding for anyone else. A clever man can bleed a patron dry, and "you don't want to be seen to be buying their friendship, because that means someone else can buy their enmity." Pay too little and it buys nothing. As Jude puts it in the transcript, coppers buy "a knife in the back."

### 4. Loyal Servants

**Definition.** A Loyal Servant has sworn fealty or loyalty to a lord or a government and serves in an official capacity. He can be trusted as long as his livelihood and advancement are not cut back too far. He feels secure in his station, but he still looks for advancement and greater opportunity.

**How Jude explains it.** This is the richest field for a young prince to cultivate. The Loyal Servant is often as bitter as the disloyal one. The difference is that "they know better than to show it." Jude's targets are the able ones who have hit the ceiling: the clever lieutenant passed over in council, the sergeant who does an enormous amount of work while his captain simply assumes it will be done, the clerk who keeps the paperwork tight, the ship's lieutenant whose captain will never give him a ship because he makes that captain's own ship run so well. "People in power don't like to promote the people under them who make them look good," because promotion would take those people out of their service. A patron represents more than money to these men. He represents a chance to rise as he rises, without their ever ceasing to serve their lord.

**Rules.**
- **Never ask a Loyal Servant to betray his lord.** He must be able to see that serving his patron does not set him against his master. This lifts a great deal of fear off him and makes it easy for him to come close. It also protects the patron, since "you in turn don't want to be betrayed."
- **Stay away from the top of the household.** Head stewards, chamberlains and masters of the household have made their nests, and they are "far more often double agents than anything else." The man who actually runs a great house under its lord already has his own web, and anyone who reaches for him will only be caught in it.
- **Show real care.** Patronage is lopsided by nature, since it says that you have and they have not. What it must promise is genuine interest in a man's talents and a wish to see him advance. "You have to make them feel that more than you make them feel your money."

### 5. Disloyal Servants

**Definition.** A Disloyal Servant was once a Loyal Servant who has become so embittered that he no longer bothers to hide his disappointment in his station. He complains openly, and he is too stupid to be worth recruiting.

**How Jude explains it.** Disloyal Servants are "the obviously embittered ones," usually very sarcastic. Their bitterness is not what disqualifies them, since the loyal feel it too. What disqualifies them is that they show it: "A servant who lets his bitterness show where his betters can see it is a fool, and a fool is no use to you." The whole point of recruiting Loyal Servants is to reach the able ones before they sour into this. Jude adds an unsentimental corollary. Strip away temper and manners, and the loyal and the disloyal want exactly the same things: a warm bed, a full belly, a little more than the man beside them, and a master who will not look too closely at how they came by it.

### 6. Disloyal Slaves

**Definition.** A Disloyal Slave is someone over whom you directly hold the power of life and death. He has no standing in the society he belongs to and no recourse but to serve you. He does not, however, see you as a god, a mother or a father.

**How Jude explains it.** A slave is a man whose life his lord can end in an instant, "and no one else would so much as blink." The disloyal slave serves because there is no alternative, not because he believes. In the chapter's wording, he "isn't quite convinced his lord is a god, but has no wish to put it to the test." His obedience rests on fear alone, and fear is a motive Jude distrusts elsewhere: a frightened man will sell your secret to the next buyer.

### 7. Loyal Slaves

**Definition.** A Loyal Slave, through upbringing or conditioning, sees you as a god. His life depends directly on your existence, and he cannot imagine a world in which you do not exist.

**How Jude explains it.** "A loyal slave looks at his lord and sees a god. He would rather die himself than see that god challenged." This is the devotion Paul asks about, men who would give their lives without being asked, and Jude's answer is that true devotion of that kind is slavery under another name. It cannot be bought with coin or kindness. "It needs a people raised to it from the cradle." Jude holds that Monmurg and the Outer Islands are not made that way, since their people are too used to bargaining and too proud. He will not say whether the Toli or the Plars are, because he knows too little of either.

### Recruiting pools and principles

The seven types describe what binds a servant. Jude also teaches where to look for them.
- **Bastards.** The easiest recruits of all, and the most loyal once bound (see Servants of Obligation, *Bastards: the special case*).
- **Orphans: not worth cultivating.** Jude considers orphans less than worthless as servants. A human raised with neither father nor mother has nothing above him to fear or love, and so is starkly aware of his own worthlessness. He will betray you if it benefits him even slightly. An orphan can be manipulated, and used for as long as the immediate advantage lines up, but his loyalty cannot be cultivated. Jude draws the line sharply: the bastard lacks a father and so idealizes one, while the orphan lacks both and so believes in nothing.
- **The unappreciated underlings.** These are the capable lieutenants, sergeants, clerks and ships' officers kept in place because they make their superiors look good (see Loyal Servants).
- **The palace web.** In the long term Paul must court people inside the palace who are loyal to him while still serving his uncle: clerks, secretaries, heralds, scribes, physicians, musicians, the steward's under-men, the letter-carriers and the wine-pourers who hear what is said over the wine. "A sword at your back is worth having, but so is the man who knows which petition reaches your uncle first, and why."
- **Always for Prince Jeon.** Paul is to talk his uncle up to everyone he takes on. The network serves Jeon, and nobody in it is ever asked to choose against him.
- **Jude himself.** He places himself outside the system as a pupil's prospect: "I'm long past all this. I've hitched my wagon to Lord Jamis." He serves Jamis by open, mutual use, in effect a Servant of Coin and of advancement who is candid about it (see Motivations). He is equally candid that Jamis will not live forever while an elf will. When Jamis dies, Jude means to find a new patron, and he tells Paul, lightly, that he would not object to one he had a hand in making (Book 2 Ch. 4).

### Drafting notes (Book 2 Ch. 4 against this canon)

These are the places where the current chapter text and the seven-type canon above do not yet line up. They are left for the author to resolve.
- **Bastards in the Ch. 4 text.** Jude's summary paragraph in Ch. 4 lists "a bastard… moved by hunger to belong" as if bastards were a type of their own. Under this canon they are a special case of Servants of Obligation, bound by replacing the idealized father. The order of fear and love (god, father, mother, lord) and the orphan distinction are not yet in the chapter.
- **Paul against the theory.** Paul is a bastard whose mother died when he was young and who was raised in his uncle's house. In Jude's terms he sits uneasily between the bastard and the orphan. Whether Jude, or Paul himself, ever draws that conclusion is worth deciding deliberately.
- **Servants of Obligation as an ideal.** Ch. 4 frames obligation purely as a personal debt. The canon also roots it in an ideal the society accepts (gratitude, duty, honor), which is what makes such a man a free servant rather than a debtor.
- **Servants of Coin will not die for pay.** Ch. 4 stresses that a mercenary leaves for a better offer but does not yet say that he fights for his master but will not give his life for money.
- **Circumstance into patronage.** Ch. 4 says Gregory will be useful "for a while." It could state outright that the bond can later become patronage, and that the trespass may also give his master a legal right to end him.
- **The count.** Ch. 4 touches all seven types, but its summary paragraph names only some of them (circumstance, coin, obligation, loyal servant) plus bastards. If Jude is meant to set out the system as seven, the summary is the place to do it.
