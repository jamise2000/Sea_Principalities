# Ferd

The watchful proprietor of Ferd's — a bar, tavern, and cheap inn in one of the rougher corners of Monmurg's Foreign District — and one of the fixed points of that quarter's underworld. Ferd pours the drink, rents the rooms above, keeps the confidences, and misses very little from behind his own counter. He is the sort of man who has stood in one place so long that half the district's business drifts through his common room whether he wills it or not, which makes him, without ever holding a title or drawing a wage for it, a quiet node in the web of eyes and errands that runs beneath the city. A minor NPC, but a durable one: landlord to Jude and to Gouge, keeper of a loaded crossbow, and a name spoken with something close to trust by men who trust almost no one.

## Physical Description

The sources give Ferd little in the way of a face and a great deal in the way of a manner. He is most often found exactly where he belongs — behind his bar, cleaning the same glasses and watching his customers with frank, unhurried suspicion, a raised brow and the occasional contemptuous spit into the corner standing in for whatever he does not trouble to say aloud. He is no longer young; by his own reckoning he has not left the city of Monmurg in twenty years, and he keeps his place with the settled territoriality of a man who has run it a very long time and expects to run it a good while yet.

The one prop that never leaves his description is the crossbow. He keeps it loaded and within easy reach beneath the bar, by such long habit that Jude marks it as a permanent feature of the room, and he is entirely willing to lift it out and set it on the counter to make a point — as he does the night he backs Jude against a table of Toli toughs, laying the weapon down between them and suggesting, without heat, that they collect their friend and go.

## History

Ferd's — Ferd's Tavern, or simply Ferd's — sits in the Foreign District, the only quarter of Monmurg where non-citizens may take a room, hard by the crooked run of Bloody Alley. It is an inn as much as a bar, in the way of the district's better dives, with two narrow letting-rooms above the common floor reached by a back stair that Ferd watches the way another man might watch his till. Those rooms tie him to the story's harder men: one has long been Jude's, kept for him across his year in Idee and reclaimed on his return; the other belongs, by unspoken arrangement, to Gouge, whenever Lord Jamis's errands leave him in the city. The half-orc Karmirg, when he was still welcome near the place, never took a room at all, preferring to drink himself down wherever he fell in the common room — a habit that cost Ferd more broken furniture than either of them cared to tally.

He has kept his counter long enough to know everyone who matters in the district and most of what they would rather he didn't. He knows Gouge and Karmirg as tenants and as dangerous men to be humored; he knew Jude of old, down to his habit of boiling his water, and takes his return in stride. He remembers Owen Black as a "loser" whom Gouge beat badly in this very bar some six months past, over a grudge Ferd cannot fully explain. He can point a man toward the mad alchemist of the square when asked. And he pours the rough "red eye" that the city's cutthroats favor, keeping the confidences that come with it — when Gouge tells him to say nothing to the elf who might come asking, and to pass off four drinks as simply "Gouge sent them," Ferd does exactly that and no more.

The blockade has pressed on him as it has on everyone: his kitchen has gone from decent food and plenty of grog to bad food and none, and he freely admits he lacks the means to set up a still of his own to turn the shortage to profit, which is part of what makes Jude's arrival — with a crate of good wine and a scheme for exactly such a still — worth his cooperation. Beyond the bar itself, he proves a willing and useful pair of hands for the quieter work: he agrees to set a few of his lads to watching which ruffians come and go and where they haunt, and to keep an eye out for the courier Kalisi, who is in the habit of coming by to speak with Gouge.

## Personality Traits

Ferd is watchful, suspicious, and dry to the bone, a man who has made a long career of seeing much and saying little. He greets newcomers with a raised brow and a wary once-over rather than a welcome, sizes up trouble before it reaches his floor, and is unbothered about ending it himself — the crossbow on the counter is offered as counsel, not threat, and he does not raise his voice to go with it. That flat steadiness is the temperament of a man who has watched every kind of ugliness cross his threshold and learned not to flinch at any of it.

His central asset, and the thing that keeps him alive and in business among such company, is discretion. He keeps confidences as a matter of professional survival: he holds rooms for Jamis's knife-men, launders a word or a drink into deniability when Gouge asks it of him, and does not gossip about the guests who most need him not to. He is loyal in the transactional, unsentimental way of a good publican — reliable to the people who pay him and protect his interests, indifferent to the rest — and he is comfortable being useful to dangerous men so long as it keeps his own place standing.

Beneath the sourness he is also, in his fashion, a creature of loyalty to the familiar. He kept Jude's room through a year's absence and greets his return with more grumbling recognition than surprise; he remembers old habits and old grudges alike; and he is rooted to Monmurg and his own four walls to a degree that borders on the immovable, a man who has not left the city in twenty years and shows no sign of wanting to.

## Motivations

Ferd's motives are those of a man who wants, above all, to keep what he has: his bar, his custom, his rooms, and his skin, in a district and a season that make none of those a certainty. He runs an honest-enough trade among dishonest people, and he understands that survival there is bought with a careful ledger of favors — silence for the right men, a loaned pair of eyes here, a held room there — as much as with coin. He is not ambitious; he does not scheme for advancement or reach beyond his counter. He simply means to weather the blockade, keep the dangerous people who use his tavern content enough to leave him standing, and go on pouring drink and watching the door for as long as Monmurg has a foreign district to pour it in.
