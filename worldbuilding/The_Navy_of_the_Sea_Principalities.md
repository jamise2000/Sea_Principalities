# The Navy of the Sea Principalities

The Hold was built by buccaneers before it was ever built by princes, and its navy has never quite let itself forget it. What began, in the accounting of Admiral Belchem Flotsome's *Rise of the Sea Principalities*, as loose captains of noble and common blood alike raiding the coasts from Gradsul to Scant, hardened over generations into the standing fleets that now guard the Strait of Monmurg and the trade that feeds it. But the transition from piracy to navy was never total, and never finished. Letters of marque are still issued from the Harbor District. Old privateer crews still drink alongside commissioned officers at the same dockside taverns. And the captains who shaped the fleet within living memory — men like Nado and Cross — are remembered less as bureaucrats of a standing military than as the last of the buccaneer captains who happened to end their careers wearing a commission instead of a black flag.

## The Shape of the Fleet

Command of Monmurg's navy runs, in peacetime, through the Commodore of the Fleet — presently Lord Jamis — who holds a squadron of twelve of the city's fastest ships under his own direct command and a battalion of mercenary guardsmen besides, charged with keeping order across the Harbor and Foreign Districts alike. In time of war, that authority folds upward into Prince Jeon II himself, captain of all fleets and, by the old Pact of Equals, supreme naval commander of the Hold in full. Helm Island, fortified and centered in the strait, remains the traditional seat where princes and commodores of every principality gather to plan a campaign, precisely because whoever holds it holds the throat of the Azure Sea.

The workhorse of the fleet is the corsair — fast, shallow enough to run the strait's tighter channels, built to move a company of Marines from one trouble spot to the next rather than to stand toe to toe with a Keolandish man-of-war. The Marines themselves are a distinct arm from the blue-water sailors proper, boarders and garrison troops first, sailors only as the job requires, and it is corsairs and their Marine complements that have done nearly all the fighting and dying at Helm Island in recent memory. Alongside them, in an uneasy but tolerated arrangement, the Hold still licenses privateers under letters of marque, so long as they leave any ship flying the Principalities' own colors well alone — a compromise that keeps old piratical talent in the fleet's orbit without quite letting it back into respectable command.

One unit stands apart from the rest: the weather crossbowmen, an elite company of marksmen trained to fight as readily with blade as with bow, historically drawn up from the ranks of the privateers rather than the regular navy. They take their name from the work they were built for — fighting effectively in the sudden squalls and rolling decks of the open Azure Sea, where an ordinary bowman's aim goes to pieces and a weather crossbowman's does not. It is a hard unit to get into and a harder one to be dismissed from with any grace, and old privateer captains have long measured their own worth in how many weather crossbowmen they could claim to have trained.

For a hundred and fifty years, the fleet's other great and quieter task has been holding the sea lanes clear of the sahaugin — not through swordwork, but through the yellow-white powder the Duke of Berghof's territories once supplied by the ton, sprinkled into the tide by ships on regular rounds. A sailor who came up in the last several decades trained for storms, for Keolandish patrols, and for the occasional privateer gone rogue; the shark-men were a thing spoken of only in old men's stories, not a threat any serving officer expected to meet in the water. That is, until recently, no longer entirely true.

## Captains of Living Memory

Ask any sailor still serving out of Monmurg's Harbor District to name the captains who shaped them, and two names come up faster than any others, for very different reasons.

**Captain Nado** commanded respect the old-fashioned way, by being right about the water more often than anyone else in the fleet. A navigator first and an admiral only because the Hold insisted on giving him the rank his skill had already earned, Nado was the sort of officer junior captains quietly copied — he read a coastline, a current, or a coming storm the way another man reads a face, and more than one ship that should have wrecked on the Scimitars in the dark came home instead because Nado, or someone he'd trained, was aboard. He carried an old, undimmed hatred of the sahaugin that struck younger sailors as faintly eccentric in an era when the shark-men were little more than a bedtime threat — a hatred he never let cool, and never stopped pouring into every recruit who'd stand still long enough to listen, his own son foremost among them. Whether Nado still walks the Harbor District docks himself or has long since given his commission over to younger hands, the fleet has not stopped invoking his name as the standard a good navigator is measured against, and Lord Jamis, who rarely says such things aloud, has been heard to hold Nado's son to that same exacting measure — the clearest compliment the commodore is known to pay anyone.

**Captain Cross** built his reputation the other way, on nerve and appetite rather than patience. A privateer captain out of the old school, Cross ran one of the Harbor District's more successful crews in the years before the current crisis, taking prizes under letter of marque and training up some of the hardest talent the weather crossbowmen ever fielded — Cross's own name is still spoken, half admiringly, half as a cautionary tale, wherever old privateers gather to drink. Dockside accounts differ on exactly how his crew fell out of Monmurg's good graces — some say a prize taken too close to the letter's limits, others a rival captain's grudge finally collecting its due — but the fall was real and it was public, and it scattered a crew that had, for a few good years, been the most feared thing on the water short of an actual Keolandish warship. What the fall did not do was erase the training Cross had given his people. It was out of that same disgraced crew that Lord Jamis quietly plucked the talent that mattered most to him, proof, if any were needed, that the commodore has never much cared whose flag a good knife used to fly under.

Neither man is unique in kind — every old salt in the Harbor District can name a captain or two of their own generation who shaped the fleet in some smaller way — but Cross and Nado between them mark the two poles the navy still measures itself by: the patient, exacting navigator who keeps a ship alive, and the hungry, half-lawless privateer who makes it dangerous. The fleet, at its best, has always needed both.

## The Fleet in Dock

It is against that living memory that the present moment reads as strangest of all. The ships are still there, the crews are still paid, and still the fleet does not sail — commodores who would once have answered a blockade with steel instead keep their squadrons tied up at dock, and the men who remember Cross and Nado's era find themselves standing watch over ships going nowhere while the granaries empty and rumor fills the space competent command used to occupy. Three vessels have been lost already trying to run the blockade in earnest, driven back or drowned outright by storms that break with a punctuality no honest weather ever showed, and no one currently serving has an explanation for it that doesn't sound like superstition. Whatever is presently choking the fleet's nerve, it is not the kind of danger Nado trained his sailors to read in the water, nor the kind Cross's weather crossbowmen were built to shoot at — and that, more than the blockade itself, is what has the old hands most unsettled.
