# *The Brewing Storm* — Trade Review

**THE BREWING STORM**
*Journey of the Stone, Book One*
James
Fantasy / Epic & Political Fantasy · Series opener · ~83,500 words

---

A besieged city-state's oldest secret becomes the fault line of a wider war in this cerebral, faction-dense fantasy debut.

When the elf wizard-spy Jude talks his way past a Keoland blockade and back into the orbit of his old spymaster patron, he arrives in Monmurg to find the Hold of the Sea Princes strangling under embargo, its granaries thinning and its fleet mysteriously unable to break out. Paired with Paul Revero — a prince's disfigured, mask-wearing nephew who has just discovered both his parentage and a talent for building clay familiars — Jude is sent to fortified Helm Island, where an unnatural fog, a garbled signal, and the return of the long-banished shark-folk expose a treason at the heart of the principalities: a commodore quietly selling off the alchemical "powder" that has kept the sea-devils at bay for a century and a half. What the world takes for an ancient enchantment is, the novel insists, only chemistry with a cover story — and its dwindling supply is a knife at the nation's throat.

James writes in a chilly, controlled, subtext-rich register, and the worldbuilding is the draw: magic treated as mechanism, politics rendered with genuine intricacy, and a bench of morally grey operators — a knife-man with a code, a sahaugin-haunted ranger, a bard whose gift is also his liability — who feel lived-in rather than assigned. Set-pieces (a wall-assault under cover of a feint, a poisoned grotto, a barracks song that nearly starts a riot) are vivid and assured. The cost is density: a large cast and several faction agendas arrive quickly, back-to-back debriefings slacken the post-climax momentum, and the apex villains stay offstage, so the ultimate menace is felt more as rumor than presence. Readers who prize propulsion over intrigue may stall in the middle; those who prize intelligence will not.

Comparable to Joe Abercrombie's moral grit, C.J. Cherryh's political density, and K.J. Parker's mechanism-minded cynicism, told in a cooler, more interior voice. A confident, demanding series opener that ends as an overture — Fairwind's body never found, the storm only gathering.

**Verdict:** A smart, atmospheric political fantasy that rewards patient readers; trim the exposition and it competes at the top of its subgenre.
