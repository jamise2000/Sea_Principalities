## PART ONE: DEBRIEFING IN MONMURG

# Chapter One — The Better Bargain

The key was cold when Jude took it out of his pocket, and it stayed cold in his hand all the way up the headland path, as though the iron had been kept somewhere the warmth of the city never reached.

It was the fourth hour of the fourth of Wealsun, and Monmurg was asleep, or pretending to be. Jude had spent what was left of the evening in the Royal Library, which he could now walk into as of right, and had come back to Ferd's late in the night meaning to trance until morning. But Gouge was in the bar, and the Marines who had come home with him, drinking with the riff-raff that always filled the place and loud with Ferd's red eye. Jude had no wish to go over the Helm with Gouge, or with any of them, and least of all in that state. He had stood a moment in the doorway without being noticed, and then turned back into the street. The key was in his pocket. The tower would be empty, and quiet, and his, and he could think there. Jamis had said that someone would take him to the tower tomorrow and see it furnished, but Jude had never been good at being taken places.

The tower stood on the last knuckle of rock before the land fell away into the Strait of Monmurg. It was square, and plain, and old, older than the principalities themselves: one of the watchtowers the pirate captains had raised along this coast when it was theirs, before there were any Sea Princes to call it a nation. They had built for sea and siege, not for show. Four stories of grey stone, the blocks fitted close without mortar enough to see, the walls at the base thicker than a man is tall. The windows were narrow slits set deep in the stone, wide enough to loose an arrow through and no wider, and the flat roof was ringed by a crenellated parapet where, once, a lookout had watched the strait for sails and a signal fire had stood ready. A century and more of winter storms had scoured the seaward face smooth and done no other harm to it. A wall of the same stone closed off a little yard at its foot, where a single stunted pine had grown sideways out of a crack and given up the effort of standing straight. Below the yard the cliff dropped sheer to the water. Jude could hear the sea working at the bottom of it, a slow, patient sound, like breathing.

He stood a while at the gate before he went in, looking it over. No lord in Monmurg would have thanked Jamis for it. It was a soldier's post and not a house: bare stone and arrow slits, a yard too small for a carriage to turn in, set out on a windy rock at the far end of a goat path. A man of refined tastes would have called it stark, and been right. Jude found he liked it a great deal. Walls that thick did not care who was outside them, and there was only the one door, and the cliff took care of the rest. Comfort could be bought, or made. A few good carpets and hangings against the stone, glass in the upper windows, a stove in the library, a ward or two on the door and the stair, and the small magics he had lived among all his life to warm the rooms and light them and keep the damp out of the books, and it would be more comfortable than most palaces. A palace could not be made as safe but at great expense.

###

The lock on the door was newer than the door, and well oiled. The key turned in it without a sound.

Inside, the ground floor was a single room with a flagged floor and a hearth big enough to roast a goat in. It had been swept, and not long ago. There was no furniture at all, only a row of iron hooks along one wall and, in the corner, a trapdoor with a ring set into it that let onto a cistern. Jude lifted it and listened to the drip of water somewhere below in the dark and let it fall again. He made a small light at his fingertip and walked the room's edges slowly, looking at the joints of the stones and the lintel of the door, at the places where a man who owned a building might set a listening-tube or a spy-hole, if he were the sort of man who did such things.

He found nothing. That did not mean there was nothing to find. He had sat across a table from the man who owned this building only hours ago, and he had come away thinking about how very little a man like that ever let you find.

Most of what had passed between them had been ordinary business, and Jude had already put it where he put ordinary business. Jamis had not wanted the Helm told from the beginning; Merrick and Gouge would give him the landings and the order of march, and from Jude he wanted only what a wizard had noticed that a soldier would not. So Jude had given him the company they had kept. The bard whose name still would not come back to him, and who was not Jamis's man. Merrick, who had asked leave to bring a few men of his own and had plainly done so. Tyrus, big and young and loyal to the point of embarrassment. And then the reckoning, which was short: two hundred Marines gone with the corsair; the twenty who had been ashore with Paul and Jude brought home alive; the thirty put off at the Southern Beacon come through with Gouge. Jamis had heard it the way a purser hears a tally, and Jude had given it the same way. It was the sort of talk that filled the first half hour of any meeting with Jamis, and it would keep.

Fairwind they had settled between them almost without argument. The Toli in his sanctum; the chair he had been tied to, or had let himself be tied to; the barrels he had been skimming for months, and the rest of them gone onto the *Sea Ghost*. Jude had laid it out, and Jamis had drawn the same line through it that Jude had drawn. Fairwind was playing politics, hunting for leverage against Jeon, and meant to lay his theft at the Toli's door when it came to light. Jeon could not see it, because Jeon and Fairwind had been boys together, and that, Jamis had said plainly, was why Jude had been sent to the Helm at all. Taking the barrels was treason. Merely knowing of them was treason, for any man not of the Admiralty. Jude had thought of every Marine on the raft and every one of Gouge's crew, and said nothing, and Jamis had watched him think it, and said nothing either. There was no more to it than that. Two men had looked at the same thing and seen it the same way.

Jude put the key back in his pocket and went up the stair.

###

The second floor had been meant for living. There was a smaller hearth here, with an iron crane still fixed in it, and a long table under a dust sheet, and two benches, and a shelf with a few cracked bowls on it that nobody had thought worth taking away. The window looked east, back toward the city, and through it Jude could see the dark bulk of the Harbor District below and the palace hill above it, with a lamp or two burning high up where someone else had not slept either.

He stood at the window a while and let the rest of the news go by him, as a man on a quay watches weather he cannot change. Keoland was close to civil war. The king was dead, an Oeridian nobody out of Greyhill whom the Suel houses had never forgiven for being crowned, and the Duke of Gradsul had wanted that throne for twelve years. *"The royal alchemist died a month before the king did,"* Jamis had said, and left Jude to draw the conclusion himself. Nearer home, the principalities were close to a war of their own with the Toli, if it had not already begun. The city's stores were going fast. What food came in came by way of smugglers running to Redshore, where the merchant shipping had gone, and Jamis had spoken of perhaps sending Jude back there as an envoy. Nothing that sailed north of Monmurg was safe. Jerlea Bay was untouched only because the Toli held it. The walls would stand against any army on earth, Jamis was sure; it was only that the people behind them would be hungry. *"Food is the thing we have least of."* Jude looked down at the dark roofs of the Harbor District and thought that he had lived in hungry cities before. It was not his to mend. He let it go.

The powder was another matter, because that was a task Jamis had given him. The salting ships had salted the waters around the Helm before it fell, and the salting had not held, and Jamis wanted to know why: whether Fairwind had been putting something worthless in the place of what he stole, which would be a deeper treason and a cleaner answer, or whether the stuff was losing its strength with age, or the sahaugin had learned to bear it, or someone somewhere had found a counter to it. Behind that question stood a larger one, which was how the powder was made at all. The Oeridian princes never had made it. It had come from the Duke of Berghof, and Berghof had stopped sending it, and there Jamis had stopped too, for the space of a breath, the pause of a man deciding whether to give the figure he gave other people or the true one, before he said *forty years, near enough*, and asked that it go no further. Forty years of living on a store the city could not refill. What remained was the four barrels they had rafted off the Helm and the twenty-one still in the grotto beneath it, if the grotto was still theirs; a few barrels in Monmurg, drawn on hard; and a store in Salinmoor Bay, between here and Saltmarsh, that sat in Toli custody this turn of the princes' old rotation and would have to be taken back one day, but not yet. Jude was to learn how it was made and what had undone it in the strait. He had asked whether he might keep a sample without hanging for it, and Jamis had given him leave, so the pinch of pale yellow grit in the fold of oilcloth in his pocket was his by right now. And he knew already where he would begin: with the old alchemist off the square in the foreign district, the shop Lord Phranck had called on before Cain Toli took him, one of the threads Jamis himself had set Jude to pull on his first night home. Jude had been cultivating the old man for weeks with small commissions. A set of sahaugin gills going bad in a wet rag, and a pinch of forbidden powder, would be a better reason to knock than a still.

###

The third floor was the one he had been waiting for, and it did not disappoint him.

It had been a library, or a study, once, and plainly not by the pirates' design. Somewhere along the way, after the coast had been tamed and the watchtower was no longer needed to watch, someone had bought it and made it over into a residence, and had spent most of the money on this room. The arrow slit on the north side had been cut out and widened into a proper window; the stone had been paneled; a carved rail ran round the top of the shelving with ships and sea-serpents and grinning corsairs chasing each other along it. Jude guessed at some young noble with more money than sense, who had liked the romance of an old pirate tower on a cliff and liked to sit up here with a glass of wine and fancy himself one of the captains of the early days, when the principalities were a pack of sea-wolves and not yet a nation. Three of its walls were shelving, floor to ceiling, of dark oak gone black with age, and every shelf was empty. The fourth wall held the window, wider than the ones below, with a deep stone seat set into the embrasure, and it faced north over the strait. There was a writing-table here too, a good one, too heavy to be worth stealing, and a chair with a split seat.

Jude stood in the middle of the room and looked at the empty shelves, and for the first time since he had come up the path he let himself feel something like pleasure. Then he looked at the writing-table, and at the chair, and thought that there ought to be two chairs. This was where he would teach Paul Revero, if he taught him anywhere.

That was the part of the night that mattered most, and Jamis had come to it last and least willingly. He had hesitated before he raised it, the first time all evening, and Jude had known him long enough to mark it. *"I confess I'm reluctant to raise it. But Prince Jeon has insisted."* The prince believed his nephew would make an excellent wizard, and wanted Jude to make him one.

Jude sat down at the table and set out, as he would have set out the components of a working, what he had been told and what he had seen.

What he had been told was not much, and Jamis had told it carefully. Whether Paul had truly built that homunculus himself, Jamis could not say. *"But the boy comes of a line that has never been short of mages. Nor of sorcerers, for that matter."* Not the house of Monmurg, then, which had never been known for magic; Paul was of that house only through his mother. His father's line. Few people knew who his father had been, Jamis said, and then spoke of the man anyway, to the shutters rather than to Jude: brilliant, and dead. *"His father's mother is a woman of great intelligence, and has some magic of her own."* Is, and not was. And the mask was no shyness and no disease. Paul had been burned as a child, badly, in one of his father's experiments. *"Magical,"* Jamis had said, when Jude asked.

What he had seen was more. He had watched the black clay lizard run along the ceiling of the Helm's inner corridor and watched Paul's head tilt half a breath behind it, as though he were listening through its ears, and whatever Jamis could not say, Jude was satisfied that the creature was Paul's own work, built out of a book that was very likely his dead father's. A man did not manage that by luck. He had seen how Paul stood near a fire, always at an angle to it, never quite facing it, and the scorched coat he had come off the ship in. He had seen him take command of twenty frightened Marines on the Helm's docks and bring every one of them home. And when Jude had asked for thirty men to be put ashore at the Southern Beacon, with no better reason to give than his own judgment, it was Paul who had backed him, and it was because of Paul that those thirty were alive. The boy was gifted. He was proud, and guarded, and used to being looked at, and he had learned to give nothing back to the looking. He would not take kindly to being handled. He was also the prince's nephew, and the only man in Monmurg who could speak to Jeon in a way Jude never could.

And against all of that Jude set what Jamis had offered him, because it was the same thing. *"Help him with his studies, see what he is capable of, and report to me."* And then, as though it were a small thing: *"You will not let on to Paul about any of this."* He was to be the boy's teacher and Jamis's eyes on him at once. That was what the patronage was for. The tower, the Royal Library, the purse that would pay for bribes in a city with nothing else in plenty, the key that Jamis had held out across the table and not let go of at once when Jude took hold: *"The tower is yours, so long as you are willing to act as my agent in this."* Every bit of it rested on one young man in a mask. If Paul learned too soon what Jude was to him, Jude would lose him, and Jeon's goodwill with him. If Jude handled him clumsily and the boy failed, Jeon would not forgive it. If Paul turned out to be what Jamis's careful voice suggested he might be, then Jamis had a reason for wanting him watched that went deeper than his studies, and Jude would need to know what it was before he could be sure which way his own reports were cutting.

*Careful, then,* he thought. *More careful than he'll thank me for. And more careful than Jamis will see.*

He got up and went to the window seat.

###

Below him the strait was still dark, a broad river of black water between the headland and the low line of the outer islands. Far out on it a single light moved slowly south. There should have been more. In better years the strait would have been busy even at this hour: merchantmen beating in toward Monmurg's quays to catch the morning market, and the corsairs of the Monmurgian navy running their patrols up and down the water to keep it safe for them.

The rest of what Jamis had told him had come out over the second glass of wine, and Jude went over it now in the order it concerned him.

Cain Toli first, because Cain Toli was the name under all the others. It was Cain Toli's ship the barrels had gone onto. It was Cain Toli's men who had stood in Fairwind's sanctum with their good blades and their better protections, and one of them who had taken Jude's fire full on and shrugged it off like rain. It was Cain Toli's house that held the store in Salinmoor Bay, and Jerlea Bay, and the road into the Amedio jungle. The Prince of Toli was a greater danger to Fairwind than Jeon had ever been, Jamis had said, and Fairwind did not know it.

Then the artifact. Jamis had first told him of it on his first night in Monmurg, in that same office, over that same wine, as a thing he would say to no man but a wizard: the thing Phranck had torn out of a dying Toli's mind, an artifact that commanded one of the turtle dragons of the Dragon Isles. Last night Jamis had spoken of it again, and this time he had said, *"That artifact, and whether it actually exists."* Jude had pushed back, because someone had to. A dying man's last thoughts, read by an Inquisitor out of Gradsul, with no one to check what the Inquisitor had found against what he had chosen to tell. *"Can anyone truly believe it?"*

*"A fair question,"* Jamis had said. *"I asked it myself."* And then something had happened to his voice that Jude had never heard happen to it in all the years he had known the man. *"Was it that artifact, then, and some beast Cain Toli holds with it? Is that why, every time we put out in force, something is waiting for us?"* He had stopped. *"I need to know, Jude. Whatever the truth of it is, I need to know."*

Jude leaned his head back against the cold stone and looked north, toward where the Dragon Isles lay somewhere below the horizon. He had seen Jamis angry, and amused, and seen him lie so smoothly that the lie had seemed a courtesy. He had never, until last night, seen him frightened. It had lasted a breath. He thought of the tavern talk he had let go by all week, fogs out of a clear sky and hulls going down in calm water, and found he could no longer dismiss it.

Then the slave trade, which Jude had thought he understood. Fairwind believed that the whole of the Toli quarrel with Jeon was the slave trade: Jeon's wish to see it ended, and the stake the Duke of Berghof had in it, and Westkeep, and the interior. *"That is not true,"* Jamis had said. And when Jude told him again of the Earl of Redshore, who had been startled to hear that Jeon was against slavery, having been given to understand that it was Gradsul that had taken up the cause and the principalities that stood on the other side of it, Jamis had told him where that came from. The Duke of Gradsul had always called the Sea Princes debased for the trade, though it had gone on in Berghof since before there were Sea Princes at all. So the same old trade was being told as one story in Monmurg and another in Keoland, and each story was made to fit the side that heard it.

And last, because it explained the others, the Brotherhood.

*"I have information that the Scarlet Brotherhood is not only working its way into the principalities. It is working its way into Keoland. And the present quarrel between Keoland and the principalities is being stoked by the Brotherhood for its own ends."*

Jude had last seen the inside of a Brotherhood temple by torchlight, with Karmirg at his shoulder and something with a red-painted face screaming at him in a tongue older than anything in this city, and he had not been entirely himself for a season afterward. He had said none of that to Jamis. He had picked up his wine and found his hand steady, and been pleased with it. He found it steady now, on the cold stone of the sill, and he looked at the whole of it laid out together and saw that it was one purpose. A blockade. A dead king. A slave trade told two ways. A prince skimming barrels to buy himself leverage. A Toli prince with an old beast on a leash, or a rumor of one. And behind all of it, the red hand, setting Keoland and the principalities at each other's throats, and waiting for whichever was left.

###

The last stair was narrower than the others and ended at a door onto the roof. Jude pushed it open and stepped out into the wind.

The sky had greyed in the east, and as he watched the sun came up out of the land behind the city. It lit the parapet under his hands first, and then spilled down the cliff and out across the strait, turning the black water to a hard, bright grey. The whole of it lay below him. To his right the harbor forts and the long spit of the Harbor District, the masts at the quays, the palace hill above with its lamps going out one by one. To his left the strait widening toward the open sea. And far out to the east, low and dark on the water at the edge of seeing, Helm Island, where two hundred Marines lay somewhere at the bottom of the harbor and twenty-one barrels of yellow powder sat in a grotto under the cliff.

He walked the parapet slowly, one full turn, and thought about Jamis.

It was the wine he kept coming back to. Jamis had risen when Jude came in, which he did not always do, and passed over the row of bottles on the sideboard and gone to the cabinet beneath for the bottle he kept for himself. Zafar Azane's vintage, out of Keoland. *"You'll remember this one,"* Jamis had said, and Jude had. It was nearly all Jamis drank. And when Jude had remarked, lightly, that Keoland was supposed to be blockading them, Jamis had answered him with a kind of pride: *"A crate that sails under the seal of the Duke's own right hand is not one of them. No captain on that line cares to be the man who opened Zafar Azane's wine."*

Jude did not undervalue it. It was a remarkable thing to have. In a starving city, under a blockade that stopped grain, the admiral of Monmurg had a private road through the Duke's own fleet, guarded by nothing more than his brother-in-law's seal, and no captain on that line would dare to touch it. Very few men alive could claim as much. It was power of a kind Jude understood and respected: quiet, personal, and older than the war.

But he had watched what came after. Jamis had drunk once, and set his glass down, and not touched it again for the better part of an hour. His eyes had gone to the shutters and stayed there. Jude had seen a great many men say one sentence more than they meant to, and they all did the same thing afterward: they found something else to look at, and waited to see whether anyone would ask. Jude had not asked. He had asked about Paul instead, and seen the line of Jamis's shoulders ease. And before any of that, over the pouring, Jamis had said the thing that Jude could not put down. *"I have never once asked him how it reaches me."*

It was a strange thing for a spymaster to say. Asking how things reached him was the whole of Jamis's trade. A man like that did not leave a question unasked by accident. He chose not to hear the answer.

The same man had told Jude, not two hours later, that he did not fully trust his own prince's judgment. He had told him to suspect everyone. He had sat in his office under the Harbor District and told Jude that the Scarlet Brotherhood was working its way into Keoland. And all the while, on the table between them, there had stood a bottle that came to him out of the heart of Keoland's court, from the hand of the man who sat at the Duke's right side and ran the Office that watched every wizard in that kingdom, along a road that no one ever opened, and Jamis had never once asked how.

Jude did not suspect Zafar Azane. He had no reason to, and he knew nothing of the man but his name, his office, and his wine. But he knew that a road through a blockade ran in two directions. He knew that whatever the Brotherhood had worked its way into in Keoland, it would want very much to have a hand on the admiral of Monmurg. And he knew that the one place Jamis would not look was the one place a clever enemy would choose to stand. *Suspect everyone,* Jamis had said, and had very carefully made no exception for himself. He had made one for his wife's brother without seeming to notice he had made it.

*He may be playing them all,* Jude thought, *as he believes. Or someone may be playing him, and he has arranged never to find out.* He did not know which. He filed it with the other things, and resolved that he would find out before Jamis did, if there was anything to find.

He came round the parapet to the north side again and stood with his hands on the stone and the wind in his face, and looked down at the tower under his feet.

He had owned a tower before. Lord Anlauf Ogmotton had given him one in Idee, and he had been grateful for it, and it had been half a ruin by the time the key came into his hand: one side of it open to the weather, its upper floors gone, its stair ending in sky. He had kept his books in the half that stood and lost a good many of them to the rain. This one was sound from cistern to parapet. It had a roof that kept out the sea wind, a lock that someone had cared enough to oil, and a library with empty shelves waiting for his numerous books and the run of the Royal Library to fill the rest. It came from a man who held the key a breath too long, in a city that was starving, under a blockade, with the Toli at its gates and the Brotherhood at its back and something in the northern sea that sank ships in calm water.

Jude weighed the one against the other honestly, as he would have weighed any bargain. Then he smiled a little, alone on the roof in the new light. Ogmotton had given him a ruin and asked nothing, just loyalty and to share in a hopeless situation. Jamis had given him a sound tower and asked for everything. It was, by a very long way, the better bargain.

He went down through the four floors of his tower, past the empty shelves and the dust-sheeted table and the hearth big enough to roast a goat in, and locked the door behind him with the long iron key, and tried it twice.

There was a sahaugin's gills in his room at Ferd's, wrapped in a wet rag, that would not keep much longer. And up on the palace hill a masked young man was waiting to be told what the prince and the spymaster had decided his life would be.
