# Chapter Four — A Web Worth Cultivating

"First," Jude said, and put up a finger. "We have the thief."

"Which thief?" Paul said. "Gregory? Or that faithless partner of his, Jobs?"

"Gregory. Jobs can wait. Gregory is what I'd call a servant of circumstance. He never chose to serve anyone. He's a desperate man who tried his knife on the wrong purse, and now he's caught the eye of a powerful lord who could have him hanged or lift him out of the gutter. A man in that position will be very useful to us, for a while, in a way he would never be otherwise. He was supposed to come to Ferd's. When he does, word will reach me."

"It's been three days," Paul said.

"I know it's been three days. He'll come, or he won't. But—" Jude lowered his voice. "You have to be the one who does the talking when we finish turning him, if he is to be your man. Not me. A servant of circumstance isn't easily made. A man has to feel he's trespassed somewhere, and carry some guilt for it, before he'll bind himself to anyone. With Gregory, that part is easy. He meant to rob and kill you but failed, and you spared him. The guilt is already there; we need only tend it. But the obligation he feels is to you, not to me. It was you he attacked. So you have to be the face of this. I can be the one they come to, the cut-out, but they have to know there's power behind me, at least at first."

"I don't know where Ferd's is."

Jude looked at him for a moment. Of everything he had just said, the young man had fastened on the name of a tavern. Jude let it pass. Paul would learn which things mattered soon enough, and patience would teach him faster than a rebuke. "You've been all over the foreign district, or so you told me. You know where the alchemist's shop is. If that's true, you've very likely walked past Ferd's door."

"Then it doesn't have much of a sign."

"It doesn't have a sign at all. A place like Ferd's doesn't want one. It's where mercenaries go when their luck has run out, and men who will kill for the price of a meal. Those who need it find it. As for you, that's easily put right." Jude waved a hand. "I'll show you where Ferd's is. We'll go down together. I'll introduce you to Ferd, and he'll let you into his place and give you whatever help you need, any time you need it."

"Ferd is the name of the place, or the man?"

"Both. The man first. He was a soldier. A Marine, years ago, and he's not left the city in twenty years since. He keeps a loaded crossbow under his bar and a very good memory above it."

"There's something else." Jude tapped a finger on his knee. "Rooms can be had in the lanes and tenements around Ferd's for very little now. A cheap set of them, near enough that a man could walk between there and Ferd's without crossing an open street, would make you an office of a kind. Somewhere discreet, where you could receive people who'll never in their lives be let through a palace gate. Gregory, to begin with, and others like him. Servants of circumstance. But it would serve just as well for the other sort."

"What other sort?"

"Mercenaries. Sellswords, caravan guards, the men who drink at Ferd's while they wait for work. Servants of coin, I call them. They'll serve whoever pays, for as long as he pays, and they don't pretend otherwise. There's an honesty in that you can use. But you don't bring them to the palace, and you don't want them seen at your door. Something cheap and unremarkable to begin with. It can grow into something else." He paused. "See Ferd's first. Then tell me whether you can stomach the neighborhood."

Paul nodded slowly.

"And you'll want men of your own," Jude said. "Not only servants of coin. All men work for coin, one way or another, but a mercenary is loyal to nothing else; the day someone pays him more, he's gone. Other men are moved by other things. Gratitude. Duty. Pride in their work. Love of their families, or of their prince. Those are the men you want, the ones who'll serve you for loyalty as well as wages. They're harder to find, and they're worth far more, because you can rely on them."

"The other four," Paul said. "From the Helm. Tyrus. Folsom. Gouge. Merrick. Would they be worth cultivating?"

"Branch out." Jude said it gently, for him. "I know Gouge, and he's loyal to Jamis and no one else. Merrick seems bent the same way. The other two? Those are Gouge's men now. Even the ones who are properly Jamis's — if Gouge is running with them, they're Gouge's. That doesn't mean they're loyal to him, mind. I couldn't tell you where their loyalty lies. But I've no intention of reaching for fruit Gouge has already taken a bite of."

"I understand that."

"What holds Gouge and Merrick to Jamis isn't coin. It's obligation. They're servants of obligation, the best kind there is, and the hardest to come by. I don't know what Jamis did for Gouge. Saved his life, perhaps, or his neck, or something he valued more than either. Whatever it was, Gouge has never forgotten it, and he never will. Merrick's the same, though his debt is plainer. Jamis made his career. Every rank he holds, he holds because Jamis put him there, and he knows it." Jude leaned back. "That's how it's done. Not with a purse. With an act. You do something for a man at a moment when it matters to him more than anything, and you ask nothing back. Then you wait. Some of them forget. The good ones don't."

Paul gave a slight nod. "So I must find men who need something only I can give."

"Or have already given." Jude leaned forward, elbows on his knees. "The soldiers. The twenty. The Marines who were with us on the quays at Helm Island. Those men are alive because of you and me. If they hadn't come ashore with us, they'd have gone down with the ship. They know it. You've already done for them the very thing I've been describing, and the debt they feel for it is owed to you. Don't ever hold it over them. Never say it. You won't need to. When you see Lord Jamis, ask him who led them — I've already raised it with him — the officer, or the sergeant, whoever was in charge of that twenty. You start with him. And in a time like this, when there isn't enough bread to go round, you let that sergeant have some coin. So long as he doesn't keep it all for himself. Enough that he can see some extra food onto the tables of the men with families. Do that, and you'll have Marines on your side. At first they'll be eyes and ears for you in the Harbor District. In time, they may become guards you can trust."

"I thought those twenty were still on the island," Paul said.

"We don't know yet. We don't know how many of them lived after we left. They were probably taken off when the corsairs came in." Jude frowned at the window. "I don't even know how they'll have sorted things out with Fairwind's men. If Fairwind's off the island, there may be three hundred of his Marines still there who've no idea what he did. I'd hope they're not locked up. We've just lost two hundred of our own."

"Fairwind said he'd put his men—"

"All he said was that he'd shut them up in parts of the keep. They weren't armed. He kept them from the armory. That was perhaps a hundred, a hundred and fifty, in the citadel. Don't forget the hundred and fifty in the barracks."

"The ones who made the diversion."

"Right. And those were Fairwind's too. They were with us, at the time. They seemed loyal enough to the principalities." Jude shrugged. "So it depends on how Prince Jeon has instructed the captains he sent on the corsairs. How he means to deal with them. I don't know. But the twenty, when they come back — they're good fighters. Don't take that from them. We're alive, you and I, because they held a very fine shield wall." He nodded to himself. "So once Gregory's in hand, I'd go to them next. As for Gregory, I know he tried to kill you. But I think that was—"

"I don't think it was personal."

"Cutpurse work. Sloppy cutpurse work, if we're honest."

"And Jobs?" Paul said. "His partner. The one who ran and left him to us. Should we try to turn him too?"

Jude considered it. "Not yet. A man who'd leave his partner in the street like that would leave you just as quickly. If he's worth having at all, he'll come cheaper once he sees Gregory has a patron and he hasn't." He spread his hands. "But Gregory first. I'd wager he'd give a good deal to have a patron like you."

"And if Gregory ever drops me," Paul said, "I can have his whole family hanged."

Jude looked at him.

"I mean," Paul said, "if I leave word — if he ever betrays me, if I'm ever brought down — his family goes to the rope. So he knows."

"Let's not go there," Jude said. "Leave threats and scorched earth to me. I'm very good at it, and you should be above such petty details. Gregory should never think you would have to order such a thing. It would simply happen."

Paul was silent for a moment. "Very well."

"That's the start of it," Jude said. "But in the long term you'll have to begin courting your own people inside the palace. Men loyal to you, but in the service of your uncle and the princes, and not only soldiers. Courtiers of every kind and every skill. Clerks and secretaries. Heralds and scribes. Physicians, musicians, the steward's under-men, the ones who carry letters and the ones who pour the wine and hear what's said over it. A sword at your back is worth having, but so is the man who knows which petition reaches your uncle first, and why. When you're looking for people to cultivate — and by cultivate I mean people for your web — do you even know where to begin?"

"I have some thoughts. I'm not sure they're the right ones." Paul spoke slowly, feeling his way. "Be civil to them, I suppose. Learn their names. Ask after a cook's daughter, or a groom's bad knee, and look as though the answer matters to me. Praise a man's work when it's nothing out of the ordinary. Defer a little more than my rank requires, so they think me humble and easy to talk to." He paused. "A performance, mostly. I don't know that I'd do it well."

"Bastards," Jude said. "Bastard children are very good candidates for henchmen, because of their character flaws. A child who grows up without a father's hand on his shoulder grows up without a mooring. Nothing ties him to his place in the world, so he drifts, and he knows that he drifts. He goes through life looking for someone to tell him he's worth the keeping, and the older he gets the hungrier he is for it. That need is the flaw. It's also the door. Whoever answers it, he'll follow."

Paul did not move at all.

"They usually stand to inherit nothing," Jude went on. "They're seldom loved by their families. And if they're young enough still, they tend to — go on hoping. Resenting the betters who never seem to notice them, and still dreaming of the day they'll be taken in. They've some sense of loyalty to their father still, and they haven't yet learned that they're very likely finished for the rest of their lives."

Paul was grateful for the silver mask. Heat had climbed his neck and into his face, and he could feel it there, hot and childish: the old shame of a boy who hears himself described by strangers and pretends he has not. *Seldom loved by their families.* He had been kept close in his uncle's house for twenty years the way a man keeps a key he does not expect to use: safe, in a drawer, out of sight. His mother was dead. His father he had come to know only through a stack of letters and a book of clay and blood. Did Jude know that? Did he know Paul was a bastard, and that his mother was dead? Paul could not be sure how much the elf had been told, or had guessed. If he knew, was this bait, a line cast to see whether he would rise to it, whether the mask would hold? Or was it only the elf's way, to speak plainly of a thing and let it fall where it fell? He could not tell. He was not sure Jude ever said anything without meaning something by it. He kept his hands where they were and his head where it was and waited for his voice to be his own again.

"You find them young," Jude said. "Early in their teens, if you can. You can make yourself the parent they didn't have."

"Mm." Paul let the sound stand for an answer. *The parent they didn't have.* He wondered whether Jude was teaching him a method or practising it on him; whether the elf had looked at a fatherless young man kept in a drawer and seen someone worth cultivating.

"I don't mean to sound entirely calculating about it. It's a useful tactic, that's all. And you won't be lying to them if you actually look after them." Jude seemed to notice nothing. Paul was not sure of that. "The next is loyal servants of the court, and how to deal with them."

"Loyal ones?"

"As distinct from disloyal ones. The disloyal ones are the obviously embittered ones. They're usually very sarcastic, and they aren't worth cultivating. A servant who lets his bitterness show where his betters can see it is a fool, and a fool is no use to you. Now — that doesn't mean the loyal ones aren't bitter and sarcastic too. It only means they know better than to show it." Jude leaned back. "What you want is to recruit the loyal ones into your camp before they turn openly bitter. They've gone as high in service as they will ever go. And if they're clever — or talented — they may well want more from life than that."

"So I represent money."

"Not only money. You represent — if they were your man, your loyal servant — a chance of rising as you rise. The clever ones in Prince Jeon's service will see that. Join your camp, and they can expect to rise as you do, without ever ceasing to serve him. That's why I say not the openly bitter ones. I don't say they aren't bitter. But you want the ones clever enough to hold their tongues about it. The loyal servants."

Paul turned that over. He had been told for most of his life, by his uncle and by his uncle's advisors, what he was to the house of Monmurg: a nephew, of uncertain use, kept close. He had not, until this moment, given much thought to what he might be to anyone else. It came to him now, not all at once but piece by piece, what Jude was actually describing. Every servant served himself first. The loyal ones and the disloyal ones wanted the same things: a warm bed, a full belly, a little more than the man beside them, and a master who would not look too closely at how they came by it. What divided them was only temper. The loyal went about their duties cheerfully, and believed their own cheer; the disloyal went about the same duties sourly, and let it show. Strip that away and there was nothing between them but manners. Paul wondered how long Jude had been looking at men that way, and how cold a view it was of humankind. Then he thought of the stewards and chamberlains and grooms of his uncle's house, all the faces he had known his whole life, and could not think of one who proved the elf wrong.

"Are there no truly loyal servants, then?" Paul said slowly. "Men who would give their lives for their lord and their country without being asked? Whose devotion is beyond question?"

"What you're describing," Jude said, "is a loyal slave. A slave is a man whose life his lord can end in an instant, and no one else would so much as blink. A loyal slave looks at his lord and sees a god. He would rather die himself than see that god challenged. A disloyal slave is one who isn't quite convinced his lord is a god, but has no wish to put it to the test. Either way, the thing rests on fear and worship, and you can't build it out of coin or kindness. It needs a people raised to it from the cradle, and the Sea Principalities aren't made that way. Not Monmurg, at least, and not the Outer Islands. Men here are too used to bargaining, and too proud. The Toli, or the Plars?" He shrugged. "I don't know enough of either to say.

"So you must start smaller, because right now you need agents whose loyalty remains to Jeon. Some of them will be your eyes. What's being said in the army, in the barracks, on the ships. Some of them will be the men who stand at your back in the foreign district while you talk to people you'd rather not turn your back on. You talk your uncle up to them, always. It's all for Prince Jeon. But know what moves each man you take on, because it won't be the same thing twice. A servant of circumstance, like Gregory, is moved by guilt; he trespassed against you and you let him live, and he'll work that off for as long as you keep it fresh. A servant of coin is moved by money and nothing else, and he'll leave you the day someone pays better. A servant of obligation is moved by a debt he chose to carry. A loyal servant of the court is moved by ambition. He's gone as high as his master will let him, and he means to rise with you. A bastard is moved by hunger to belong, to be claimed by someone. Know which you're dealing with, and you'll know what to give him and what to watch for. That's how it begins. You give them your patronage, and you have a small company of your own. And the best of that company are the ones who'll stand with you against your rivals, when they come, and go against them when you ask it."

"Rivals?" Paul said. "I don't have any rivals."

"You will. Every man who stands to lose if you rise." Jude considered him. "Cousins with an eye on your uncle's favor. Courtiers who've made a living out of being the only voice in his ear. Captains who'd rather the next command went to their own man. Most of them won't think of themselves as your rivals yet, and some of them will smile at you across the table. Watch who goes quiet when you're praised, and who turns friendly when you stumble. Your people will tell you the rest. That's what they're for." He held up a hand. "Now. Back to loyal servants. Never, ever ask a loyal servant to betray his lord."

"Never."

"They have to be able to see that serving you doesn't set them against their master. That's why I say loyal servants who are talented. Who've hit the ceiling. And stay away from the ones at the very top. The stewards of the royal apartments. The chamberlains. The masters of the household. They're usually very comfortable. They've made their nests. And they're far more often double agents than anything else."

"Who, then?"

"Take someone like Lord Jamis." Jude weighed his words. "Not the lord of a place — the other man. The one who actually runs it, under the lord. You don't go after that man. Not because he can't be had. Because he already has his own web, and you'll only be caught in it."

Paul understood what he was being told, and understood also that Jude would not say it outright, and was careful not to smile. Lord Jamis was not a man anyone would draw away from Prince Jeon. Least of all Paul.

"So you want me to go after middle and lower people," he said.

"When I said servants — let's say you wanted to build something inside the palace. When I talk of loyalty, then. I'd say the captains of the guard."

"The captains."

"No — the lieutenants. Not the captain. The man below him." Jude sat forward. "Here's another way to find them. The clever lieutenant. Loyal. Shows no bitterness, no resentment. Say there's a lieutenant under the commander of the watch, or of the gate, and you see him in some meeting giving the commander something worth having — and being slightly passed over for it. Not thought much of. But he's there, and you can see it. Or the sergeant. The sergeant who does an enormous amount of work, and doesn't spend his days fawning on his commander, because the commander just assumes he'll do his job. But you, watching, see that he's a very able man. Able enough to be a commander himself."

"The unappreciated ones."

"The unappreciated underlings. Still loyal." Jude nodded. "And don't use only money. In a time like this — money will get you your poor townsman, certainly. But men are also clever enough to bleed you dry with it. There's a limit to anyone's purse. And you don't want to be seen to be buying their friendship, because that means someone else can buy their enmity. You have to actually show care. Real care. That's what I meant, with the bastards. If you're not lying to them, and you truly would, for their talents, see them advanced to whatever post you might hold one day that's grander than now — then you would actually be looking after them."

"I understand."

"Patronage, when you cultivate it, is a lopsided thing. It says you have and they have not. But what the patronage suggests — what it promises — is that you're interested in their skills, their talents, and that you'd like to see them rise. You have to make them feel that more than you make them feel your money. Money, in a time like this, can help them feed themselves and the people they love. That will buy you more than you can measure. But if you only throw money about without seeming to care whether they get on—"

"Then someone with more money buys them."

"Just so." Jude nodded. "And I said never ask them to betray, because you in turn don't want to be betrayed. It'll be understood. I can't think of any situation in which you'd betray Prince Jeon, or Lord Jamis. So don't ask anyone you cultivate to do it either, and don't hint that you'd ever expect it. You'll lift a great deal of fear off them at once. You'll make it easy for them to come close to you, because they don't have to go against their own sense of loyalty to do it. That's how you make bonds."

"Good."

"And don't concern yourself with me," Jude said lightly. "I'm long past all this. I've hitched my wagon to Lord Jamis."

"Right."

"But we're talking about other people fastening themselves to your rising star. You understand?"

"Yes."

"So watch for it. The man who does all the paperwork. Who runs everything tight, and that the lieutenant or the commander simply assumes will always be done right. That man. He's very likely unappreciated. Or at least kept where he is. He'll never be promoted." Jude lifted a finger. "Remember this. People in power don't like to promote the people under them who make them look good. Because to promote them usually means promoting them out of your service. So they keep them where they are. Not always out of spite. They keep them trapped because those men make them look good to the people they want to look good to."

"It's not the rank and file who get noticed," Paul said. "It's the generals."

"It's not only the rank and file who get passed over, though. Think of all the ships' lieutenants under captains who dream of the Admiralty. Those captains will never give a good lieutenant a ship of his own. Because he makes their ship run so well."

"I'm going to the alchemist's," Jude said. "You can come by and see the place. It has some connection with — do you know Lord Phranck?"

"He's part of the noise. Something that's been going on in the background."

"This alchemist I'm trying to cultivate — I don't think it would be wrong for you to be there, when we talk to him properly. But I want to take these gills to him now, before they rot any further than they have already." Jude touched his robe again. "I've had them wrapped in a wet rag since the corsair, to keep them from drying out."

"That's not very alchemical."

"It's not. He'll probably tell me so." Jude sighed. "So I'll go there. It shouldn't take me long to talk with the alchemist and set him to work on the gills. And then—" He looked at Paul. "Then we can begin your training. Today, if you like. What would you want to learn first?"

"Spellcasting," Paul said.

"Well, yes. Is there anything in particular?"

"I don't really know anything."

"Nothing at all?"

"Only what I've seen in the Grand Market," Paul said. "The hedge-wizards who work the stalls for coppers. One of them touched the end of a stick and it shone like a lamp, a cold white light with no flame to it. Another took a torn sail from a fisherman, ran his fingers along the rip, and the cloth knit itself together as if it had never been cut. Small things. I don't know how any of it is done."

"What you saw are the lesser magics," Jude said. "Cantrips. Most people who can't work them take them for market tricks, and the men in those stalls don't trouble to correct them. But they're worth a great deal more than that. Once you've truly learned one it's yours. It's always to hand, and it doesn't draw on you the way the greater workings do. We start with those and work you up to the ones with some weight in them. Some of them will do a surprising amount of harm, for all they're the first things anyone learns. But the ones I'd start with are the ones that are useful. That second trick you saw, for one. A great many people think nothing of mending a thing. But you can put things right. Quickly, and without a trace."

"Such as?"

"A rope that's been cut and needs joining, because you're about to have to leave somewhere in a hurry."

"Or a door," Paul said, "that's been forced."

"A door that's been forced. Yes. Cover your tracks." Jude nodded, pleased. "But these gills are wilting in my robe. And it's making me smell a little of fish."

"A little?" Paul had been breathing through his mouth behind the mask for some time. The reek had come into the room with Jude and settled over it, rank and briny and a day past turning, and it clung to the elf's robe like a second skin. "More than a little, I'd say."

"Levitation," Paul said, and for once there was something like eagerness in the even voice behind the mask. "That's what I'd want. To lift a man off the ground, or myself, by nothing but will. That's what brought me up out of that chasm. I'd give a great deal to learn it."

"It is, and it's no small working. You'll need a great deal more skill than you have now before you can lift so much as yourself." Jude smiled faintly. "Harder still is the fire you saw me throw on the Helm. Wait until you've grown into that. It fills a room. Burns everything in it to death."

"Is that a promise?"

"It's a promise, if you work for it." Jude tapped his knee. "So. What else? The yellow powder. Fairwind. Teaching you."

"Those three," Paul said. "That's all I can think of."

"That's all I can think of too. So let's begin with the alchemist." Jude hesitated, and then said the thing that had been sitting at the back of his mind since the Helm. "One more reason I want to go. I don't think his life is in danger, not yet. But someone may want him taken out of the way. I've no proof of that. Nothing at all."

"Why do you think so?"

"His connection with Lord Phranck. I don't think he knows himself that it puts him in danger."

"I don't know Lord Phranck," Paul said. "Is he a real lord? Or only some killer who calls himself one? He's from Keoland, isn't he?"

"He's from Keoland. He came here and met with Lord Jamis. Think of him as a kind of disciplinarian — for people who use magic. He goes about saying, 'Shame on you. Stop raising the dead.'"

"What?"

"He polices wizards, for an office in Keoland. Wizards who deal in what they call unlawful magic. Necromancers are the darker end of it, granted. So he's an inquisitor. He hunts them down."

"Ah."

"Phranck isn't in Monmurg any longer. But while he was here, he met with this alchemist." Jude turned a hand. "So I've been trying to learn a little more. Indirectly. Bringing him the gills, asking for his help, because he's far more of an alchemist than I am. So that's another reason to go. But if you should ever learn, through your people, as you start to have people — or through anyone else you know — that there's someone in the city who's taken an interest in him, or men hanging about his shop, I'd like to know it."

"Yes."

Jude stood, and the smell of the rag bundle came up with him. "Good?"

"Good."

"Then I'm going to the alchemist before these robes have to be burned."

"You've been carrying that in your robe all morning?"

"I didn't have a container. I thought it was wrapped well enough."

"You're a wizard," Paul said. "You don't have a box? A jar?"

"I came straight up here from the foreign district, the same as you came straight up from the docks last night. It hasn't been a full day since we landed." Jude went to the door and stopped with his hand on it. "Come with me, if you like."

Paul shook his head. "I should see Lord Jamis first. About the twenty. And I owe my uncle an account of — of what I mean to do."

"Then I'll go alone. I'll see him, leave these with him, and come back." Jude considered the sun on the gravel outside. "Early afternoon? We'll begin."

"Where?"

"Lord Jamis's house. His end of the palace." Jude opened the door. "I'm to be shown a tower. I may as well collect a pupil at the same time."

The alchemist's shop stood in a small courtyard off one of the foreign district's crooked streets, a squat building of real stone among the driftwood and plaster around it, with a heavy oak door that had been bolted and barred for as long as Jude had known it. He knocked, and waited, and eventually heard the bar lift.

Old Albashon looked out at him through his thick lenses, sniffed, and recoiled.

"Gills," Jude said, holding out the rag. "Sahaugin. Two days dead. And something else." He took the fold of oilcloth from his inner pocket and set it on top of the rag. "A pinch of the powder that killed it. I'd like to know what's in both."

The old man took the bundle and the packet between two fingers as though they were both slightly on fire, and looked from them to Jude and back.

"You have been keeping these," he said, "in your clothes."

"I had no jar."

"Evidently." Albashon's eyes went, through the lenses, to the oilcloth, and something changed in his face. "Give me until evening," he said, and shut the door.
