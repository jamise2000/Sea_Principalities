# Chapter Six — The First Cantrip

On the afternoon of the fourth of Wealsun Jude came back up the hill to Prince Jeon's palace and was shown, for the second time that day, to Paul's sitting room in the east wing. It had a table, four chairs, a sideboard with a painted vase on it, a hearth with no fire laid, books on every surface that would hold them, and a window that looked out over the palace gardens. Jude had spent the first part of the afternoon being walked through a tower that was now his. Its key sat in his pocket, heavy and satisfactory.

Paul was waiting for him, exactly on the hour. Jude had expected nothing else.

"So," Jude said, when they were both seated, and looked at him across the table for a while without saying anything more.

Paul bore it. He was good at being looked at, Jude thought. He had had a great deal of practice.

"What do you want?" Jude said at last. "Where do you see yourself? You've met Gouge."

"Yes."

"If you go on as you're going — speaking purely of your skills — that's where you'll end up. Don't mistake me. I like Gouge. I've travelled with him, I've worked with him a long time, and I'll go on working with him. But he's a killer. That's all he is." Jude folded his hands on the table. "Is that what you see for yourself? Or want for yourself?"

"Not exactly," Paul said. "I think I'd also like to learn to be something of a mage."

"And what is it you think you know about being a mage?"

"From what I understand of it—" Paul stopped. "What are you asking? Reading from scrolls?"

"I'm curious. Every new initiate to the art arrives with a head full of nonsense. I'm asking what your particular nonsense is."

Paul thought about it longer than Jude expected. "I want to know how it works," he said. "But I also want to be able to stand against people who have magic behind them."

"Power."

"Influence."

"I like that. Magical influence." Jude leaned back. "So you want power. What do you want it for?"

"The Toli," Paul said. "On the Helm. The two in the sanctum. The ones who fought with blades and spells both."

"They annoyed me too."

"I wasn't as useful as I'd have liked. There was nothing I could do against what they were casting."

"I can work with that," Jude said. "So you felt useless."

"Yes."

"There's a limit to what you can do by cutting throats."

"Yes."

"Gouge is very good in a tight place. But when you're facing someone who knows how to handle arcane force, it's a different fight." Jude studied the mask. "Do you have any natural leaning toward it? Any talent that you know of?"

"I think so. I'm not sure." Paul reached into his coat and set something on the table between them.

It was the homunculus. It lay still for a moment, a small black lizard of fired clay no longer than Jude's hand, and then it lifted its head and turned it, and its eyes — two chips of something dark and faintly glinting — fixed on Jude with a steadiness no lizard's eyes had ever had. Its tail moved once across the wood. Then it ran up Paul's sleeve to his shoulder and settled there, against the collar of his coat.

"Your homunculus," Jude said.

"Yes."

"You made it."

"I did."

"Which is a very interesting thing," Jude said, "because I've never made one." He looked at the creature on Paul's shoulder and made himself admit the rest of it. "I know they were fashionable in Keoland for a long time. Hundreds of years. There were men who made a great deal of money building the things for the amusement of kings. And nearly all of it was fraud. Pickled toads in jars with their mouths stitched up in a grin. Painted clay on a wire. Things a competent illusionist would have been embarrassed to put his name to." He paused. "That isn't a fraud."

"No."

"So tell me how you did it. And don't hold back. If it sounds like nonsense, I'll think it's nonsense."

Paul was quiet for a moment. Jude could see him deciding, and could see him decide against something, though not what.

"I had a book," Paul said. "It told me how."

"A book."

"A tome. I pricked my finger. Blood, and clay, and the stones. It's all in the book."

"Who gave you the book?"

Paul said nothing.

"How did you know how to read it?" Jude pressed. "It's an arcane text, I take it."

"It's an arcane text."

"And you could read it? Or did someone teach you?"

"I could read it."

"I'm not prying for the sake of it," Jude said. "I'm trying to decide whether I can teach you. This isn't something a man learns out of a palace library, however good the palace library is. I know you have a way into places most don't, through your family. But reading the arcane is more than reading."

"When you spend twenty years shut up in a palace," Paul said, "with not much to do, your mind wanders."

"You were locked up?"

"Not exactly. A figure of speech. You take my meaning."

"No," Jude said, "I don't. Even if it's a figure of speech — why would you be kept to the palace grounds? For twenty years? I know who your uncle is. I'm not sure why you'd be kept wrapped in wool."

"Because I'm royal."

"Plenty of royals go riding. Go to sea. Go to Keoland to be educated." Jude let his eyes rest on the silver mask. "There's the mask. I've never seen you without it. Are you hideous? Deformed? A cleft lip? Were you burned?"

"No," Paul said.

Jude had been told otherwise, the night before, by the one man in Monmurg best placed to know, and he watched the lie go past and let it. He had wanted to see what Paul would do with the question. Now he had seen.

"Then why?"

"Why do you care?"

"Because I'm about to be your teacher in the arcane arts. And if you're sick in the head — if you're twisted somewhere I can't see—"

"I'm not twisted," Paul said evenly. "I'm not the one who blew a man's head off and pitched him off a cliff."

"That was necessary."

"Was it."

"You won't make me sorry for killing a man. Least of all a Toli." Jude waved it aside. "What I'm saying is that you told me you were shut up, even if you weren't. Let's say you were kept to the palace grounds. For twenty years, you said. That sounds extreme to me."

"What I'm saying," Paul said, "is that I've lived here my whole life."

"So you don't know anything of the world except what's in books."

"Yes."

"Then you've read a great many books."

"Yes."

"Such as?"

Paul told him.

Jude began with the easy ones, the histories and the philosophers every tutor in the Flanaess set a boy of good family, and Paul had read them. He went on to harder ones, and Paul had read most of those too, and could say what was in them, and which of them he thought were wrong and why. After a while Jude stopped choosing books to test him and started choosing them because he wanted to know the answer. It became clear, not in a moment but over a quarter of an hour, that someone had built this man's education with great care and great intelligence — had chosen what he read, and in what order, and why — and had done it better than most royal tutors, and better than most of the academies of Keoland. Better, in places, than what Jude had heard of the Arcane Order.

"Who taught you?" he said.

"My mother." Paul said it flatly. "She laid it out for me. What to read. When. She died when I was young. But by the time I was ten she'd given me—" He stopped.

"A taste for it," Jude said.

"Yes."

So the foundation was sound. Better than sound. Jude sat with that for a moment and did not let his face do anything.

"So you want to be of consequence," he said.

"Yes."

"Tell me your hang-ups, then."

"My hang-ups?"

"Everyone has them. I don't know. You want to go back and blast every boy who ever bullied you when you were small. That's a hang-up. A vengeance trip."

"No," Paul said. "I don't care about that."

"Nothing else?"

"Such as what?"

"Hang-ups. Things you're stuck on. Things from before you came to the art that are going to come with you into it."

Paul was quiet for longer this time.

"I want to prove myself," he said. "My worth. Earn my keep."

"You want to be valuable."

"Yes."

"I can understand wanting to be valuable." Jude leaned forward. "Now give me one way that wanting to be valuable could get you into very deep trouble."

"My family has had a hard time seeing me as any use to the principalities."

"That's along the lines of it. I understand. You want to prove yourself. So — if I'm teaching you the arcane arts, how can that turn against you? What's the failing that's going to haunt you, given that you want what you want?"

"If I fail."

"Go a step further. You don't want to fail. How will that hurt you, in the study of magic?"

"It could kill me."

"And beyond killing you?"

Paul was silent. Then, slowly: "Something evil."

"If you want so badly to be valuable," Jude said, "to prove yourself, that you take on things you can't control before you're able to control them, you can bring a whole world down around your ears. Say you decide to summon something you can't command, because you think you can command it."

"Yes."

"Do you follow me?"

"Yes."

"As long as you keep that in mind." Jude sat back. "Look. I want to be valuable too. I want power. But the thing is, if you're dead, what you wanted doesn't matter at all, because you're dead. So you have to be careful of overreaching. I've had to check myself a great many times. And sometimes I've overreached anyway." He looked at the homunculus on Paul's shoulder, which was looking at him. "When I start to teach you, there'll come a time when I can't teach you any more, and you begin to teach yourself. You have the habit of study, because your mother gave it to you. So there'll come a time when you're studying the art and you don't come to me. And if you can't keep it in your mind that you can overreach, you will overreach. And it may cost a great deal more than your own life."

"Yes."

"Do you understand that?"

"Yes."

"We'll see," Jude said. "All right. I'll teach you."

He let that stand a moment. Paul did not move.

"Now. Given what you can do already — how would you describe what you are? What you do?"

"Is this a petition for employment?"

"It is," Jude said. "Because you reflect on me. If you go out there and make a fool of yourself, every assassin in the principalities will want a try at me, because they'll think I'm a fool as well. So if you're to be my pupil — how do you see yourself? What's your particular skill? Gouge is very good at meeting things head on. I don't even know that he's a good poisoner."

"He might be."

"He might be. I think Gouge is very good at frightening people and very good at cutting throats, and I don't know that he has any subtlety beyond those two. I'll go further. Gouge isn't even a good fighter. He's a great dirty fighter. That's a different thing." Jude tapped the table. "So what are your strengths?"

"Disguise," Paul said. "The masks we made on the Helm. I don't see myself as someone who meets things head on."

"That's subterfuge. Disguise. Misdirection."

"And the art?"

"In the art, that's—"

"Being unseen," Paul said.

"Illusion," Jude said. "Yes. All right."

He opened his satchel and took out his spellbook and set it on the table, and laid his hand flat on the cover, and thought.

"You're owed six," he said. "That's the old custom. A master gives a new initiate six workings out of his own book to begin, and a few of the small ones besides — three. I'll give you two of the three. The third you'll find for yourself, in the library, when I've got you into the part of it that matters. The first two will be mending, and light."

"Mending," Paul said, "and light."

"And the reason is this. From what you're telling me of who you are, you need to grow in a particular direction. When I was learning, I needed something to kill with from the very first day. That's the kind of mage I was going to be. You can already kill people. So you need to think of the arts as something that adds to your ability to kill people. Mending. Give me one way mending could help you."

"Repairing some piece of equipment. A mechanism."

"That's weak. Come on."

"In the work I do. My grapple, if the hook fails."

"So — mending your own gear."

"Yes."

"What about when you break a window to get into a house, and then mend the window you broke?"

"Yes. I suppose."

"Cover your tracks."

"Yes."

"And if I give you light—"

"If you're going to ask me how to use light," Paul said, "that's fairly obvious."

"Have you ever put a light on the inside of a man's helm? A full helm, with the visor down?"

Paul stopped.

"It blinds him," he said.

"It blinds him. So light is more than light. It's only as limited as you are."

Paul sat very still for a moment. Jude could almost watch the thought move behind the mask, and then another behind it, and a third.

"Seals," Paul said. "Letters under a seal. You break the seal, read what's inside, fold it again, and mend the wax."

"And no one knows you've read it." Jude inclined his head. "Now you're thinking."

"Do you have dark sight?" Jude asked, after a moment. "Some of the old blood does. Elves. Half-elves."

"No."

"Then light is worth a great deal to you. If only to search an empty room before you set foot in it. Hidden traps. Alcoves. Places a man might be standing." Jude turned a page of his book. "The small workings you can use as often as you like, once they're truly yours. But there's a cost, once you start into anything heavier. It draws on you. On your strength. The heavier the working, the more it takes."

"So I'll have only so many of the greater ones."

"Before you have to rest. Yes. Those small ones aren't even what I'd call first workings. They're like lighting a fire. You can light a fire from your fingertip, and then you have a fire." He turned another page. "Six, then. And you'll write them. We'll get you a book, and you'll copy them into it from mine, and you're going to make mistakes. Writing the art is a skill of its own. You'll have to practice. I'll watch. If you've got any part of it wrong, I'll tell you, and you'll write it again."

"Yes."

"What would you do with sleep? In your line of work."

"Instead of killing a guard I needed to get past, I'd put him to sleep. And pull him into a corner."

"Good. What would you do with protection from good and evil?"

"What does protection from good and evil do?"

"That's a fair question," Jude said. "Moving on. Have you ever run into a magical trap?"

"Yes. On the Helm."

"Then detect magic will help you before you ever start looking for ordinary traps. It'll tell you what's a ward and what's mundane. You know the word mundane?"

"Physical. Of the material world."

"Yes. So. Detect magic. Sleep. Protection — think of it as only protection, for now. You don't need a familiar." Jude glanced at the lizard. "You have that. What do you use it for? I've never kept one. Tell me."

"I look through its eyes," Paul said. "Hear through its ears. And I can shape it. It's a lizard now. I could make it into a burrowing thing, if I needed to."

"And it would behave like one? Claws? It could dig?"

"Yes. Well — I control it. But yes."

"Could you make it a bird? Could it fly?"

"You might be able to. I can't."

"So there are limits to what you can make of it."

"It's clay," Paul said. "What happens to clay when it gets wet?"

"It goes to mud."

"So I made it of a clay I prepared myself. It should stand the wet."

"Should."

"I haven't tried it in the sea."

"So you can't simply drop it in the ocean and have it swim about as a fish."

"I haven't tried."

"If it's destroyed," Jude said, "can you call it back?"

"No. But it isn't alive. It was never alive. If it breaks I make another." Paul lifted one shoulder, and the lizard rode the motion without shifting its grip. "Clay is cheap. The stones are what matter."

"And what's it chiefly good for?"

"Scouting. You've seen me use it."

"I'm asking you to tell me."

"Scouting," Paul said again. "Getting where I can't go."

"You've seen me use my toad," Jude said.

"Your toad."

"My toad drew those guards off, in the Helm, so we could take them."

"It wasn't half as good as mine going up the wall and over the top of him," Paul said. "Upside down, along the ceiling."

"A toad is a natural creature."

"In a castle?"

"In a castle. Yes. Your little clay gingerbread man is going to frighten people. It's going to draw the wrong sort of attention."

"And you think a toad draws the right sort?"

"It did."

"Where do toads come from?"

"Water."

"We were on a rock in the middle of the sea," Paul said. "In a castle. How many toads have you ever seen hopping down a castle corridor?"

"Do you think no one on the Helm drinks fresh water? There are cisterns."

"Toads don't hop through corridors."

"Of course they do."

"They do not. You're making that up." Paul's voice did not rise, but it hardened a degree. "What sort of mage are you?"

"Quiet," Jude said. "That's the other thing. You have to do what I tell you."

Paul said nothing, very distinctly.

"So. Protection. Sleep. Detect magic." Jude went on. "Have you ever carried a weapon, or worn a piece of jewelry, that you took off someone — that you probably shouldn't have put on? Something that turned on you?"

"I don't think so."

"That's what identifying is for. What about that mask of yours?"

"I've been wondering the same thing."

"The one you bought in the market. The black iron one. The one I didn't like."

"This isn't that one. This is the one I wear in the palace."

"I know. The silver's for court." Jude turned it over. He had already tested the black mask when they first met, with the working that shows what holds power, and found nothing in it. It had come off Paul's face easily enough. It was not the mask itself that troubled him, only what it was: a Scarlet Brotherhood face, sold on a market stall to a man who had no idea what he was buying, or else to a man who did. "I'll give you identify," he said. "And that's where I'll stop, for now. Sleep, identify, detect magic, protection. And shield."

"Shield."

"Shield is a good one. It's what's called a reaction. It's the only one I can give you, at your level, that you can throw up in the instant before you're hit. It raises a wall of force that almost nothing can get through. There are some things that can. But it will stop the bolts of force I put through that Toli dead."

"How long does it last?"

"A moment. The moment you need it." Jude closed the book. "That's five. The sixth you'll earn."

Paul did not argue. Jude noted that.

"Now. The way the art works — and I want you to understand this — is that you'll only have so much. I'll give you these, and you'll have to write them into your own book, in your own hand. I'm going to give you a proper book, but you're going to spoil it, so you'll practice first on plain parchment. It won't be the same. It's not like writing a scroll. But it will be a fair copy to learn on." He held up one hand, fingers spread. "At the start, you'll be able to hold perhaps five of these in your head, ready. But you'll only have strength enough to cast twice before you need to sleep. You'll have more knowledge than power. As you go on, that changes. And at some point it changes a great deal."

"Twice."

"Twice, and then you're spent. You can use the same working twice, if that's what you need. But twice." Jude looked at him. "So. Do you want to go this way?"

"Yes," Paul said.

"Then we start with writing. I start everyone with writing." Jude stood. "And then mending. Because I think mending will matter more to you than any of the rest."

###

He broke the chair first.

He did it without warning, picking it up by its back and bringing it down across the edge of the table so that the seat split and one leg came off and went skittering across the boards into the hearth.

Paul was out of his own chair before the leg had stopped rolling. "That's my chair."

"Then you'd better mend it," Jude said. "You'll want to sit in it again."

"You're paying for it if I don't."

"I'm not paying for anything." Jude glanced at the painted vase on the sideboard. "Or would you rather start with that?"

"Don't touch that."

"Then mend the chair."

The working itself was simple enough to say and very hard to do. Jude showed him the shape of it — the words, which were few, and the gesture, which was fewer, and underneath both the thing that could not be shown at all, only described: the act of holding in the mind what the chair had been, whole, and insisting on it. Paul knelt among the pieces and began.

For most of the first hour it seemed to be working. Paul would lay the broken edges together and speak, and Jude would see the split in the seat draw closed along its length like a seam being sewn from the inside, the grain knitting across it, and then — a breath later, two breaths — part again, cleanly, as if it had never closed. Paul would sit back on his heels and look at it, and begin again, and it would close, and open.

"You've got a problem with permanence," Jude said.

Paul did not answer.

Near the end of the hour, while Paul was laying the broken leg against its socket, Jude reached out one finger and tipped the vase off the sideboard. It hit the floor and broke into four pieces.

"You're going to have a great many things to mend," he said.

"You're paying for that."

"You're mending it."

"The chair's still broken."

"Because you're not very good yet."

"And who broke it?"

"You're about to fix it," Jude said. "So get on."

In the second hour the chair stayed mended. It happened without any change Jude could see in what Paul was doing; the seam closed, and the grain knit, and this time it held. Paul put his hand on the seat and pressed and it held under his hand. The leg went back into its socket as if it had been turned there. Then Paul turned to the vase, and the four pieces drew together on the floor with a sound like a fingernail on glass, and the painted birds on its side met up again across the cracks, and it stood on the boards whole and a little dusty.

Jude looked at it for a long moment. It was, he had to admit, well done.

"There," he said. "It's yours. You'll have it now."

"Good."

"You can stop here, you know," Jude said. "You don't have to become a wizard. You could be an assassin who knows how to mend a chair."

"I'm not stopping."

"I'm not a pleasant teacher."

"I'd noticed," Paul said. "How long do I have to have you as a teacher?"

"More or less forever. Do you see another wizard anywhere about?" Jude spread his hands. "You can always go and complain to your mother."

"She's dead."

"Your uncle, then."

"I might."

"Go on," Jude said. "He hired me."

Paul turned the silver mask on him for a long moment, and then turned back to the vase and set it very precisely back on the sideboard.

"What's next?" he said.

"Light," Jude said. "And for light we want the dark."

###

They went down into the dungeon beneath the palace, where the two of them had questioned the thief only days before, though it felt a good deal longer. Jude stopped at the guard post at the head of the stair and told the man on duty that they wanted a room for an hour or two and would not require anyone to be put in it, and the guard, who had seen stranger requests from the prince's nephew, gave them a lamp and let them pass.

The room they chose had chains let into the walls at shoulder height and a drain in the middle of the floor, and no chairs at all. Jude set the lamp in a niche by the door and sent out for parchment, and when it came, he paid for it out of his own purse — one gold coin for a quire of it, which was robbery, and which he mentioned.

Paul took a handful of coin out of his own purse and flung it at him.

"You're very dramatic," Jude said, picking a single gold piece out of the straw and pocketing it. "That will come back on you."

He put out the lamp and lit a candle, and set it on the floor between them, and began to talk about light.

Light was harder than mending. Mending asked Paul to remember a thing whole. Light asked him to call something out of nowhere and make it stay, and he could not make it stay. For an hour he could not make it come at all. Jude talked him through the words, and the shape of the hand, and the thing underneath both, and Paul did all of it, and the dark stayed dark.

"If you don't get it this time," Jude said, "I'm throwing a chair at you."

"There aren't any chairs down here."

"There are chains."

"They're fastened to the wall."

Paul tried again. The candle guttered and nothing else happened.

Jude looked around, found a brass bowl the guards used for slops standing by the door, and threw it. It went a foot wide of Paul's head and rang off the wall behind him like a bell.

"You may have magic," Paul said, not flinching, "but I can throw a thing and hit what I throw it at."

"May I?" Jude said, and lifted one finger, and spoke a single word, and a point of white light sprang into being on the silver of Paul's mask, square between the eye-slits.

Paul jerked back and threw up a hand against it. It went with his face wherever he turned it, a hard white star a finger's width from his eyes, and he could see nothing but that. He stood there, blinded, and made a gesture in Jude's direction that had not been taught him by any tutor his mother chose.

Jude walked across the room, picked up the brass bowl, and struck him across the shin with it.

Paul did not make a sound. He stood on the one leg a moment, and set the other down again, and turned the blazing mask in Jude's direction.

"Are you finished?" he said. "Being a child?"

"I'm very old, by human reckoning," Jude said. "I'm allowed."

"How old?"

"Older than you." He spoke the word that ended it, and the light went out, and the room was only candlelit again, very dim after it. "Again."

"You're going to hit me again."

"If you don't get it."

"I'll call the guards."

"Go on," Jude said. "Call them."

Paul tried again, and failed again, and Jude bent and cracked the bowl across his other knee. This time Paul hissed through his teeth.

"If you do that one more time," he said, very quietly, "I'm going to cut your heel through."

"You'll want to kill me a great many times before this is finished," Jude said. "But I'll tell you what it proves. When the fighting comes, and there's a man in full plate trying to take your head off your shoulders — and trust me, I've had that happen — you'll have it. You won't need to think. It'll be there."

Paul stood breathing for a moment in the candlelight.

"Fair enough," he said.

The third hour went the way of the second. Once, near the end of it, Jude thought he saw the air in front of Paul's lifted hand turn faintly pale, like breath on a cold morning, but it was gone before he could be sure, and Paul did not seem to have noticed it.

A guard came down to trim the lamp at the stair-head and said, when Jude asked, that it was past the seventh hour.

"Do you want to go on?" Jude asked.

"Do you?"

"One more hour. Nothing good happens except in the dark." Jude glanced toward the stair, where a thin grey light still came down from the guardroom window. "And there's still some twilight left. It's late spring—"

"Early summer."

"Early summer. It'll be light until eight. One more hour."

They did one more hour. Jude hit him once more, not hard, and Paul did not threaten him, and did not get the light. By the end of it Jude could see the fatigue in the way he stood: not in the shoulders, which Paul kept square, but in the hands, which had begun, very slightly, to shake.

"Enough," Jude said. "Come on. That's enough. It's been a good first day."

"I didn't get it."

"You got one. You got the first one quickly. The second one — don't concern yourself with it. It'll come." He picked up the candle. "I got beaten a great deal, when I was learning. Stop taking it personally. Keep some of that — coolness. Distance."

"Objectivity."

"Objectivity." Jude hesitated, and then gave him the rest of it, because it was owed. "You remember me on the Helm? When we had the Toli on the stair?"

"I remember."

"I'd have killed every Toli between there and Jerlea Bay, that night, and not stopped to count. That's what it looks like when you lose it. It feels like strength. It isn't."

Paul looked at him in the candlelight for a long moment and did not answer.

###

They went up the dungeon stair together, slowly, because Paul's knees were stiffening.

"Do you ever sleep?" Paul said, halfway up.

"What do you care?"

"It's a question with two answers."

"Yes. Sometimes."

"Sometimes."

"Mostly. I'm an elf."

"So you ask me a great many gloomy questions," Paul said. "Are you gloomy?"

"No."

Paul looked at him, at the turn of the stair where the light from the guardroom came down across them both. Jude was young. That was the first thing and the strangest: that the face was so young, younger than it had any right to be, younger perhaps than Paul's own under the mask — smooth, fine-boned, with the faint tapering of the ear showing where his hair had fallen back from it. And under that youth there was a weight Paul had never seen in any face, not his uncle's in the worst of the blockade, not in old men at the end of long lives. It sat in the eyes. Everything was in the eyes. In the dimness of the stair the pupils had opened long and narrow, like a cat's, or like something older than a cat.

"You're very young," Paul said. "And you look as though you've not slept in years."

"You're dwelling on the wrong things," Jude said. "I told you, you did well today. You've some bruised shins, that's all. I hit you with a brass bowl."

"You broke about five chairs."

"I broke one chair. It took you five tries to mend it."

"And a vase."

"Which you mended." Jude climbed another step. "Look. You have this thing. And this thing is going to get you killed, whether you're an assassin, or a thief, or a mage. You have to let it go."

"The thing."

"The thing. You know." Jude's voice went light and mocking. "Mother's dead. Father never loved me."

Paul stopped on the stair.

"You don't know that," he said.

Jude stopped a step above him and looked down. "I know your mother's dead. Everyone in Monmurg knows your mother's dead. You wear a mask. You're very mysterious. You don't want people to see your face. All right. I've never once said a word against any of it. Until now. But you're my pupil now. Which means I'm going to poke you and prod you and kick you — in every sense — until you're better than you are. If you can't bear that, you can go and learn from Gouge. But I promise you, he'll put you on a ship and have you gutting and skinning things as a test of your character."

"I'd guessed."

"You can still choose."

"So what's the equal of that, for you?" Paul said. "Blowing a man's brains out with your wand?"

"I'm not going to teach you to blow a man's brains out."

"That's what you've been teaching me all afternoon."

"It is not."

"The bolt you put through that Toli. The force. What was it?"

"Force. A bolt of it. It's not in your six."

"So you'll have me hold your toad."

"First of all," Jude said, "you'll use your fingers, not a wand. I use a wand. And second, I'm fairly sure that if you ever took a Toli prisoner, he'd not live to the end of the corridor. You'd kill him out of hand."

"Yes."

"Well, then. That's my point."

"Isn't that what you wanted to do?" Paul said. "With the one we took?"

"No."

"Yes, you did. You said so. You said you wanted to be rid of him, because something was going to go wrong. Kill him and leave him."

"And then something went wrong," Jude said, "and I killed him and left him."

"No. The rest of us said we might need him. I said we might need him. And you decided to kill him anyway."

"What are you talking about? I didn't kill him until everything had already gone to pieces." Jude's voice sharpened. "Do you remember the fool who stopped dead halfway across that plank bridge? The one I'm certain was set to drop us? It's only the stupidity of the men on the far end that kept us from all going into the water with that shark."

"I did go into the water."

"And who brought you out?"

Paul was quiet for a step. "You did."

"Levitation."

"When do I learn that one?"

"Later. You'll get a great many more brass bowls across the shins before you're ready for that. Can you bear it?"

"I'm here."

"Then stop complaining."

"I'm not complaining."

"You are."

"You want to see me complain?" Paul said. "Truly?"

"Oh, is there more?"

"There's a great deal more."

"Let's see it, then."

"You're not breaking my shins for it. We're walking up a stair."

"So what you're telling me," Jude said, "is that when you're upset, you lash out."

"Here we go."

"I'm telling you, some of these workings need your whole mind to hold them. If you're going to be all feeling and no discipline, that won't do."

"Who said I had feelings?" Paul said. "Do you hear tears?"

"Yes. Yes, and yes."

"Then someone has hurt you very badly, because there are no tears."

"You don't need tears to have a tantrum."

"If I were having a tantrum," Paul said, "I'd be kicking the walls."

Jude looked at the silver mask for a long moment in the grey light of the stair, and could not honestly have said whether the man behind it was capable of weeping at all.

They went on up, arguing.
