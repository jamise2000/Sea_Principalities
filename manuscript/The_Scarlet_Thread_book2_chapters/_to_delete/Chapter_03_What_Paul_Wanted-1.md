## PART TWO: THE FOURTH DAY

# Chapter Three — What Paul Wanted

Jude came up to the palace on the morning of the fourth of Wealsun looking entirely unhurried, as though he had slept the night through and had nowhere in particular to be.

He had come down from the tower on the headland as the sun cleared the city and gone by the back lanes to Ferd's, where Gouge and the Marines lay snoring among the benches and no one looked up as he passed. In his room he collected what he wanted. He had not tranced, and did not yet feel the lack of it. He came up the hill to the palace before the markets opened, with a satchel over one shoulder and a damp bundle of rag inside his robe. The guards at the palace postern had his name. A servant brought him through a series of rooms each quieter than the last and left him at a door in the east wing, and when it opened it was Paul himself who opened it, masked, dressed, and plainly awake for some hours.

The room behind him was a sitting room with a window over the palace gardens and a good many more books in it than furniture. The mask was not the one Paul had worn on the Helm. This one was silver, smooth and close-fitted, with narrow eye-slits and nothing at all in the way of expression. A palace mask. Jude found he disliked it slightly less than the black iron one Paul had been wearing when they met.

"You're early," Paul said.

"I'm an elf. We don't sleep. We brood." Jude came in and took the chair by the window without being asked. The morning light through the glass was thin and clean, and he sat with his back to it. "I've been thinking about Fairwind."

Paul closed the door and sat across from him. "What about him?"

"I'm not sure we should have killed him," Jude said. "If we did kill him. Perhaps we didn't. I don't know whether Prince Jeon and Fairwind could ever have mended it between them in any case." He looked at Paul. "Did you want him dead?"

"I wanted him in custody."

"So did I, at the time. That's why I tried to bring him out. When we lost him on the stair, I wasn't sure — I wasn't sure whether you'd wanted him dead. And then I found I couldn't say what I'd have done with him if we'd got him home. I wouldn't have known what to do with him."

"Arrest him," Paul said. "He's a traitor. It's treason."

"We never had time to think about it. Not going down through the keep, not on the raft, not on the corsair." Jude leaned forward and put his elbows on his knees. "You said, 'We've got him.' Dead to rights, you said. I believe you. But I've never actually heard from you what you heard. What did he say?"

"I already told you."

"You told me he was dealing with the Toli. Plotting. I'm asking what you heard, exactly."

Paul was quiet a moment, and Jude watched him go back for it.

"He was talking with them about moving it," Paul said. "Getting rid of it — selling it on. The powder. And one of the Toli said, 'Well, we noticed you took most of it anyway.'"

"Most of it."

"Of the powder. Yes."

"And he'd said as much himself, on the way down, once we had him tied." Jude nodded slowly. "All right."

"And then," Paul said, "on the stair in the cave. When I'd gone into the water and you were bringing me back up. He was talking to Gouge about something. What was he saying?"

"To Gouge?" Jude frowned. "No. Gouge had gone forward — or someone had. The whole trouble on that stair was—" He stopped and shook his head. "It wasn't Gouge. It was the bard. He'd dropped back beside the bard and had his head down, murmuring to him, from the moment we started down. I saw it and didn't hear any of it. The moment you went off the edge he went for Gouge, and Gouge threw him over."

"The bard," Paul said. His voice did not change, but Jude saw his hands go still on the arms of his chair. "What was he telling the bard?"

"I've no idea. I'd like to know." Jude filed it with the other things he would like to know. "And I don't know the man's name. It's been annoying me since last night."

"Folsom."

"Folsom." Jude repeated it once, to fix it. "Thank you."

He sat back and let the silence go on for a moment. Somewhere below the window a gardener was raking gravel, a long patient scraping that came and went.

"I've never been to Fairwind's holdings," Jude said. "His island, his ports. Have you?"

"I've been to the main harbor. There was a feast there, a few years ago. I've seen what they show visitors." Paul shrugged. "I didn't go poking about. I've never toured the island."

"So if we have to go and find where he's hidden his stock, you'd have no more idea than I would where to begin."

"Not of where."

"Then let me ask you something else. What sort of man is he? Is Fairwind the kind who keeps what he values close — physically close, where he can put his hand on it? Or the kind who hides things away somewhere else entirely? A different island, somewhere far off?"

Paul considered it, and Jude watched him do it and was pleased by how he did it: not reaching for the first answer, but going back through what he had seen of the man, at the feast, in the sanctum, bound in a chair and still behaving as though the room belonged to him.

"The outer islands are old pirate stock," Paul said at last. "All of them. Fairwind's house more than most. They made their fortunes on hidden coves and buried chests and cargo that never touched a customs dock. He's that school through and through. He wouldn't put it all in one place."

"Divided."

"Divided. Several places. And if he's true to type, he'll have one place that's well defended and dangerous to get into — somewhere obvious, somewhere anyone looking would look first. That's the one he wants found. And then the others, that are simply secret."

"A decoy and a scatter."

"Yes."

"How big are the outer islands? I've never seen them charted properly. Are we speaking of a handful of rocks within a day's sail of his hall, or—"

"Hundreds of square miles," Paul said. "Not just his island. Islets, reefs, sea caves. You couldn't search it. Not with a fleet."

Jude swore quietly. "Then we don't search it. We find someone who knows."

He thought about how to put the next thing and decided to put it straight.

"You're of Jeon's family," he said.

"Yes."

"Which might mean your knowledge of some things is more limited than you'd think — because you're young, and because of how the family keeps its business. I'd not expect you to know much in particular about the Toli. Or about Fairwind. His habits. His ventures. Where he's sailed and why."

"I probably know more than most people would."

"I'm sure you do. I'm not asking for anything definite now. Only give it some thought." Jude weighed how much to say, and said a little. "For instance. I've had dealings with the Scarlet Brotherhood, in the past. If I have reason to suspect there's a close association between the Brotherhood and the Toli — and I do — it colors how I look at the Toli. A great deal."

"I don't know anything about that."

"No. I'm not asking you to. I'm only saying it's the kind of thing I mean." Jude opened a hand. "Things you'd have heard at your uncle's table when you were growing up. Talk over dinner that went past you at the time. If we're going to find where Fairwind has hidden his barrels, we need to know where he's been. Raids he's led. Places he's put in. Waters he knows."

"Then we should go to the library," Paul said. "The Admiralty sends copies of its logs and returns there. Every prince's ship that sails under the council's commission reports where she's been. We'd find his voyages. Where he's watered, where he's careened. That would tell us more than anything I remember."

Jude looked at him with real interest. "I'll certainly be going to the library."

"You'd have to go with me to get into that part of it."

"Then I'll go with you. I appreciate it."

Paul inclined his head a quarter of an inch.

"The Helm," Jude said. "What do you make of it now? Would you call it a success?"

"No."

"No."

"I haven't spoken to Lord Jamis," Paul said. "Only my uncle. This morning, at first light, for a little while."

"But you've told Jeon. Did he ask you much about Fairwind?"

"He wanted to know what happened. All of it."

"And how did he take it? When you told him what Fairwind had done?"

"He was upset." Paul said it without inflection. "He didn't want to go to war with him."

"But he believed you."

"Yes. He believed me."

"You didn't have to argue with him? He wasn't making excuses for Fairwind?"

"No. Not really. He doesn't want a war. But he wants Fairwind found, and he wants him to answer for it."

"Good," Jude said, and meant several things by it.

"He wants you and me to find him," Paul said. "Take him, if we can, and bring him home. But first he wants us to find the powder and work out how to make it. Find where Fairwind took his barrels, and bring them back. Get Fairwind, if it can be done." He paused. "And he wants you to teach me to be a wizard."

"Prince Jeon said that?"

"Yes."

Jude studied the silver mask. It told him nothing, which he supposed was the point of it. "And how do you feel about that?"

"I feel fine about it."

"Is it something you want?"

"Yes." Paul's hands moved very slightly on the chair arms. "It benefits the family. We aren't spellcasters, my uncle's house. We never have been. We buy it when we need it. One of our own who could do it would be worth a great deal to them."

"So he's trying to mend a weakness he sees in the family."

"Yes."

"And your homunculus," Jude said. "You did make that yourself."

"I did."

"From a book."

"I'm an intelligent man," Paul said. "I think."

"I'm sure you are."

"We're about equal."

Jude laughed, once, in spite of himself. "I doubt that."

"I don't see you making homunculi," Paul said.

It was a fair hit and Jude let it land. He had told Paul to his face, not long ago, that the creatures were a fairground swindle, and had been shown a black clay lizard running across a ceiling for his trouble. "No," he said. "You don't. It tells me you have a bent for it, which is a good place to start. And you're curious. That's better." He sat back. "So. He wants Fairwind found. The powder made. The powder first, I'd think. That's his first concern?"

"We have months," Paul said. "That's what he told me. Before what's left runs out."

"Months. Perhaps more, if there's more to be had."

"If the twenty-one barrels are still in the grotto on the Helm."

"They should be secured by now. Or soon." Jude frowned. "Do we need to go back to the Helm? To help drive out the rest of the hobgoblins?"

"Nothing was said about us going back," Paul said. "He asked me whether it was safe for ships to go in. He means to send them."

"With troops."

"With troops, and with barrels aboard. Jamis is to see to it."

"Then he has it in hand." Jude let out a breath. "Good. I'd go if we were needed. But I'd rather stay here and show you how to go about this kind of research. Alchemical research. It's slower than fighting hobgoblins, and it matters more." He was quiet for a moment. "Do you remember — you may not have seen it — when I was cutting the gills out of that dead sahaugin in the grotto?"

"I saw you doing something to it."

"I'd meant to use them for a water-breathing draught. It seemed a waste to leave them. And then on the way home, on the corsair, it came to me that the creature was dead because of what we'd put in the water. The powder. It went into the gills and killed it. It's almost like an acid to them."

"Right."

"So we've seen the strength of it with our own eyes. It killed that shark — and that shark was forty feet long — and it killed eight or ten of the sahaugin in the cove, and it did it fast. So the potency isn't in question."

"One barrel keeps the whole strait," Paul said.

"So I'm told. But what Lord Jamis has asked me to find out is whether there was something else. A counter-agent. Something that was used to undo it." Jude reached into his robe and drew out the damp bundle of rag, and the smell of it came into the room — brine, and old blood, and something sharper and more chemical underneath. He did not unwrap it. "So. We take these. We examine them. They'll show us what the powder does to a gill. Whether it swells them, or burns them, or congeals the blood in them. But there may also be traces left in them — if there was some counter-agent that had been used to push back an earlier salting. Remember, the salting around the Helm has to be renewed every day or two. If something had been working against it before the last barrel went in, there may be some sign of it still in the tissue."

"So we start with the gills."

"We start with the gills." Jude put the bundle away again. "Do you know the alchemist's shop, just off the square in the foreign district?"

"Of course I do."

"No, you don't."

Paul's mask turned toward him and held there.

"There's a good alchemist there," Jude said, "and I've been trying to get to know him. This is one more reason to call on him. We can buy some materials. And ask whether he knows of any reagents we could use to find out if something else is present in these — something that would counter the powder, still in the flesh, even though the thing died of the powder in the end."

"But why would anyone make a counter to it?" Paul said. "Who'd want that?"

"Not us. Not us." Jude shook his head. "What worried Lord Jamis is this. The waters around the Helm were salted with the powder shortly before that fog came in. A salting lasts a couple of days. So it should have kept the sahaugin off. It didn't."

"So it could have been false."

"It could have been a barrel of sand. Yellow sand, instead of the powder."

"That doesn't account for the hobgoblins."

"No, no. The hobgoblins came from outside. I don't even think Fairwind brought them. I think the Toli did — on the Sea Ghost, under that fog. That would be my guess. They came in under cover of it."

"But Fairwind was working with the Toli," Paul said.

"I don't disagree. But I think Fairwind was working for himself. I don't think he's working for the Toli."

"No. I agree."

"He saw a chance," Jude said, "and he took it. With the Toli, yes, but not for them. You told me yourself the Toli said he'd taken most of it. Which means he'd been taking it for himself."

"So then what did the Toli get out of it?"

"They got some of the barrels. And that was the last of it. That was what finally stripped the Helm bare." Jude hesitated. The next thing on his tongue was Jamis's — the theory that Fairwind had meant all along to lay the empty stores at the Toli's door — and he could not remember, for a moment, exactly where the line Jamis had drawn for him ran. He decided to keep well back from it. "It gave him someone to blame," he said. "If anyone ever came looking."

"He could blame the Toli," Paul said slowly. "Which means he's still working for himself first."

"That's my suspicion. With the Toli, alongside them, under cover of them. But what he wants is more power."

"The one thing I keep coming back to," Paul said, "is the signals."

Jude looked up.

"Before we sailed. They were saying it at the Admiralty, and on the corsair. The last day or two, the lamp signals from the Helm weren't right. They were sending, but the codes didn't answer. The countersigns were wrong." Paul's head tilted. "The powder only lasts a day or two. So did the sahaugin simply wait it out? Wait for a salting to wear thin and then come in?"

"I'd thought the hobgoblins took the citadel, and then the signals went wrong because it was hobgoblins sending them."

"They came in under that fog."

"They came under the fog. They took the citadel. And by taking the citadel they took the salting — and the signal tower." Jude stopped. "And that's why the signals didn't match."

"But," Paul said.

"But." Jude sat forward again. "If Fairwind had really been working as closely with the Toli as all that — hand in glove — he'd have known the right countersign. He could have given it to them. Or sent it himself. The Toli would have wanted the right code going out. Nothing to alarm anyone. Everything quiet on the Helm."

"And instead it was wrong."

They looked at each other.

It came to both of them at nearly the same moment, and it was Paul who said it, very quietly.

"He was playing them against each other."

"He wanted Jeon to see the signals were wrong," Jude said. "At the same time, the Toli very likely thought he was sending exactly what they wanted sent. They'd not know our codes."

"He was saying, 'Warning. Something's wrong. Come out and look.'"

"While his hands were full of the Toli's gold. So that when Jeon's ships arrived, he'd be the loyal lord who raised the alarm." Jude laughed shortly. "And he was, too. He did raise it."

"That's what I was saying."

"That's what I was saying." Jude sat back. "He's out for himself. Mostly for himself."

"I'd worked that out."

"I know you had. I'm not saying you didn't. I'm saying this proves it." Jude turned it over again and liked it better each time. "In a way, it's as if he's saying to Jeon, 'Respect me.' Lord Jamis told me Fairwind has always been jealous of Monmurg — of Prince Jeon. So it's not that he isn't working with the Toli. Obviously he is, to some degree. But he's not in their pocket. I had the same sense of him when we had him tied. He's still his own man. If he weren't, we'd never have had that signal. We'd never have had the wrong code."

"So."

"So I think he may still be willing to deal. If what he wants is respect — and power's how he measures respect — then perhaps we take the plain road for once. Instead of creeping round behind him, we go and talk to him." Jude spread his hands. "We didn't kill him. Whatever happened to him in that chasm, it wasn't us. We only killed the two Toli."

"You killed the Toli," Paul said. "Both of them, as it happens. I didn't. I was in the water."

"I think," Jude said, "I've more Toli to kill before I'm done."

"I can see it."

"I'll kill every one of them." He said it lightly, and it did not come out as lightly as he had meant, and he let it go. "In any case. It's a thought. I'd like to know what you make of it — the idea that we don't creep around Fairwind. That we try dealing with him."

"We could put it to Lord Jamis," Paul said. "And to my uncle."

"When you see Jamis, why don't you raise it?"

"You're not coming with me?"

"No. I've things to do." Jude patted his robe, where the rag bundle sat. "I'm going to get started on these. They won't keep."

"And we need more—" Paul stopped. "We need agents. People. Just — people to start looking."

"We do." Jude looked at him a moment, weighing something, and then set it down in front of him. "May I give you some advice?"

"Yes."

"Do you like to take it straight? Or dressed up?"

Paul's mask considered him. "Straight."

"Good," Jude said. "Then listen."
