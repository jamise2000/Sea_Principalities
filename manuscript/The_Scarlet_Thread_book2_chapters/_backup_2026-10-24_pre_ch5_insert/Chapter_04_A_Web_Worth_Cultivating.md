# Chapter Four — A Web Worth Cultivating

"Every man who serves another," Jude said, "serves himself first. Hold on to that, and the rest is only a matter of finding out what he serves himself with. I've watched your people a long time, and I count seven kinds of servant. Know which kind a man is, and you'll know what to give him and what to watch for." He put up a finger. "We'll start with the one nearest to hand. We have the thief."

"Which thief?" Paul said. "Gregory? Or that faithless partner of his, Jobs?"

"Gregory. Jobs can wait. Gregory is what I'd call a servant of circumstance. He never chose to serve anyone. He's a desperate man who tried his knife on the wrong purse, and now he's caught the eye of a powerful lord who could have him hanged or lift him out of the gutter. A man in that position will be very useful to us, for a while, in a way he would never be otherwise. He was supposed to come to Ferd's. When he does, word will reach me."

"It's been three days," Paul said.

"I know it's been three days. He'll come, or he won't. But—" Jude lowered his voice. "You have to be the one who does the talking when we finish turning him, if he is to be your man. Not me. A servant of circumstance is easily made, but hard to keep. In some cases, if you know what you're doing, he can be made into a slave. A man has to feel he's trespassed somewhere, and carry some guilt for it, before he'll bind himself to anyone. With Gregory, that part is easy. He meant to rob and kill you but failed, and you spared him. The guilt is already there; we need only tend it. And the law is on your side of it, which he knows as well as you do. One word from you and he hangs. But the guilt he carries is owed to you, not to me. It was you he attacked. You saw it in the cell yourself: it was the mask in the corner he kept looking at, not the elf asking the questions. So you have to be the face of this. I can be the one they come to, the cut-out, but they have to know there's power behind me, at least at first."

"I don't know where Ferd's is."

Jude looked at him for a moment. Of everything he had just said, the young man had fastened on the name of a tavern. Jude let it pass. Paul would learn which things mattered soon enough, and patience would teach him faster than a rebuke. "You've been all over the foreign district, or so you told me. You know where the alchemist's shop is. If that's true, you've very likely walked past Ferd's door."

"Then it doesn't have much of a sign."

"It doesn't have a sign at all. A place like Ferd's doesn't want one. It's where mercenaries go when their luck has run out, and men who will kill for the price of a meal. Those who need it find it. As for you, that's easily put right." Jude waved a hand. "I'll show you where Ferd's is. We'll go down together. I'll introduce you to Ferd, and he'll let you into his place and give you whatever help you need, any time you need it."

"Ferd is the name of the place, or the man?"

"Both. The man first. He was a soldier. A Marine, years ago, and he's not left the city in twenty years since. He keeps a loaded crossbow under his bar and a very good memory above it."

Paul absorbed that without comment. Behind the silver mask nothing moved.

"Understand what a man like Gregory feels for you," Jude went on. "It isn't loyalty. He doesn't see a leader when he looks at you. He sees the rope. He lives by your good graces, and he knows it, and he'll serve you exactly as long as he believes it. That can change. Treat him well enough for long enough and it can grow into something nearer patronage. But not yet, and not quickly, and never by your showing him anything soft." He turned a hand palm up. "Fear isn't the only thing that holds a servant of circumstance. Distance holds him too. He should never see you uncertain. He should never see you angry, or pleased, or tired. He should see a man above him, who could end him without raising his voice, and who hasn't yet chosen to."

"The mask helps," Paul said.

"The mask helps a great deal." Jude's mouth moved, very slightly. "Don't let that make you careless."

"And if Gregory ever drops me," Paul said, "I can have his whole family hanged."

Jude looked at him.

"I mean," Paul said, "if I leave word — if he ever betrays me, if I'm ever brought down — his family goes to the rope. So he knows."

"Let's not go there," Jude said. "Leave threats and scorched earth to me. I'm very good at it, and you should be above such petty details. Gregory should never think you would have to order such a thing. It would simply happen."

Paul was silent for a moment. "Very well."

"I know he tried to kill you. But I think that was—"

"I don't think it was personal."

"Cutpurse work. Sloppy cutpurse work, if we're honest." Jude considered the window. "I'd wager he'd give a good deal to have a patron like you."

"And Jobs?" Paul said. "His partner. The one who ran and left him to us. Should we try to turn him too?"

"Not yet. A man who'd leave his partner in the street like that would leave you just as quickly. If he's worth having at all, he'll come cheaper once he sees Gregory has a patron and he hasn't." He spread his hands. "But Gregory first."

"That's one kind of servant," Paul said. "What is next?"

"That's one. The second is the best kind there is, and the hardest to come by." Jude leaned forward, elbows on his knees. "A servant of obligation. You've met two already. What do you suppose holds Gouge and Merrick to Lord Jamis?"

Paul thought about it. "Not coin, I'd have said. Gouge could sell that knife of his to any lord in the Hold for more than Jamis pays him, and he hasn't. Merrick is good enough at what he does to have found a richer master years ago, if he'd wanted one." He paused. "Fear, then? I wouldn't care to be the man who crossed Lord Jamis. But Gouge doesn't strike me as a man who fears anyone, and Merrick doesn't serve like a frightened man. He serves like a man paying something priceless back." Another pause. "It's something else. I don't know what to call it."

"I do. Obligation. I don't know what Jamis did for Gouge. Saved his life, perhaps, or his neck, or something he valued more than either. Whatever it was, Gouge has never forgotten it, and he never will. Merrick's the same, though his debt is plainer. Jamis made his career. Every rank he holds, he holds because Jamis put him there, and he knows it. These are free men. Either of them could walk away tomorrow and no law in the principalities would stop him. They stay because they believe they owe a debt, and because everyone they grew up among believes a debt like that must be paid. Gratitude. Duty. A man's word to a man who did right by him. Your people honor those things, even the ones who've never practised them, and the servant of obligation lives inside that honor." Jude sat back. "That's how it's done. Not just with a purse. With an act. You do something for a man at a moment when it matters to him more than anything, and you ask nothing back. Then you wait. Some of them forget. The good ones don't."

"You saw the other side of it on the Helm," he added. "Fairwind, tied to a chair, a traitor by his own mouth, and Tyrus and Folsom still standing a little straighter when he spoke to them. Even Merrick, for a while. He was a lord of the Hold, and they were raised to give a lord his due. It wasn't really the man they were standing straight for. It was his name, and the house behind it, and every lord of the Hold their fathers had taught them to honor. With your kind, loyalty runs down the generations. A man can owe a lord what his father owed the lord's father, and pay it without ever asking what the debt was for. Half the respect a lord gets is what was owed to his parents. That's how deep it goes."

Paul gave a slight nod. "The other four, then. From the Helm. Tyrus. Folsom. Gouge. Merrick. Would they be worth cultivating?"

"Branch out." Jude said it gently, for him. "Gouge is loyal to Jamis and no one else. Merrick is bent the same way. The other two? Those are Gouge's men now. Even the ones who are properly Jamis's — if Gouge is running with them, they're Gouge's. That doesn't mean they're loyal to him, mind. I couldn't tell you where their loyalty lies. But I've no intention of reaching for fruit Gouge has already taken a bite of. An obligation is owed to one man. You can't borrow it. But it doesn't die with him, either. If Gouge ever falls, those men will be left holding a loyalty with nowhere to put it, and they'll look for the nearest man worth giving it to. Be someone they already know, who's been decent to them and asked nothing. You'd be very well placed."

"I understand that."

"So you must find men who need something only you can give."

"Or have already given," Paul said slowly.

"Or have already given." Jude pointed at him, pleased. "The soldiers. The twenty. The Marines who were with us on the quays at Helm Island. Those men are alive because of you and me. If they hadn't come ashore with us, they'd have gone down with the ship. They know it. You've already done for them the very thing I've been describing, and the debt they feel for it is owed to you. Don't ever hold it over them. Never say it. You won't need to. When you see Lord Jamis, ask him who led them — I've already raised it with him — the officer, or the sergeant, whoever was in charge of that twenty. You start with him. And in a time like this, when there isn't enough bread to go round, you let that sergeant have some coin. So long as he doesn't keep it all for himself. Enough that he can see some extra food onto the tables of the men with families. A debt like theirs is the start of a bond, not the whole of it. You keep it alive by going on doing right by them. Do that, and you'll have Marines on your side. At first they'll be eyes and ears for you in the Harbor District. In time, they may become guards you can trust."

"I thought those twenty were still on the island," Paul said.

"We don't know yet. We don't know how many of them lived after we left. They were probably taken off when the corsairs came in." Jude frowned at the window. "I don't even know how they'll have sorted things out with Fairwind's men. If Fairwind's off the island, there may be three hundred of his Marines still there who've no idea what he did. I'd hope they're not locked up. We've just lost two hundred of our own."

"Fairwind said he'd put his men—"

"All he said was that he'd shut them up in parts of the keep. They weren't armed. He kept them from the armory. That was perhaps a hundred, a hundred and fifty, in the citadel. Don't forget the hundred and fifty in the barracks."

"The ones who made the diversion."

"Right. And those were Fairwind's too. They were with us, at the time. They seemed loyal enough to the principalities." Jude shrugged. "So it depends on how Prince Jeon has instructed the captains he sent on the corsairs. How he means to deal with them. I don't know. But the twenty, when they come back — they're good fighters. Don't take that from them. We're alive, you and I, because they held a very fine shield wall." He was quiet a moment. "There's one sort of servant of obligation who comes more cheaply than any other, and binds himself harder. I'll come to him last."

Paul's mask tilted, a fraction. He did not ask.

"Third," Jude said, "the servant of coin. Sellswords. Caravan guards. The men who drink at Ferd's while they wait for work. They'll serve whoever pays, for as long as he pays, and they don't pretend otherwise. There's an honesty in that you can use. Pay them and they're loyal enough; they'll fight for you, and fight well. What they won't do is die for you. No purse ever made a man glad to be killed. The day the danger outweighs the wage, or someone else offers a better one, he's gone. Mind, few of them took up the sword for the pay alone. Most are drawn to the fighting and the road, and they'll often risk a good deal more than the coin is worth for the sake of the thing itself. But that's their own appetite they're feeding, not your cause, and you can't command it any more than you can command the weather."

"The hobgoblins," Paul said.

"The hobgoblins?" Jude tilted his head. "Perhaps. There was money at work on the Helm, that much is plain. Hobgoblins don't come down out of the Hellfurnaces for the sea air. But who paid, and who took the purse, I couldn't tell you. The Toli, by the look of it. Fairwind, perhaps, or whoever was paying Fairwind. Their captain may have sold his company for gold, and the rank and file behind him may never have seen a copper of it; they may have been serving their chieftain, or their own people, or something I haven't thought of. They fought for someone, and they fought well. I'd want to know a good deal more before I called them servants of coin." He tapped a finger on his knee. "Which brings me to something practical. Rooms can be had in the lanes and tenements around Ferd's for very little now. A cheap set of them, near enough that a man could walk between there and Ferd's without crossing an open street, would make you an office of a kind. Somewhere discreet, where you could receive people who'll never in their lives be let through a palace gate. Gregory, to begin with, and others like him. But it would serve just as well for the servants of coin. You don't bring them to the palace, and you don't want them seen at your door. Something cheap and unremarkable to begin with. It can grow into something else." He paused. "See Ferd's first. Then tell me whether you can stomach the neighborhood."

Paul nodded slowly.

"And when you pay them, pay properly. Coppers won't buy you anything but a knife in the back. But you'll want men of your own besides. All men work for coin, one way or another, but a mercenary is loyal to nothing else. Other men are moved by other things. Gratitude. Duty. Pride in their work. Love of their families, or of their prince. Those are the men you want, the ones who'll serve you for loyalty as well as wages. They're harder to find, and they're worth far more, because you can rely on them. Which is the fourth kind." Jude settled back. "Loyal servants. Men who've sworn themselves to a lord or to a government and serve it in an office. The captains of the watch. The clerks and secretaries. Heralds, scribes, physicians, musicians. The steward's under-men. The ones who carry letters and the ones who pour the wine and hear what's said over it. In the long term you'll have to begin courting your own people inside the palace. Men loyal to you, but in the service of your uncle and the princes. A sword at your back is worth having, but so is the man who knows which petition reaches your uncle first, and why. When you're looking for people to cultivate — and by cultivate I mean people for your web — do you even know where to begin?"

"I have some thoughts. I'm not sure they're the right ones." Paul spoke slowly, feeling his way. "Be civil to them, I suppose. Learn their names. Ask after a cook's daughter, or a groom's bad knee, and look as though the answer matters to me. Praise a man's work when it's nothing out of the ordinary. Defer a little more than my rank requires, so they think me humble and easy to talk to." He paused. "A performance, mostly. I don't know that I'd do it well."

"It's a beginning. But you're looking at the whole household, and you want only a part of it." Jude held up a hand. "A loyal servant can be trusted, so long as his living and his hopes of advancement aren't cut back too far. He feels safe in his station. That doesn't mean he's content in it. The clever ones have gone as high in service as they will ever go, and if they're clever — or talented — they may well want more from life than that."

"Then how are they any different from servants of coin?" Paul said. "If what they want is more than they have, and I'm the one with the purse, I'm only a better-paying master."

"Not only money. You represent — if they were your man, your loyal servant — a chance of rising as you rise. The clever ones in Prince Jeon's service will see that. Join your camp, and they can expect to rise as you do, without ever ceasing to serve him."

"Who, then?"

"Here's one way to find them. The clever lieutenant. Loyal. Shows no bitterness, no resentment. Say there's a lieutenant under the commander of the watch, or of the gate, and you see him in some meeting giving the commander something worth having — and being slightly passed over for it. Not thought much of. But he's there, and you can see it." Jude sat forward. "Or the sergeant. You've met one. The sergeant who drove those twenty up the quay on the Helm, and kept them in their places while the wall climbed. Nobody in the palace will ever hear of it unless you tell them. That's the man. The sergeant who does an enormous amount of work, and doesn't spend his days fawning on his commander, because the commander just assumes he'll do his job. But you, watching, see that he's a very able man. Able enough to be a commander himself."

"The unappreciated ones."

"The unappreciated underlings. Still loyal." Jude nodded. "The man who does all the paperwork. Who runs everything tight, and that the lieutenant or the commander simply assumes will always be done right. That man. He's very likely unappreciated. Or at least kept where he is. He'll never be promoted." Jude lifted a finger. "Remember this. People in power don't like to promote the people under them who make them look good. Because to promote them usually means promoting them out of your service. So they keep them where they are. Not always out of spite. They keep them trapped because those men make them look good to the people they want to look good to."

"It's not the rank and file who get noticed," Paul said. "It's the generals."

"It's not only the rank and file who get passed over, though. Think of all the ships' lieutenants under captains who dream of the Admiralty. Those captains will never give a good lieutenant a ship of his own. Because he makes their ship run so well."

"And the men at the top?"

"Stay away from them. The stewards of the royal apartments. The chamberlains. The masters of the household. They're usually very comfortable. They've made their nests. And they're far more often double agents than anything else." Jude weighed his words. "Take someone like Lord Jamis. Not the lord of a place — the other man. The one who actually runs it, under the lord. You don't go after that man. Not because he can't be had. Because he already has his own web, and you'll only be caught in it. Wait him out instead. A man like that dies one day, the same as anyone, or he gets caught in his own web. Then everything he held is lying loose, and it goes to whoever is nearest and ready."

Paul understood what he was being told, and understood also that Jude would not say it outright, and was careful not to smile. Lord Jamis was not a man anyone would draw away from Prince Jeon. Least of all Paul. But Jamis was not young, and a web did not die with the spider that spun it. Someone, one day, would inherit his. Paul turned that thought over once, and put it away for later.

"Now," Jude said. "Never, ever ask a loyal servant to betray his lord. They have to be able to see that serving you doesn't set them against their master. That's why I say loyal servants who are talented. Who've hit the ceiling. And it's why I say never ask them to betray, because you in turn don't want to be betrayed. It'll be understood. I can't think of any situation in which you'd betray Prince Jeon, or Lord Jamis. So don't ask anyone you cultivate to do it either, and don't hint that you'd ever expect it. You'll lift a great deal of fear off them at once. You'll make it easy for them to come close to you, because they don't have to go against their own sense of loyalty to do it. That's how you make bonds."

"And don't use only money," he went on. "In a time like this — money will get you your poor townsman, certainly. But men are also clever enough to bleed you dry with it. There's a limit to anyone's purse. And you don't want to be seen to be buying their friendship, because that means someone else can buy their enmity. You have to actually show care. Real care. If you're not lying to them, and you truly would, for their talents, see them advanced to whatever post you might hold one day that's grander than now — then you would actually be looking after them. Patronage, when you cultivate it, is a lopsided thing. It says you have and they have not. But what the patronage suggests — what it promises — is that you're interested in their skills, their talents, and that you'd like to see them rise. You have to make them feel that more than you make them feel your money. Money, in a time like this, can help them feed themselves and the people they love. That will buy you more than you can measure. But if you only throw money about without seeming to care whether they get on—"

"Then someone with more money buys them."

"Just so. You've seen what that looks like at the very top." Jude's voice flattened. "Fairwind served the Hold all his life, by every account, and served it well. A man doesn't sell a secret like the powder for gold alone. Gold and a grievance. The grievance was there first; the gold only came and found it. Somebody watched him the way I'm telling you to watch your sergeant, and saw the bitterness he was too clever to show, and went to him with a purse before anyone in Monmurg thought to go to him with anything else."

Paul said nothing for a moment. "I've seen many servants with grievances. What are they?"

"The fifth kind. Disloyal servants. They were loyal servants once, and they've gone sour. They're the obviously embittered ones. They're usually very sarcastic, and they complain where anyone can hear them. Every great house has a few. The groom who mutters over the horses. The under-cook who'll tell any stranger how little she's thanked." Jude shook his head. "They aren't worth cultivating. A servant who lets his bitterness show where his betters can see it is a fool, and a fool is no use to you. It doesn't mean the loyal ones aren't bitter and sarcastic too. It only means they know better than to show it. That's why you want to reach the loyal ones before they turn openly bitter. Leave it too long, and you've lost them."

Paul turned that over. He had been told for most of his life, by his uncle and by his uncle's advisors, what he was to the house of Monmurg: a nephew, of uncertain use, kept close. He had not, until this moment, given much thought to what he might be to anyone else. It came to him now, not all at once but piece by piece, what Jude was actually describing. Every servant served himself first. The loyal ones and the disloyal ones wanted the same things: a warm bed, a full belly, a little more than the man beside them, and a master who would not look too closely at how they came by it. What divided them was only temper. The loyal went about their duties cheerfully, and believed their own cheer; the disloyal went about the same duties sourly, and let it show. Strip that away and there was nothing between them but manners. Paul wondered how long Jude had been looking at men that way, and how cold a view it was of humankind. Then he thought of the stewards and chamberlains and grooms of his uncle's house, all the faces he had known his whole life, and could not think of one who proved the elf wrong.

"Are there no truly loyal servants, then?" Paul said slowly. "Men who would give their lives for their lord and their country without being asked? Whose devotion is beyond question?"

"What you're describing," Jude said, "is a loyal slave. That's the seventh kind, and to get there we have to pass through the sixth." He laced his fingers. "A slave is a man whose life his lord can end in an instant, and no one else would so much as blink. He has no standing among his own people and no one to appeal to. The principalities know all about slaves. They sell more of them than any nation in the Flanaess, most of them taken out of the Amedio jungle, and in Berghof and Hokar the lord holds life and death over every slave on his land, and keeps the hobgoblins to see it done. Those are disloyal slaves, nearly all of them. They serve because there's nothing else they can do. They don't look at their master and see a god, or a father, or a mother. They see a man with a whip and a rope, and they obey him for exactly as long as the whip and the rope are there. Fear holds them, and fear is the worst binding there is. A frightened man will sell your secret to the next buyer, if he ever finds one."

"And the loyal slave?"

"A loyal slave looks at his lord and sees a god. He would rather die himself than see that god challenged. He can't imagine the world without his master in it; if the master falls, he has nothing left to be. You can't build that out of coin or kindness. It needs a people raised to it from the cradle, and the Sea Principalities aren't made that way. Not Monmurg, at least, and not the Outer Islands. Men here are too used to bargaining, and too proud. The Toli, or the Plars?" He shrugged. "I don't know enough of either to say. But think of what Gregory told us. Four Toli assassins, noble-born, trained from birth in murder and magic both. If I wanted to know what a loyal slave looks like with a knife in his hand, I'd start there."

Paul was quiet. Somewhere in the palace a door closed, and footsteps went away down a gallery.

"So you must start smaller," Jude said, "because right now you need agents whose loyalty remains to Jeon. Some of them will be your eyes. What's being said in the army, in the barracks, on the ships. Some of them will be the men who stand at your back in the foreign district while you talk to people you'd rather not turn your back on. You talk your uncle up to them, always. It's all for Prince Jeon. But know what moves each man you take on, because it won't be the same thing twice. You give them your patronage, and you have a small company of your own. And the best of that company are the ones who'll stand with you against your rivals, when they come, and go against them when you ask it."

"Rivals?" Paul said. "I don't have any rivals."

"You will. Every man who stands to lose if you rise." Jude considered him. "Cousins with an eye on your uncle's favor. Courtiers who've made a living out of being the only voice in his ear. Captains who'd rather the next command went to their own man. Most of them won't think of themselves as your rivals yet, and some of them will smile at you across the table. Watch who goes quiet when you're praised, and who turns friendly when you stumble. Your people will tell you the rest. That's what they're for."

"You said there was one more," Paul said. "A servant of obligation who comes cheaply."

"I did." Jude leaned back, and for a while he looked not at Paul but at the light on the gravel beyond the window, as though the thing he wanted to say were written there and he were reading it off. "You have to understand how your kind are made first. A man fears and loves four things, and in this order. A god. His father. His mother. His lord. For a small child the first three are one and the same. His father and mother are his gods. They know everything, they can do anything, and the world goes on because they hold it up. And then, if he's lucky, he grows up beside them, and he watches them fail. He sees his father drunk, or wrong, or afraid. He hears his mother lie. And it's that, watching the gods turn into people, that makes him a man. He takes all that fear and love, which has to go somewhere, and he gives it to a lord, or to a god who stays in his temple and can't be caught out. That's the whole of human loyalty, as far as I can make it out. A child's worship, grown up and given to someone new."

"And the cheap servant?"

"Bastards," Jude said. "Bastard children are very good candidates for henchmen, because of their character flaws. A child who grows up without a father's hand on his shoulder grows up without a mooring. He never watches his father fail, because his father isn't there to fail. So the man stays a god. He grows in the dark, the way a thing does when it's never looked at, until he's finer and stronger and wiser than any man who ever lived. The boy never gets to grow out of him. Nothing ties him to his place in the world, so he drifts, and he knows that he drifts. He goes through life looking for someone to tell him he's worth the keeping, and the older he gets the hungrier he is for it. That need is the flaw. It's also the door. Whoever answers it, he'll follow."

Paul did not move at all.

"They usually stand to inherit nothing," Jude went on. "They're seldom loved by their families. And if they're young enough still, they tend to — go on hoping. Resenting the betters who never seem to notice them, and still dreaming of the day they'll be taken in. They've some sense of loyalty to their father still, and they haven't yet learned that they're very likely finished for the rest of their lives."

Paul was grateful for the silver mask. Heat had climbed his neck and into his face, and he could feel it there, hot and childish: the old shame of a boy who hears himself described by strangers and pretends he has not. *Seldom loved by their families.* He had been kept close in his uncle's house for twenty years the way a man keeps a key he does not expect to use: safe, in a drawer, out of sight. His mother was dead. His father he had come to know only through a stack of letters and a book of clay and blood. Did Jude know that? Did he know Paul was a bastard, and that his mother was dead? Paul could not be sure how much the elf had been told, or had guessed. If he knew, was this bait, a line cast to see whether he would rise to it, whether the mask would hold? Or was it only the elf's way, to speak plainly of a thing and let it fall where it fell? He could not tell. He was not sure Jude ever said anything without meaning something by it. He kept his hands where they were and his head where it was and waited for his voice to be his own again.

"You find them young," Jude said. "Early in their teens, if you can. You can make yourself the parent they didn't have. Take the father's place, and he's bound to you the way a child is bound. More than that. He'll be loyal past all sense, because what he's giving you was never meant for a man at all."

"Mm." Paul let the sound stand for an answer. *The parent they didn't have.* He wondered whether Jude was teaching him a method or practising it on him; whether the elf had looked at a fatherless young man kept in a drawer and seen someone worth cultivating.

"I don't mean to sound entirely calculating about it. It's a useful tactic, that's all. And you won't be lying to them if you actually look after them." If Jude noticed anything, he gave no sign. "Only don't mistake them for orphans."

"What's the difference?"

"All the difference there is. A bastard has lost a father, and he worships the hole where the father should be. An orphan has lost both. He's got nothing above him to fear or to love, nothing he ever looked up to. So he looks at himself instead, and he sees exactly what he's worth to the world, which is nothing, and he knows it better than anyone. An orphan will betray you the moment it profits him, and it needn't profit him much. You can use one. You can steer one, as long as what you want and what he wants run the same way. But you'll never cultivate his loyalty, because there's nothing in him for loyalty to grow in." Jude shrugged. "Less than worthless, as servants go. Give me a bastard any day."

Paul held very still. His mother had lived long enough to teach him, and long enough, he thought, for him to watch her be wrong about a thing or two and love her anyway. Not long enough for much else. He did not know where that left him in the elf's reckoning, and he found he did not want to ask.

"And don't concern yourself with me," Jude said lightly. "I'm long past all this. I've hitched my wagon to Lord Jamis." He paused, and added in the same easy tone, "Lord Jamis won't live forever, mind. Your kind don't, and I'm an elf. When that day comes I'll be looking for a new patron, and I'd not object to one I'd had a hand in making."

"Right."

"But we're talking about other people fastening themselves to your rising star. You understand?"

"Yes," Paul said, and his voice was his own again.

"Good." Jude slapped his knees. "That's enough of servants for one morning. Your training, then. Today, if you like. What would you want to learn first?"

"Spellcasting," Paul said.

"Well, yes. Is there anything in particular?"

"I don't really know anything."

"Nothing at all?"

"Only what I've seen in the Grand Market," Paul said. "The hedge-wizards who work the stalls for coppers. One of them touched the end of a stick and it shone like a lamp, a cold white light with no flame to it. Another took a torn sail from a fisherman, ran his fingers along the rip, and the cloth knit itself together as if it had never been cut. Small things. I don't know how any of it is done."

"What you saw are the lesser magics," Jude said. "Cantrips. Most people who can't work them take them for market tricks, and the men in those stalls don't trouble to correct them. But they're worth a great deal more than that. Once you've truly learned one it's yours. It's always to hand, and it doesn't draw on you the way the greater workings do. We start with those and work you up to the ones with some weight in them. Some of them will do a surprising amount of harm, for all they're the first things anyone learns. But the ones I'd start with are the ones that are useful. That second trick you saw, for one. A great many people think nothing of mending a thing. But you can put things right. Quickly, and without a trace."

"Such as?"

"A rope that's been cut and needs joining, because you're about to have to leave somewhere in a hurry."

"Or a door," Paul said, "that's been forced."

"A door that's been forced. Yes. Cover your tracks." Jude nodded, pleased.

"And levitation," Paul said, and for once there was something like eagerness in the even voice behind the mask. "That's what I'd want. To lift a man off the ground, or myself, by nothing but will. That's what brought me up out of that chasm. I'd give a great deal to learn it."

"It is, and it's no small working. You'll need a great deal more skill than you have now before you can lift so much as yourself." Jude smiled faintly. "Harder still is the fire you saw me throw on the Helm. Wait until you've grown into that. It fills a room. Burns everything in it to death."

"Is that a promise?"

"It's a promise, if you work for it." Jude tapped his knee. "So. What else? The yellow powder. Fairwind. Teaching you."

"Those three," Paul said. "That's all I can think of."

"That's all I can think of too. So let's begin with the alchemist." Jude touched his robe, where the rag bundle sat. "I want to take these gills to him now, before they rot any further than they have already. I've had them wrapped in a wet rag since the corsair, to keep them from drying out."

"That's not very alchemical."

"It's not. He'll probably tell me so." Jude sighed. "And it's making me smell a little of fish."

"A little?" Paul had been breathing through his mouth behind the mask for some time. The reek had come into the room with Jude and settled over it, rank and briny and a day past turning, and it clung to the elf's robe like a second skin. "More than a little, I'd say."

"This alchemist I'm trying to cultivate — I don't think it would be wrong for you to be there, when we talk to him properly." Jude hesitated, and then said the thing that had been sitting at the back of his mind since the Helm. "One more reason I want to go. I don't think his life is in danger, not yet. But someone may want him taken out of the way. I've no proof of that. Nothing at all."

"Why do you think so?"

"His connection with Lord Phranck. I don't think he knows himself that it puts him in danger."

"I don't know Lord Phranck," Paul said. "Is he a real lord? Or only some killer who calls himself one? He's from Keoland, isn't he?"

"He's from Keoland. He came here and met with Lord Jamis. Think of him as a kind of disciplinarian — for people who use magic. He goes about saying, 'Shame on you. Stop raising the dead.'"

"What?"

"He polices wizards, for an office in Keoland. Wizards who deal in what they call unlawful magic. Necromancers are the darker end of it, granted. So he's an inquisitor. He hunts them down."

"Ah."

"Phranck isn't in Monmurg any longer. But while he was here, he met with this alchemist." Jude turned a hand. "So I've been trying to learn a little more. Indirectly. Bringing him the gills, asking for his help, because he's far more of an alchemist than I am. But if you should ever learn, through your people, as you start to have people — or through anyone else you know — that there's someone in the city who's taken an interest in him, or men hanging about his shop, I'd like to know it."

"Yes."

Jude stood, and the smell of the rag bundle came up with him. "Good?"

"Good."

"Then I'm going before these robes have to be burned."

"You've been carrying that in your robe all morning?"

"I didn't have a container. I thought it was wrapped well enough."

"You're a wizard," Paul said. "You don't have a box? A jar?"

"I came straight up here from the foreign district, the same as you came straight up from the docks last night. It hasn't been a full day since we landed." Jude went to the door and stopped with his hand on it. "Come with me, if you like."

Paul shook his head. "I should see Lord Jamis first. About the twenty. And I owe my uncle an account of — of what I mean to do."

"Then I'll go alone. I'll see him, leave these with him, and come back." Jude considered the sun on the gravel outside. "Early afternoon? We'll begin."

"Where?"

"Here." Jude glanced round the sitting room, at the books and the window over the gardens. "These chambers will do as well as any," he said, and opened the door.

The alchemist's shop stood in a small courtyard off one of the foreign district's crooked streets, a squat building of real stone among the driftwood and plaster around it, with a heavy oak door that had been bolted and barred for as long as Jude had known it. He knocked, and waited, and eventually heard the bar lift.

The old alchemist put his head out, already talking. "Can I help you, can I h—" He sniffed, and recoiled.

"Gills," Jude said, holding out the rag. "Sahaugin. Two days dead. And something else." He took the fold of oilcloth from his inner pocket and set it on top of the rag. "A pinch of the powder that killed it. I'd like to know what's in both."

The old man took the bundle and the packet between two fingers as though they were both slightly on fire, and looked from them to Jude and back.

"You have been keeping these," he said, "in your clothes."

"I had no jar."

"Evidently." The old man's eyes went to the oilcloth, and something changed in his face. "Give me until evening," he said, and shut the door.
