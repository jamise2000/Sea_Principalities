# Chapter Six — The Wager

Paul's rooms were dark when he reached them. He did not light the lamp. He sat down on the end of the bed in his coat, with his legs out in front of him because his knees had stiffened on the stair, and let the homunculus run down his arm and onto the coverlet, where it turned twice and lay still.

He had made his choice. He had made it at the table that afternoon, with the spellbook shut under Jude's hand, and he found now that he did not feel very much about it one way or the other. It was done. It sat in him like a stone he had swallowed, not uncomfortable, only heavy, and certain to be there in the morning.

What he felt instead, sitting in the dark, was the size of what he did not know.

Jude had asked him that morning what he had heard at his uncle's table about the Toli, and he had said he probably knew more than most men, and it had been true, and it was not very much. He went over it now, the way he would have gone over a lesson for his mother, to find the edges of it.

Monmurg was a city and a principality in one: the seat of his uncle's house, and first among the five. Fairwind was an island, and its prince was chosen by its captains. So were Flotsom and Jetsom, each its own island, each its own prince. And the Toli were a city, as Monmurg was — Port Toli, on the far shore of Jerlea Bay, ruled by its own prince, Sacnon, of the house of Toli, which had ruled it as long as there had been a Port Toli to rule. Five princes, then. Monmurg, Toli, Fairwind, Flotsom, Jetsom. Each with one voice in council, and each voice the equal of any other.

What set the Toli apart from the other four was their blood. The other princely houses were Oeridian, like his own, or had become so by long marriage. The Toli were Suel. Old Suel, the pale-haired blood of the empire that had burned itself to ash before any of the principalities were born. They kept to their own. They married among their own. They were, by every law and custom of the council, the equal of Monmurg — and every man at his uncle's table had spoken of them as though they were something else, without ever quite saying what.

And Cain Toli, who sailed the Sea Ghost and who had his hand, if Jamis was right, on something that drowned ships, was of that house. Paul did not know what he was to Sacnon. A son, a nephew, a cousin, a servant with the family name. He did not know, and it came to him sitting there that he ought to.

Behind the five princes stood two more powers who had no vote in council and did not need one. The Plar of Westkeep, in the north on the Hool River, who held the principalities' standing army. And the Plar of Hokar — whom most men in Monmurg still called by his other title, the Duke of Berghof — in his mountain domain to the southwest, who had sold them the yellow powder for a hundred and fifty years and then, a long while ago now, without explanation, stopped.

That was the shape of it. He had known it all his life, the way a man knows the walls of the house he was raised in without ever having counted the stones. He sat now with his hands on his stiff knees and counted, and found the walls a good deal thinner than he had believed.

Someone knocked at the door.

###

It was Jude, of course. He had not gone down to the foreign district. He stood in the corridor with a candle in one hand and an expression Paul had begun to recognize, which meant that he had had an idea and would be unbearable until he had been allowed to act on it.

"Let's not call it a night," Jude said.

"I thought we had."

"You're spent on the art. You've nothing left in you today. Fair enough. I understand that." Jude stepped back to let him out into the gallery, and Paul found himself following, as he had apparently been meant to. "So why don't you teach me what you do? I'll follow you. You can criticize everything I do wrong. Throw brass bowls at my shins, if you like. And we'll get into Ferd's without anyone in Ferd's knowing we're there."

"Ferd's."

"The tavern. Yes."

"You want to get into your own tavern without being seen."

"This is instruction," Jude said. "I'd genuinely like to know who's drinking in Ferd's. I ran some very disreputable men out of there before we went to the Helm, and I'd like to know whether they've crept back in."

"So you want to go to Ferd's without being seen at Ferd's, and find out who's there."

"Yes."

"Then here's the first rule of moving unseen," Paul said. "The more of you there are, the harder it is."

"So we're two. It doesn't get much less crowded than that. Short of one."

"Short of one."

"And I can float."

"You might." Paul considered him in the candlelight. "Why can't I just send the homunculus?"

Jude paused. "Can you? How far can you send it? Do we have to be in the alley outside?"

"A good long way."

"A hundred yards? A mile?"

"A good long way," Paul said again.

"So you could stand here, in the palace, and send that thing down into the foreign district and tell me who's in Ferd's?"

"I think so. Perhaps not that far." Paul turned it over. "Less than the whole distance. But from the bottom of the hill, yes."

"And if something steps on it, you've lost it."

"I make another."

They had come to the end of the gallery, where it turned toward the stair down to the great hall. There was a window there, and beyond it, far down the hill, the lamps of the foreign district were coming on one by one in the dusk.

"Why do you want to go to Ferd's in any case?" Paul said.

"That's where I'm staying."

"Why are you staying there?"

"Ferd's a good man."

"From what you've told me of it," Paul said, "it's a sty."

"It is a sty. I know it's a sty." Jude shrugged. "Should I be staying here instead?"

"If my uncle is paying you and you won't stay here—"

"Why would I stay under Jeon's thumb?"

"You tell me."

"A poor wandering elf, living under a prince's roof," Jude said. "No."

"You'd rather sleep in a tavern loft."

"You stayed under his thumb for twenty years."

"That isn't the same."

"You'd have me believe you'd prefer the palace, where everything is—"

"I've slept in the saddle," Jude said. "I've slept in worse than Ferd's."

"You look," Paul said, "as though you haven't slept for days. For years, possibly."

"Not years."

"Months, then. Is that closer?"

Jude smiled slightly. "I like that you're trying to get things out of me."

"I'm only asking how long an elf can go without sleep."

"That's important for you to know, is it?"

"So you're an elf."

"I have pointed ears." Jude lifted a hand to one of them, where it showed faintly through his hair. "Yes."

"I thought you hid them."

"I do. But if you spend enough time with me, the points will show. I assume you're observant."

"I'd noticed," Paul said. "I don't want to go to Ferd's. It seems like a waste of time. If you want to learn to move unseen, let's move unseen on something that matters."

"Such as?"

"You tell me."

"Your uncle," Jude said. "He's right here. He's easy. He's close."

"Why would we creep up on my uncle?"

"Because he's here. It's close, and it's easy, and you won't go to Ferd's."

"What are you after?"

"Nothing. I'm saying let's keep on. You've been complaining."

"Lord Jamis, then."

"Lord Jamis, then," Jude agreed readily. "Or the alchemist. We could creep up on the alchemist. You could get me in, and tell me what I'm doing wrong."

Paul stopped walking.

"No," he said.

"Why not?"

"Because you're trying to find out what I am." Paul's voice was quite level. "Whether I'm something rotten. Whether that mask means what you think it means. I'm not. And I can't prove it to you standing here in a gallery."

"What are you talking about? Rotten?" Jude looked at him with what seemed to be honest surprise. "I didn't say kill anyone. I said go in quietly and watch."

"Watch whom?"

"The alchemist. The old man."

"Then why the alchemist?"

"You mean the man who sold you your mask," Jude said, after a moment. "No. Not him. The alchemist, off the square. I went to him this morning. Weren't you—" He stopped. "No. You weren't with me. I'm tired."

"We could find the man who sold me the mask," Paul said. "Before he shuts his stall."

"And follow him," Jude said, "and you could tell me what I'm doing wrong."

"No. He's in the market on the far side of the city. It's a hard walk through bad streets at this hour."

"Then your uncle."

"No. What is it with you and creeping up on people?"

"I'm offering you the chance," Jude said, "to turn it round. Teacher and pupil. You've been angry with the way I've been teaching you."

"I'm not angry."

"You're certain? You've been making rude gestures at me for some time."

"I've been pulling your chain," Paul said. "It's not serious." He hesitated, and then said the rest of it, because it was true and he thought Jude had earned it. "If I didn't want to be around you, I wouldn't have agreed to learn from you."

Jude was silent for a moment.

"Then call this a test of endurance," he said. "You said yourself I look as though I've not slept in days. How long have I been up, do you think?"

"Jude."

"Let's keep going."

"No."

"Why not?"

"Why do you want to keep going? What is it with you pushing this? You're pushing it very hard now."

"I'm saying we change the lesson."

"To what?"

"Listen." Jude set the candle on the windowsill. "Out in the world — when you've not been kept on palace grounds your whole life — things happen. And when bad things happen, they never happen neatly. It's never once, and then you're done after a bad half-day, or a bad day. So what I'm saying is, you've finished with the art for today. I understand that. But we don't have to be finished. You choose a mark. We creep up on it together. You can throw a brass bowl at my knees if I get it wrong."

Paul looked at him in the candlelight for a long moment.

"All right," he said. "Then let's make it simple. How close can you get to a guard? Whoever gets closest without being seen, wins."

"You're sure?"

"I'm sure."

"Then let's put money on it."

"How much?"

"I don't know how much you've got."

"More than you," Paul said.

"Very likely. Fifty gold? A hundred?"

"A hundred."

"A hundred gold." Jude picked up the candle again. "I'll go first."

"All right."

###

There was a guard at the head of the great stair, where the gallery opened onto the landing above the hall. He was a middle-aged man in the prince's household livery, with a halberd grounded beside him and his eyes on the stair below, and he stood in the full light of the two lamps that flanked the landing, which made him a very poor mark for creeping up on and an excellent one for this.

Jude stopped out of his sight line, behind the angle of the gallery wall, and considered him.

"We still have a great deal to do," he said, low. "And you said I look as though I've not slept in days. Fine. That's why I say it. We have things to do still. You're spent. You did well, learning your first working. You had the first one very fast. You couldn't hold the second. That's nothing. If you learned one working a day you'd be better than any initiate in any academy in the known world. So you're doing well."

"You said that."

"I'm saying it again. But when things go wrong in the world I live in, they don't let you tuck yourself up in bed with a nightcap. You're trying to stay alive. Sometimes for days on end. We've work that Lord Jamis has set us, and work your — that Prince Jeon has set you — that has nothing to do with learning magic. And I have work of my own from Jamis. Being finished with the lesson doesn't mean being finished for the day." He looked at the guard. "Now. I'd like a hundred gold. I think I can beat you. How would you like me to prove I can get closer to that guard than you?"

"How?"

"I'll kiss him."

Paul turned the mask on him. "You'll use your magic."

"Of course I will."

"You'll go invisible."

"Are you really so slow," Jude said, "that you thought I'd tiptoe up to a guard and kiss him?"

"I thought the point of this was to see who could get closest to a guard without being seen."

"Invisibility is very unseen, wouldn't you say?"

"I think it's weak."

"Weak?" Jude's voice did not rise, but it went very sharp. "What do you think I'm training you to be? It isn't weak. It's the arcane arts joined to your gift for putting a thin blade into the back of a man's neck. That's the whole of it."

"But if you can't get behind a man without it, you shouldn't be—"

"I'm not a thief," Jude said. "I'm not an assassin. So if I can get closer than you can—"

He stopped there, and waited.

Paul stood in the shadow of the gallery wall and looked past him at the guard in the lamplight, and made himself think it through.

On his own he could have reached that man. Not quickly — the light was bad for it, and the landing was bare stone that carried sound — but he could have done it, with patience, along the wall and behind the pillar and then the last three steps at the moment the guard turned to look down into the hall. He was better at it than Jude. They both knew it. That was not the point Jude was making.

The point was that Jude, who could not have crossed that landing unseen to save his life, could walk up to the man in full light and put his lips to his cheek, because Jude could make himself not be there. And Paul could do the same, one day, if he learned it. And then it would not matter how bare the landing was, or how bright the lamps. He would walk up to any man in the world, in any light, as close as breath, and then do what he already knew how to do.

"You want me to use what I already have," he said slowly, "and what you're going to give me. Together. And see what I do with both."

"Yes."

"You're telling me I'm better at this than you are."

"At a good deal of it. Yes." Jude's voice had gone quiet again. "But put the two together — the art, and what you already are — and there's very little that could stand in front of you. I can walk up to that man invisible and kiss him and he'll never know. You could walk up to him invisible and kiss him, and cut his throat, and be gone, and he'd never know that either."

"And once you'd kissed him," Paul said, "you'd have said, 'Surprise.'"

"I'd have been tempted."

Paul looked at the guard for a moment longer. The man shifted his weight, glanced down the stair, and settled again, entirely unaware that two men were standing twenty feet from him discussing how best to kill him.

"All right," Paul said. "I understand what you're telling me."

"Good." Jude did not move. "Do we still have to do this?"

"No."

"Are we done with the test, then?"

"We're done with the test."

"Then keep your hundred gold." Jude picked the candle up off the ledge. "We've things to learn tonight anyway. It's only just eight. I took some samples to the alchemist this morning. The gills. And some of the powder." He added, with some satisfaction, "Which, I'm told, I'm now allowed to have without hanging for it. I've given them to him. He asked me to come back this evening. Let's go and see what he's found."

"Now?"

"Now. You're all right with that?"

"Yes."

"Good. Then we needn't go to bed yet. You can still be in bed by midnight."

"I don't keep a bedtime," Paul said.

"You're a very trying pupil."

"Let's go." Paul started toward the service stair that went down beside the kitchens, the way that would take them out of the palace without passing the guard on the landing at all. After a few steps he said, over his shoulder, "At least I have a home to go back to."

"Mine's a long way off," Jude said, following. "I left it a very long time ago. Come on."

The guard at the head of the great stair heard nothing, then or afterward, and went off duty at midnight none the wiser.
