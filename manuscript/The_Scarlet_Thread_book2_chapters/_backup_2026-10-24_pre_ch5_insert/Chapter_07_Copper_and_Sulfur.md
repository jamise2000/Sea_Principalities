# Chapter Seven — Copper and Sulfur

They left the palace a little after eight by the kitchen postern and went down the hill on foot.

It was a half-hour walk from the palace to the foreign district, and it took them through two gates. The first, at the foot of the palace grounds, was already swinging shut as they came to it; the guards held it a moment for the prince's nephew and then let it close behind them with a long iron groan, and the bar dropped into its brackets. The second stood between the common city and the foreign district, and it was never closed at all, because nobody in Monmurg had ever thought the foreign district worth keeping out, only keeping in. The sun was going down behind the harbor forts as they passed under it. The light lay long and red across the roofs, and then went grey, and the lamps began.

Both of them knew the way. Paul had come this way before on his own, in plainer clothes, before he had ever heard of the Helm. It was on this same road, a few turns further on, that two men had followed him out of the market and tried to take his purse in an alley, and he found that he could still name the doorway where the second man had stood. He did not look at it as they passed.

The foreign district met them the way it always did, all at once, with its smell. Tar and fish and the sea. Unwashed wool and cheap tallow. Old beer soaked into old boards. And over all of it tonight, strong enough to make the eyes water, the smell of onions.

"It smells like home," Paul said, and was a little surprised to find he meant it.

Jude was looking at the street with the ease of long familiarity: the knots of hard men on the corners, sizing up everyone who passed; the painted women in the doorways, calling out prices that had fallen sharply since the blockade; the boys who ran errands and carried knives. And everywhere, on every corner, in every mouth of every alley, the onion sellers — men and women with sacks at their feet and strings of them hung from poles, shouting their wares over the heads of the crowd.

"Onions," Jude said. "Not even the dignity of garbage. Onions."

"Good for the blood," Paul said. "Clears the plague."

It was, in fact, nearly all there was. Grain had gone dear and then gone altogether, and meat was a thing men spoke of; but onions grew in any poor soil, stored through any season, and could be boiled with water and salt into something that would pass for soup. Every cookshop in the district sold onion soup now. Nothing else. The onion sellers, by the look of them, were doing a better trade than the women in the doorways.

Jude stopped at a stall, looked the stock over with a critical eye, paid a copper for a small red onion, and bit into it raw.

"That's disgusting," Paul said.

"It keeps off the vampires."

"That's garlic."

"Not in Monmurg," Jude said, chewing. "Nobody here can afford garlic. Garlic is an exotic spice. Come on."

He led the way on into the warren. The streets of the foreign district were not streets so much as gaps left between buildings that had been thrown up by men with no plan and no money, of driftwood and wreck-timber and old sailcloth, leaning together overhead until in places they nearly met, so that a man walked through a tunnel of other men's walls with a strip of darkening sky above him no wider than his hand. Jude went through it without hesitation, left, right, through an arch, along a covered way that stank of cats, and out at last into a small courtyard where the buildings stepped back.

On its far side stood the alchemist's house. It was built of real stone, squat and square, the only thing in the courtyard that looked as though it would still be standing in a hundred years, and it had a door of heavy black oak, iron-strapped, with a small grille set in it at the height of a man's face.

Jude knocked.

There was a long wait. Then the grille slid back, and a pair of eyes looked out through it, enormously magnified behind thick lenses.

"I remember you," said a voice, dry and old. "Who is your friend?"

"My friend," Jude said. "I'm Jude. He's—" He glanced at Paul. "Introduce yourself."

"Paul."

"Jude," said the voice. "Paul." The magnified eyes moved to the silver mask and stayed there. "Paul Revero."

Neither of them said anything.

"I have said that aloud," the old man said. "Forgive me. Paul Revero. What does Prince Jeon want with me?"

"No," Jude said. "No. Don't think of it like that. Paul Revero is my student."

"Ah." The eyes considered them both. "Hence." A pause. "So. What is it you wish to know, Jude?"

"The samples I gave you this morning. I'd like to know what you've learned."

"I have information for you." The eyes went back to Paul. "And what does Paul Revero want? An interest in the art?"

"Yes," Paul said.

"An interest in the art is well enough." The grille closed. They heard a heavy bolt draw back, and then a second, and a bar lift out of its brackets, and the door swung inward on the dark. "Come in. Be respectful. Touch nothing."

The room beyond was crowded from floor to ceiling. Shelves ran up every wall, and every shelf was full: jars and flasks and stoppered bottles, bundles of dried things hung from pegs, boxes of stones, coils of copper wire, books stacked sideways and on end and open face-down, glass vessels of shapes Paul had no names for. A long workbench ran down the middle of the room under a hanging lamp, cluttered with apparatus, and at the far end of it something bubbled very slowly in a flask over a small blue flame.

The alchemist shut the door behind them and shot the bolts again before he turned.

His name was Albashon. He was human, and very old, and bent nearly double at the shoulders under a robe that had once been some dark color and was now a map of every spill he had made in it. His face had sunk in on itself around the bones, and his teeth, when he showed them, were few. He wore spectacles of a kind Paul had seen at court, but thicker, heavier, with a second pair of small lenses hinged above the first that he could swing down over them, so that his eyes swam behind the glass like fish in a bowl. He smelled of sulfur, and of other things under the sulfur that Paul chose not to identify, and of something that might have been bat guano and might have been the old man himself.

"Paul," Albashon said. "Forgive me. I should not call you by your given name. But I do not know what to call you, sir. Revero is not your family's name. I know a little of your family's history. Which do you go by?"

"I go by Revero."

"Is that a given name? You do not use the name Azani?"

The name meant nothing to him. Paul turned it over and found no purchase on it anywhere: not in his mother's curriculum, not in the histories, not in the letters. It had the old Suel cadence, like a name off a tomb. But the old man was not asking idly. The magnified eyes rested on the silver mask with the patience of someone who already knows the answer to his question and is only curious to see what the other man will say.

"No," he said. "I've never heard it."

"Azani," Albashon said again, softly, as if to himself, and seemed satisfied by something in Paul's silence. "No. Of course not. And how is your grandfather?"

"Yes," Jude said, with interest. "How is your grandfather?"

Paul turned his head and looked at the old man through the eye-slits of the silver mask, and thought of Lord Jamis sitting in his office among his charts with a glass of wine he did not drink, and of what Lord Jamis did not know. It came to him, with some discomfort, that this old man in his stained robe knew more about Paul's blood than Paul did.

"It's been a while," he said.

"Has it?" Albashon's magnified eyes did not leave him. "It has been a while since he visited me, also." Then he turned away, quite suddenly, as if the matter had never been raised. "Yes. Well. I am only observing. You wish to know." He looked at Jude. "Do you want him to know what I know? What we spoke of?"

"Yes. He's part of this."

"Copper," Albashon said.

Jude went still.

"Copper is the element. And sulfur." The old man shuffled toward the workbench. "Combined in a way that few know. Come."

"We gave him a sample," Jude said to Paul, low, following. "From the Helm. The powder. That's what's under all of it — what protects the island. And the gills. I asked him to take them apart."

"It is combined," Albashon said, over his shoulder, "in a way that is very strange. New to me. It is a process."

"New to you?" Jude said. "You've never seen that process?"

"I do not know it. I know the elements. I have been able to break them down. I know they are there. There are some other things in it besides — traces — that I cannot name. But at its heart it is copper and sulfur, combined in a way I have not seen before." He reached the bench and turned. "The old books would call it vitriolum. A blue vitriol, though it is not blue, and it is not any vitriol I have ever made."

"Is it a poison?" Jude said.

"It is poison."

"The copper? The sulfur?"

"Together." The old man held up one crooked finger. "Not for a man."

He took a glass dish from among the clutter and set it under the lamp. In it was a small heap of fine pale powder, yellowish-white, dry as flour.

"This is what you saw," he said to Paul. "On the island. This is the sample he gave me. Look."

He filled a beaker from a jug of water, took up the barest trace of the powder on the tip of a glass rod, and stirred it in until it vanished. Then he dipped his finger in the beaker and touched it to his tongue.

"Nothing," he said. "Copper, a little. Nothing to me. Sulfur. Nothing to me. Men. Elves. No harm, in the water, like this." He wiped his finger on his robe. "To the gills of a fish, it is knives. It cuts and it shreds."

He pointed with the rod to the end of the bench, where a glass bowl of water stood with a single small golden fish in it, turning slow circles.

"Shall I show you?"

"Please," Jude said.

"This is what you gave me?" Paul said. "He didn't make this?"

"No," Jude said. "It's from the Helm."

Albashon took a pinch of the dry powder between finger and thumb — a very small pinch, barely enough to see — carried it to the bowl, and let it fall on the surface of the water.

"That," he said, "is a thousand times too much. A thousandth part of that would kill this fish."

For a moment the fish went on circling. Then it jerked, and turned, and came up toward the surface in a series of short frantic darts, its gills flaring wide, bright red at the edges. It rolled. It hung under the surface on its side, mouth working. Then it was still.

"That," Jude said quietly, "is what we saw in the grotto."

"No man would be troubled by this," Albashon said, "in the water. It is nothing to us, in this measure and this form. It passes through the gills into the blood, and the blood is where it kills. We do not breathe water." He tapped the beaker. "Sulfur with iron, now — the green vitriol — that is a death to men. This is for their blood. Not ours."

"In the water," Paul said.

Albashon looked at him.

"In the water it's nothing to us," Paul said. "Thinned out across miles of sea. But not dry. Not like that, in the dish." His voice was quite level. "Breathe in even a little of it, dry, and it'll kill a man as surely as it killed that fish."

He did not say how he knew. He heard it as he spoke, as clearly as if she were standing in the room: his mother's voice, sharp, over three barrels marked with red crosses in a hidden room behind a wall, when he had been small enough to reach for one. "Breathing in even a minute amount of that will kill you, Paul." He had not thought of it in years. He had thought of it every day since the grotto.

The old man inclined his head, slowly, and there was something new in the way he looked at Paul.

"Just so," he said. "Just so. In the sea, spread thin, it is nothing to us. That is the whole cleverness of the thing. Dry, and breathed, it is death to any lung. And it burns the skin, if you are careless with it. Keep it in the barrel. Keep the barrel shut." He covered the dish with a glass lid. "Whoever told you that told you truly."

"But you said there were elements you couldn't tell," Jude said.

"Ah. Yes. That is right." Albashon's finger came up again. "It is how they are joined. That is what I cannot tell. I do not know how to make this. The way it is built — the structure of the thing — is copper and sulfur. But how they are brought together, I cannot say."

"And both are cheap," Paul said.

"Cheap. Copper has some value. But sulfur you can find. You can buy it in the market. It is not nothing, but it is not dear." The old man looked at the covered dish. "If a man knew how, it could be made in quantity. By the barrel. By the ton."

"If I told you," Jude said, "that this came originally out of the Duke of Berghof's territories — would that mean anything to you?"

"Oh." Albashon's head came up. "That would explain a great deal to me."

"Because it did. The sample I brought you was made forty years ago. That's the last time Prince Jeon's people were able to get any. It was made — or discovered — by the Duke of Berghof. In the mountains, I'm told, along the southwest of his lands."

"His domain," Albashon said absently. "Not his lands. His domain." He was quiet a moment. "This was made by the Duke. And perhaps by the Fieraxians themselves, long ago. Some would say so."

"I don't know the Fieraxians," Jude said.

"Few do, now."

Paul was looking at the covered dish.

"I've seen this before," he said. "A long time ago."

Jude glanced at him. "On the Helm."

"Before the Helm." Paul did not elaborate. He thought of the three barrels in his mother's hidden room, and of the papers he had found near them, in a hand he now knew was his father's: columns of figures, equations, notes crowded into margins and crossed out and written over. Grayson had been trying to make this. He had not known, when he found those papers, what they were for. "I have some papers," he said. "Equations. They may bear on how it's made."

"Why is it so important?" Albashon said. "To make it?"

"The sahaugin," Paul said.

"To keep them back," Jude said. "It's a finite store. Whatever's left is all there is. Those stores have been lasting since the last of it came, because nothing more has come."

"You know it's explosive," Paul said to the old man.

"It is not." Albashon lifted a finger. "Not ordinarily. It explodes only under certain conditions. Under pressure — shut up tight and pressed. And if it gets wet — in the sea — and sits a certain while. It is not inert, in the water. The joining with seawater gives off heat. When it has been wet a long time, and aged, and packed close, the heat can build until it catches. And then, yes, sometimes it will burst." He peered at Paul. "Is that what happened to you?"

"No," Paul said. "What I'm asking is whether any of my equations would be of use to you."

"Possibly."

"I can show you."

"Show me."

Paul hesitated. "I don't have them with me."

"Then how will you show me?"

"I can write them out."

Albashon found him a scrap of paper and a stub of chalk-pencil and cleared a space on the bench, and Paul stood over it and wrote, slowly, from memory, what he remembered of the columns in his father's hand. He had a very good memory. It was not good enough. He knew it by the fourth line, and by the sixth he knew that the old man knew it too.

Albashon took the paper, held it up close to his lenses, turned it over, turned it back.

"This is nonsense," he said.

"Yes."

"I have the equations," Paul said. "I don't have them with me."

"Then bring them to me. Bring them to me." Albashon set the paper down. "This is an interesting problem. I should like to solve it. I assume you want more of this made. But my question to you is — why do you wish to kill fish?"

"Not fish," Jude said. "Sahaugin."

"Ah." The old man stood very still. "Yes. Ah. That is—"

"That's why the sahaugin haven't troubled the principalities," Jude said. "For a hundred and fifty years."

"This is the magical shell," Albashon said slowly. "The great warding. The ancient enchantment." He looked at the covered dish as if it had changed shape. "I have always wondered about this. I have always wondered. This answers so many questions for me."

"We're permitted to know it," Jude said.

"And I am not?"

"No. But you are, through us. Do you understand?"

"I understand," Albashon said, "that if Paul Revero is here, I am protected. Is that so?"

"Yes," Paul said. "But if you were to let this become known, to anyone who matters, that protection ends."

"This is the life of a transmuter. I keep many secrets for many people."

"This one would mean your death," Paul said. "Immediately. I should warn you."

"Death is always immediate." The old man showed his few teeth. "I am sure there are many worse things. That is the best kind of death. The quickest."

"I say immediately," Paul said, "because I don't know how they'd do it."

"No. Nor do I. That is the part that interests me least." Albashon turned to Jude. "Was there something else?"

"Do you happen to have any of Lord Phranck's secrets?" Jude said lightly.

"Lord Phranck has no secrets. I do not know him well." Albashon considered. "Lord Phranck's mind is an open book. No. I take that back. Lord Phranck's mind is a book that has been left out in the rain. There are pages missing."

"Sir," Paul said. "I don't know how to make this yet. But you have knowledge—"

"Possibly."

"That you could teach me."

"Yes." Albashon picked up the scrap of paper again and shook it at him. "Because what you have written on this page might as well have been written by a dog. I will find you chalk, and a board, and you will work it on the board in front of me. But I wish your mind were more orderly than this."

Paul took that without answering. It was, he thought, fair.

"I'll bring you the papers," he said. "Tonight."

He went to the door and stood for a moment looking at it, and Jude came across to him while the old man turned back to his bubbling flask.

"If I stay here with him," Jude said, very low, "can you get back into the palace on your own? The gate's shut."

"I could send the homunculus up to look at them," Paul said. "The papers."

"Can you do that? Read through it?"

Paul turned it over, and let it go. The creature could see for him and hear for him, and it could carry a little, but it could not lift a chest lid or turn the leaves of a book, and it could not bring back forty pages of figures. "No," he said. "Not this."

"But you know a way in. Without the gate."

"I do."

"Then go that way. I'll stay here with the old man and make sure we don't lose him." Jude's glance went back across the room to the bent figure at the bench. "Within the hour?"

"Within the hour."

"All right." Jude hesitated, and then said the rest of it, so quietly that Paul had to lean in to hear. "If you come back, and you see flashes of light coming out of this place — fire, anything like that — it's because I've been worried about exactly this. I have been. Truly. It means come fast, and kill anyone who isn't him."

"It'll be done," Paul said.

"What are you two talking about over there?" Albashon said, without turning around.

"Only telling him to be quick," Jude said. "To fetch his papers."

"Good. Tell him to be quick."

"How old is he?" Paul murmured.

"I have no idea," Jude said. "Older than he looks. And he looks older than the harbor."

"Bring the papers themselves," he added, as Paul drew the bolts. "Not what you remember. He'll want to see them in your father's—" He stopped. "In the hand they were written in."

Paul looked at him for a moment through the eye-slits of the mask. Then he pulled the door open on the dark courtyard, and went out into it, and heard the bolts go home behind him one after another.

The foreign district at night was a different place from the foreign district at dusk. The onion sellers had gone. The women in the doorways had not. The men on the corners had grown in number and dropped their voices, and the lamps had been lit only where someone was paying for them, which was not many places.

Paul went through it quickly and without hurrying, the way he had learned to walk here years before, with his hand near the hilt of the short sword under his coat and his eyes on the edges of things. The homunculus rode in his inner pocket, a small cool weight against his ribs. He did not take the road they had come down by. He took another, narrower and steeper, that went up through the back of the common district to a place in the palace wall that no guard had ever been posted on because no one but a boy who had spent twenty years looking for ways out had ever found it.

Behind him, in a stone house in a small courtyard, an old man who knew more of Paul's lineage than Paul himself bent over a flask of something blue, and an elf who did not sleep sat with his back to the wall and his wand across his knees, watching the door.
