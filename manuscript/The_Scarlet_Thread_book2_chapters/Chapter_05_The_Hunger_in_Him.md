# Chapter Five — The Hunger in Him

The sun was well up by the time Jude let himself out by the palace postern, and the city had got on with its morning without him. The air coming up the hill was warm already and carried the foreign district with it: smoke from a hundred cookfires, the sweetish drift of temple incense, and under both the sewage that ran in open channels down its lanes. Little of it reached him. The bundle in his robe had gone past fish into something riper, and within an arm's length of Jude it overpowered everything else. He had stopped noticing. The guards at the postern had not, and he had seen the younger of the two turn his face aside as he passed.

He went out through the North Gate and down the hill, and he took his time about it. There was no hurry that an hour would mend, the gills would be no deader at noon than they were now, and he wanted the time to think. So he walked slowly, his hands folded in his sleeves, and let the street go past him.

*The young man will do.*

He let the thought stand a moment, the way he would let a reagent sit in the dish before he trusted what it showed him. Then he began to take it apart.

At the foot of the hill the streets narrowed and the city's hunger came up to meet him. It was not the hunger of a famine, not yet. Nobody lay in the gutters. But the bakers' shutters were half down at an hour when they should have been open wide, and outside the one that was open a line of women stood with their baskets over their arms, not talking, watching the door. A man at a corner had three onions laid out on a cloth, and a price chalked on a slate beside them that had been rubbed out and chalked again higher, and rubbed out again. Two boys sat on a step with their knees drawn up and watched Jude go by with the steady, unembarrassed attention of children who have learned that anything passing might be an opportunity. The blockade had been squeezing Monmurg for months. It had not yet squeezed hard enough to break anything, only to make everyone a little quieter, and a little more careful with their belongings and their tongues.

Hungry people, every one of them. And not one of them, Jude thought, hungry in the way that mattered to him.

He had told Paul a good deal that morning, and most of it had been true. He had not told him the part that interested him most, because it was not the kind of thing one said to a pupil, and because he had not finished deciding it.

There were two ways to look at Prince Jeon's nephew, and Jude had been looking at him both ways since the Helm.

The first was simple arithmetic. Lord Jamis was not young. He was hale, and shrewd, and likely to remain both for some years yet, and Jude had no complaint about the bargain he had made with him. But every patron Jude would ever have was going to die before him; that was what it meant to be an elf among men. A wise elf did not wait for the funeral to look about him. And here, set down in front of him by Jamis's own hand, was the nephew of the richest prince in the Hold, raised in the palace, trusted by his uncle as Jeon trusted almost no one, and charged by that uncle, in so many words, to become a wizard. A young man with no friends at court, no web of his own, no one to tell him how any of it was done. A young man who had sat across from Jude for an hour that morning and asked him, in effect, to teach him how to be powerful.

Jude had been glad to oblige. Every lesson he gave was a thread, and threads ran both ways.

That was the first way of looking at it, and it was a comfortable one, and Jude did not trust comfortable conclusions. The second was more interesting.

Farther on, the lane bent once and opened into the Row of Gods. Nobody in Monmurg called it anything else. It was a long, uneven street of warehouses and old merchants' halls that had been let, one by one, to missionaries from every land that traded through the strait, men and women who had crossed the sea to tell the city about their gods and had found that the city would let them do it for rent. Each had made what it could of its hall. One front was hung with yellow cloth gone grey at the hems, and a priest in a sun-disc stood on its step and sang in a language Jude half knew to a congregation of three. The next had a painted sign of a many-armed figure over its door, and a smell of sandalwood rolling out of it thick enough to lean on. Farther down a bell rang without stopping, thin and flat, and a bearded man with a staff harangued the street in the Common tongue about the sea and its wrath and the wages of sin, while the street went past him without listening. The incense of a dozen rites hung over all of it, and the smoke of their offerings, and every temple's door stood open to show its god in the gloom: gilt, or carved, or painted, or merely a stone. The blockade had brought them custom. Two of the halls were handing out bread at their doors, a heel to each comer and a blessing with it, and the lines outside them were longer than any congregation Jude had ever seen there. Bread first, and the god after. He supposed there would be a good many converts in Monmurg this year, and that most of them would lapse the week the ships came back.

Across the street the Row looked at its gods and kept its own counsel. The houses on that side were narrow and tall, their upper floors leaning out over the lane, and their doors were painted in colors no respectable house would wear: red, and peacock blue, and a green like poison. It was late morning, which was early for them. Shutters stood half open on rooms where the beds had not been made, and on one balcony a woman in a loose wrap sat with her bare feet on the rail, eating something out of a bowl and watching the bearded man with the staff with the tolerant interest of a neighbor. Below her an older woman swept the step of a house with no name, only a lantern of red glass hung beside the door, unlit now. Business was thinner than it had been, Jude guessed. Hunger did that. But it had not stopped, and it would not. Bread, drink and a warm body in the dark: to most men they were all the same hunger, and a man short of one would spend what he had on another.

Gods on one side and whores on the other, and the street between them belonging to whoever had the coin. Jude had always liked the Row. It was the most honest street in Monmurg.

Between two of the temples, on a mounting block, a hedge-wizard had set up to work the crowd. He was doing the trick with the stick that Paul had seen in the Grand Market, touching the end of it and making it shine with a cold white light, and holding it up, and lowering it again, and holding out his other hand for coppers. He was quite good at it. He had probably been quite good at it for twenty years.

Jude watched him for the space of a few steps, and walked on.

*There it is.* A man who could make light out of nothing and was content to sell it on a corner for the price of a loaf had already told you everything you needed to know about him. The art was not lacking in him. The hunger was. And without the hunger the art went nowhere. It sat in a man like a coin in a purse and was spent a copper at a time.

Jude had known a good many wizards, in Idee and before it, and he had come to believe that the ones who mattered all had the same thing at the root of them, whatever school they followed. Not cleverness. Cleverness was common. Not talent, though talent helped. Hunger. Not the kind these women in the bread line felt, which could be satisfied with bread, but the other kind, which could not be satisfied with anything. Hunger to know what lay on the next page, and the page after that. Hunger to make the world do what it would not do for anyone else. Hunger to be more than one had been allowed to be. A wizard had to want, and go on wanting, past the point where any sensible man would have sat down content. The study was too long and too hard otherwise, and the price of it too high. The ones without that hunger stopped. They learned to make light, and they sold it on a corner.

Jude knew his own hunger well. It had walked him out of more than one safe life and into rooms he should not have entered, and it would very likely kill him someday. He did not regret it. He had only learned to recognize it in others.

And he had seen it in Paul.

Not everywhere, and not all the time. The young man hid it, the way he hid everything else. But it had been there that morning, when Jude spoke of the fire that filled a room, and the even voice behind the mask had come back at once: *Is that a promise?* It had been there when he asked for levitation, with something in his voice for once that was very nearly eagerness. It had been there in his reasoning about the barrels, the leap from a failed salting to a counterfeit cargo, made faster than Jude had made it himself. And it had been there, crudely, in the threat against the thief's family. That had been badly done, a boy reaching for a lever he did not yet know how to use. But the reaching was the thing. A man without hunger did not reach.

Jude left the Row behind and went on down through the district toward the square, and went over it again.

Intelligence: certainly. He had watched the young man think, and it was a good mind, quick and orderly, a mind that had been trained early by someone who knew what she was about. Ability: very likely. Whether Paul had truly made that homunculus himself, from one book and in a day, Jude still could not say. Jamis could not say either. But Jamis had told him the boy came of a line that had never been short of mages, and Jude had seen the creature run along the young man's sleeve like a live thing. If he had made it, he had a gift of the uncommon sort, and he had found it on his own, with no one to show him.

And he had been shut out. That, Jude thought, was the part that would make him. Kept behind palace walls the whole of his life. Burned as a child in his father's workroom, and masked ever since, so that no one at court had looked him in the face in twenty years. Spoken of, when he was spoken of at all, as the prince's nephew, of uncertain use. More at ease in the alleys of the foreign district than in the halls of his own family's house. A young man like that had spent his life on the wrong side of a door, listening to what was said on the other side. That bred its own kind of hunger, and Jude knew it well. It was the hunger of the outsider for the room he has been kept out of, and it did not go away with age. It only learned to wait.

Put that together with the rest: the blood, the palace, the uncle's favor, the uncle's charge. A wizard needed hunger, but he also needed time and books and protection, and a door that would open when he knocked. Paul had all of those, or soon would. Jude could not have designed a better candidate if he had set out to.

*Then why do I doubt him?*

He knew why. He had known since the Helm, and he had seen it again that morning.

There was something in the young man that had not been starved out of him. Something stubborn and, Jude suspected, inconvenient. On the Helm, shown Fairwind's treachery, Paul had not weighed it, or asked what it might be worth to him. He had simply called it evil and set out to expose it, as if the matter were obvious. This morning, in the middle of a lesson on how to bind men to him, he had stopped and asked whether there were no truly loyal servants, men who would give their lives without being asked. He had asked it straight, as a real question, the way a man asks about something he hopes is true. And through all of it his loyalty to his uncle had not wavered once. Jude had told him to build a web of his own, and Paul had plainly heard it as one more way to serve Prince Jeon. When Jude said he could think of no situation in which Paul would betray his uncle, the young man had not so much as blinked. And the first thing he meant to do with the morning's lesson was go to Jeon and give him an account of what he intended.

None of that was weakness, exactly. But all of it was a leash. A wizard with a conscience could go a long way. He could not go all the way. Somewhere along the road there would be a door he would not open, a page he would not turn, a price he would not pay, and he would stop there, and call it principle. Jude had seen it happen to better minds than Paul's.

He did not think the father had put it there. A brilliant man, Jamis said, and dead. A man who would run his experiments with a child close enough to be burned by them had not been much troubled by scruple. No, someone else had taught the boy to ask whether loyalty could be true, and had done it early, and done it well. The same someone, Jude suspected, who had given him that orderly, well-furnished mind.

The mother, then.

Jamis had said almost nothing of her, only that Paul was of the house of Monmurg through her line. But the silence where she ought to have been told its own story. Dead, most likely, and young. Dead, and still in the room. Whatever she had been, she had given her son a conscience along with his schooling, and she had done it so thoroughly that twenty years behind palace walls had not been enough to wear it away. Jude found that he resented her for it a little, this woman he had never met. She had made his work harder.

Or more interesting. He was not yet sure which.

The father was dead as well. Jude turned that over as he walked. A young man whose father was dead and whose name few people knew. A young man who would want, more than he would ever say aloud, someone to show him the way his father would have shown him, if his father had lived. A teacher stood in that place, near enough. A teacher who was patient, and clever, and asked for nothing back, at least not yet. Jude had told Paul that morning how a man filled that empty place in another man's life, and what it would buy him. He had not, at the time, been thinking of Paul. He was almost sure he had not.

He put the thought away for later. It would keep.

The foreign district closed around him as he went: the crooked lanes and leaning upper stories, the smells of a dozen kitchens that were cooking less than they used to, the babble of half a dozen tongues. Somewhere off to the left, down toward Blood Alley, Ferd's would be opening its shutters on a common room that very likely still had men asleep in it, sleeping off the night before's indulgences. Jude went the other way, toward the square.

He would teach the young man. That much was decided, and had been since Jamis put it to him. He would teach him well, because there was no point in doing it badly, and he would watch. He would see whether the hunger grew faster than the conscience, or the conscience faster than the hunger, and he would learn a good deal about Paul either way. If the hunger won, Jude would have made a wizard, and a patron, and very possibly a prince's man who owed everything he knew to an elf. If the conscience won, he would have made a decent mage of the middling sort, a loyal nephew of a good prince, and a friend. There were worse outcomes. Jude could not, at the moment, think of one that would do him much harm.

He found, a little to his surprise, that he was curious which it would be. He had not been curious about a human being in some time.

The alchemist's shop stood in a small courtyard off one of the foreign district's crooked streets, a squat building of real stone among the driftwood and plaster around it, with a heavy oak door that had been bolted and barred for as long as Jude had known it. He knocked, and waited, and eventually heard the bar lift.

The old alchemist put his head out, already talking. "Can I help you, can I h—" He sniffed, and recoiled.

"Gills," Jude said, holding out the rag. "Sahaugin. Two days dead. And something else." He took the fold of oilcloth from his inner pocket and set it on top of the rag. "A pinch of the powder that killed it. I'd like to know what's in both."

The old man took the bundle and the packet between two fingers as though they were both slightly on fire, and looked from them to Jude and back.

"You have been keeping these," he said, "in your clothes."

"I had no jar."

"Evidently." The old man's eyes went to the oilcloth, and something changed in his face. "Give me until evening," he said, and shut the door.
