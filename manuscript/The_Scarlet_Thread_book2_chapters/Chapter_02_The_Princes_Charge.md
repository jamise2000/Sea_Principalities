# Chapter Two — The Prince's Charge

Paul Revero lay in his own bed in the hour before sunrise on the fourth of Wealsun and watched the shutters turn from black to grey, and did not try to sleep. He had not slept much all night. He had gone back over the previous day instead, the way he would have gone back over a page he meant to remember.

The corsair had come into Monmurg the evening before with the coast air behind her and the light going amber over the harbor forts. She had not made for the Harbor District, where a warship of her kind would ordinarily tie up among the naval berths. Someone had signaled ahead, and she had been waved instead toward the Royal Docks, the long stone quays below the palace hill where the prince's own vessels lay, and where no ship berthed without leave from the palace. Paul had stood at the rail as the lines went ashore and had not needed to ask why. He was aboard. That had been reason enough.

He remembered the quay already busy when they came in: men in the plain dark coats of the palace household waiting at the foot of the gangway, and behind them a file of soldiers who were not Marines, standing with the unhurried patience of men who had been told to take possession of something and would not be leaving without it. They had gone aboard and come back down with the four small barrels the survivors had rafted off Helm Island, each one marked with its three red crosses, carried between two men as carefully as a coffin. Nobody on the quay had asked what was in them. The men carrying them had not looked as though they needed to be told. Paul had found that more reassuring than anything else he had seen since the Helm.

The rest of the ship's company had gone off by the harbor road — the Marines, the bard, the big, earnest fighter, Merrick in his rumpled officer's coat, and Gouge, who had come aboard at the Helm looking like one more crossbowman and had left looking like one more crossbowman, which was a skill of its own. Paul and Jude had been separated from them at the foot of the gangway by an attendant in household livery, who bowed to Paul and addressed them both. Master Jude was expected at Lord Jamis's office that night. The prince would see his nephew himself, in the morning, and asked that until then he rest, and speak to no one of the Helm.

Jude's glance had slid to his and away again, and Paul had understood it well enough. Lord Jamis commanded the fleet and ran more of the city than most men knew, but he did not debrief a prince's nephew. That was the prince's privilege, and Jeon meant to exercise it in his own time and his own room. What Paul had not understood, and had lain awake turning over, was why that time should be the morning and not the night. His uncle had never in his life been slow to hear bad news.

Then the stair: up the private way cut into the hill behind the docks, past a guard post at the first landing and another at the second, through a postern that opened on a garden he had played in as a child, and into the palace. It was his home. It had always felt, walking into it, a little like walking into somebody else's. And then these rooms, aired while he was away, with a fire laid against the evening and wine set out that he had not wanted. He had stood a long while in the middle of the floor without moving, listening to the palace settle around him, a door somewhere below, the wind working at a shutter, and had been surprised to find how glad he was to be standing in it.

The coat lay folded by the door where he had left it. He could smell it from the bed. It had still been damp through the lining from the chasm when he stripped it off, and it smelled of the sea and of the thing that had come up out of the dark water beneath him. The shoulder of it had been scorched brown when the tower went up in flame behind them, and the burnt leather had a smell of its own, one he knew from long before the Helm and did not care to think about. The servants would want to take it away and burn it, and he did not mean to let them. It had gone up the wall with him, and into the chasm, and out through the smuggler's caves, and come home, as he had. He would have the lining dried and keep it, scorch and all.

And somewhere down in the Harbor District, while he lay here, Jude would have been telling Lord Jamis his version of the Helm, and Gouge and Merrick would have told theirs after him. By now his uncle's spymaster had the whole of it from three mouths, and Paul lay in the grey with his own account still untold, waiting to be sent for.

###

The summons came at first light.

His uncle's office sat high on the palace's seaward side, a long room with tall windows and a table that took up most of the floor. Prince Jeon was already there. He stood with both hands flat on the table, bent over a chart of the outer islands, the Salinmoor coast drawn along its northern edge and the wide sweep of Jerlea Bay across the south, the whole sea between them scattered with markers Paul could not read from the door. The shutters were open on a grey sky, but the lamps were still burning from the night. Their light caught the grey coming into Jeon's close-cut beard and the lines at the corners of his eyes, and Paul noted, before his uncle had said a word, that the shoulders were wrong. Jeon carried himself like a man who had been bent over that chart for most of the night and did not expect it to tell him anything he wanted to hear.

"Paul." Jeon straightened. "Sit down, please."

Paul sat. The chair was hard and too low, as the chairs in this room had always been. He had never decided whether that was an accident.

"I've heard there was some trouble on Helm Island," Jeon said.

"Yes."

"The reports I have say the ship we sent you on was overwhelmed. Two hundred and fifty Marines aboard that corsair." He did not phrase it as a question. "All gone."

"Not all," Paul said. "Twenty came ashore with us, and those lived. So did the thirty we put off at the Southern Beacon. The rest—" He had seen it from the tower, the water around the hull churning white and then dark, and he did not describe it. "It was the sahaugin. And there were hobgoblins holding the island besides."

Jeon came around the end of the table and took the chair across from him. It put them at the same height. "Tell me more of that."

Paul looked around the room once — at the door, which was shut, at the corners, which were empty, at the chair beside his uncle's, where Lord Jamis would ordinarily have been sitting.

"Is Lord Jamis joining us?"

"No. It is only you and I."

Paul turned that over. His uncle trusted Jamis further than he trusted anyone living; everyone at court knew it. And yet Jamis had taken Jude's account last night, and Gouge's and Merrick's after it, and Paul was being heard alone, without him, as though the prince wanted one account in this room that had not passed through his spymaster's hands first. It did not mean Jeon suspected Jamis. It meant, Paul thought, that the prince was no longer certain whom he could afford to trust without checking, and wanted the one account he could vouch for to come to him straight.

"Fairwind is a traitor," Paul said.

Jeon did not move. He was a big man, broad in the chest in the way of the Oeridian houses, and he had the big man's trick of going perfectly still when he was struck by something. "How do you know this?"

So Paul told him, and made himself tell it in order.

The corsair had put thirty Marines ashore at the Southern Beacon before running into the Helm's harbor. That had not been in the orders; Jude had asked for it, and Paul had backed him. Paul and Jude had gone onto the docks with twenty more to scout ahead of the main landing, and they had not been on the stones a quarter hour before the towers above the harbor opened on them. There had been nothing to do but take the nearest one, and they had taken it. "When we turned to fall back to the ship," Paul said, "there wasn't a ship to fall back to. The sahaugin were over the rails. The water was full of them. I think she's on the bottom of the harbor now, and everyone aboard her with her."

They had fought their way inland to the barracks and found men there still holding: a company of Fairwind's own Marines, stationed on the island, shut in behind their own doors while hobgoblins held the citadel above them.

"Fairwind's men," Jeon said.

"His garrison. They didn't know anything. How the hobgoblins had gotten inside the Helm, whether any of their people were still fighting up there, what had become of their lord — none of it. They'd been holding the barracks and waiting for someone to tell them what had happened."

"Under siege themselves."

"Yes. We joined them and made a plan. I took a small party up over the outer wall on the seaward side, in the dark, while the rest of the garrison made a show at the gate to draw every eye there. We got inside and worked our way through the keep toward where I thought he'd be."

"The inner sanctum."

"The inner sanctum. I sent my homunculus ahead." Paul felt, as he said it, the small cool weight of the creature where it lay curled in the inner pocket of his coat, and the memory of the corridor as it had seen it: stone at eye level, the underside of a lintel, lamplight on a ceiling. "I could see and hear through it. He was in the room with them. Standing with them, talking with them. Dealing."

"With whom?"

"The Toli."

Jeon's face did not change, but something went out of it. "The Toli. In the Helm."

"Yes."

"How do you know they were Toli?"

"I saw them," Paul said. "And we took one of them alive."

His uncle breathed out through his nose, slowly. "Go on. You saw Lord Fairwind in the same room with Toli agents, talking with them, making a bargain with them. What was the bargain?"

"The powder," Paul said. "Moving it off the island. The substance that keeps the sahaugin away. And one of the Toli said to him — I remember this exactly — 'We noticed you took most of it for yourself.'" He let his uncle hear that. "He was skimming the stores. I think he meant to keep most of it for himself and let the Toli carry the blame for what was gone. That was his game. He'd been emptying the Helm for months, and when there wasn't enough left in the water to keep the sahaugin off, they came back."

"What became of Fairwind then?"

"We took him," Paul said. "I put him under arrest. He made a great deal of noise about it and played the injured innocent the whole way down to the smuggler's caves, but I knew that he wasn't. He led us down out of the sanctum into a cave system under the keep — a smugglers' way, cut down through the cliff to the sea, that nobody on the island seemed to know was there. We had the Toli prisoner with us. On the stairs over the water we were spotted, and it went bad. The Toli was killed and went into the water; we couldn't carry him through a fight. Fairwind was whispering to one of our people on the way down, and when it broke he shoved his way past me and I went over the side. I fell into the chasm. There was a shark down there the size of a fishing boat." He said it flatly, because there was no other way to say it. "Jude brought me back up. Fairwind went for Gouge at the edge, and Gouge threw him off. He went into the water."

"He's dead, then."

"I don't know. We didn't find a body. He could be at the bottom of that chasm. He could be on a Toli ship. I don't know."

Jeon said nothing.

"We fought our way down through the rest of Fairwind's men to the grotto at the bottom," Paul went on. "Built a raft, and poled it out through the sea cave. We put four barrels of the powder on it and opened one as we went, and the sahaugin didn't follow us."

"How many barrels were there?"

"Twenty-five, counting those four. The four we brought. The other twenty-one were still in the grotto when we pushed off."

"And the rest? The stores that ought to have been there?"

"Gone. The storerooms in the keep were empty."

"Who else," Jeon said, "witnessed Fairwind speaking with the Toli agents?"

Paul had known the question would come. It did not make it pleasant. "Nobody. It was only me."

"So you are the only man with a definite witness to his betrayal."

"Yes."

"And your companions. How much have you told them of his—"

"Treason," Paul said.

Jeon did not answer at once. He had left the sentence open on purpose, Paul realized — left a space at the end of it for a softer word to fall into. 'Suspicions,' perhaps. 'Dealings.' 'What you believe you saw.' His uncle had been testing him from the moment he sat down: going back over the sanctum twice, asking who else had heard, asking how he knew the Toli for Toli. Not doubting him, exactly. Looking for the one loose thread in Paul's account that he could pull, and so leave some small room in which his oldest friend was a fool, or a dupe, or a prisoner, and not what Paul had said he was.

Paul did not give it to him. He sat in the low chair with his hands still on his knees and the mask turned full on his uncle's face, and he did not qualify the word, or explain it, or take it back.

Jeon held his eyes for a long moment. Then something in the big frame gave, very slightly — not the shoulders, which he kept square, but the jaw, where a muscle that had been working since Paul came through the door went still. He drew a breath through his nose and let it out slowly, and Paul watched his uncle set down the last of whatever he had been carrying through the night. When he spoke again his voice was quieter and a good deal harder.

"Treason," he said. "Yes."

"I told them he's a traitor. That he was dealing with the Toli. That's the whole of it. I didn't give them particulars. They believed me over him." Paul shifted in the low chair. "It doesn't make sense any other way. What he told us was that he'd had his men give up their arms and locked them away in the lower keep so none of them would be killed. We never found those men. But whatever he did with them, he let the hobgoblins walk into the Helm."

"So it is your judgment that Fairwind allowed the hobgoblins to take the fortress, and put his own garrison where it couldn't stop them."

"And that the hobgoblins were working with the Toli."

"Which is strange," Jeon said, half to himself, "except that the Duke of Berghof is known to hire hobgoblin mercenaries. Which puts his hand somewhere in this as well." He looked at Paul. "Were they red?"

"They were. I'd never seen one before."

"I have. Red-skinned, and taller than you'd think. They come out of the Crystalmist Mountains, which the Duke's domain backs up against, and he's dealt with their chiefs for years. They're easier to handle than most of their kind, if you have the coin. But it's a disturbing thing, to see them used like this." He was quiet for a moment. "Is there any notion of how they got onto the island?"

"We never found out for sure, but most likely on Cain Toli's ship up through the smuggling caves. We had to get clear and get word home. I didn't know whether you were sending ships after us, or what they'd sail into."

"We had a signal from the Southern Beacon," Jeon said. "Your first landing party, I assume. It came in just after you went ashore, and it told us to send no more ships until the island was secured."

"The sahaugin," Paul said.

"That's why, I assume. By the time the warning reached us you were already fighting." He rubbed his thumb along the edge of the table. "From what you tell me, it's safe now to go back in, if the ships carry barrels of their own."

"They should."

"There'll still be hobgoblins on that island," Paul added. "I don't know what became of the men in the barracks after we left. And there was that ship; Cain Toli's ship, as I said. We'd see her sometimes, a long way out, when the fog opened. Not a big ship, by Toli standards, but she looked fast. They were moving the powder out to her through the caves. That's where most of it went. By the time we got down there she'd gone."

"Did you learn her name?"

"The Sea Ghost."

Jeon's mouth tightened. "Cain Toli's ship. You're certain of that?"

"That's what we believe. That's what Fairwind said, at least."

"Then I'd wager you're right about the hobgoblins as well. It's the likeliest answer." Jeon looked back at the chart as if it might have altered while they talked. "They were shipping it out through the caves?"

"Back and forth. Boats to the sea cave and out to the ship, and back."

"Then I'll have Lord Jamis send a flotilla to Helm Island, with troops enough to finish it. We'll retake the smugglers' caves and bring off whatever powder is still there. The waters around the island are blockaded now, and they'll stay that way."

He sat back. The chair creaked under him.

"I want to speak about the men you went with," he said. "Most of them were Marines, I understand. But there were men of note among them. Merrick, whom Lord Jamis recommended to me. And Jamis had two of his own agents with you. Gouge, whom I've heard of in passing. And this Jude — a wizard for hire, I'm told. We've had dealings with him before. He's been questioned." His eyes came back to Paul. "What do you make of them?"

"They're capable," Paul said. "Good at what they do."

"Are those the only men you discussed Fairwind's treachery with?"

"No. There were five of us who went up over the wall together, besides me. Merrick. Gouge. Jude. And two more — Folsom, and Tyrus."

"And those two are—"

"Marines. Ordinary Marines, as far as I know."

"Then they'll be spoken to," Jeon said. "Quietly. They'll need to be kept quiet, certainly. I'll leave that to Lord Jamis."

"They report to him anyway," Paul said.

"And Merrick. He acquitted himself honorably?"

"What do you mean?"

"He was loyal," Jeon said. "To me."

Paul understood that he was being asked whether Jamis's man could be trusted, and that his uncle would not ask it any more plainly than that. "Did you send him?"

"Jamis recommended him."

"He's loyal," Paul said. "They all are. I don't think they'll give us trouble."

"And Gouge?"

"Gouge is—" Paul considered how to describe a man who had carried a crossbow in the ranks for three days without a single Marine noticing him unless he wanted them to, and then thrown a prince of the principalities into a chasm without appearing to think twice about it. "He makes quick decisions under pressure. He's dangerous in a fight; angry, but controlled. He knows who his enemies are. He's a professional."

"And this Jude?"

"He's a powerful wizard," Paul said. "The task you gave him was to keep me alive. He did it."

It was a thinner answer than the truth. The truth was the Toli prisoner on the plank bridge, on his knees with his hands bound, and Jude lifting his wand and ending him as calmly as a man snuffing a candle, then tipping the body into the water because it would have slowed them down. It was the way Jude spoke of killing, when he spoke of it at all: as a sum to be worked, a cost set against a gain, never once as a thing that weighed on him afterward. Jude had kept Paul alive because he had been paid to keep Paul alive. Paul did not doubt that he would have spent any other life on that island, his own excepted, just as readily, had the sum come out that way. None of that went into his answer. His uncle, who knew him, looked at him a moment longer than necessary, and then let it stand.

"Now," Jeon said, "there is something else I want to say to you, Paul, and I've given it a good deal of thought."

His tone had changed. It was lower, and slower, and more careful, the voice he used in council when he had already decided a thing and meant to bring the room around to it without seeming to push.

"You came to me, not long ago, with what you'd found out about your past. Your father. And since then I've been thinking. I want you to take what I'm about to say as a suggestion, regarding your own path, and how you might be of most use to your family in a time when it needs every use it can get." He folded his hands. "I've noticed that you've given yourself over to what might be called the ledger side of things. Spycraft. I think that valuable, and I don't say otherwise. But I knew your father. I knew Grayson when we were young men together, and I was very impressed by his mind. And by your mother's, as well. They were both brilliant people."

Paul held perfectly still behind the mask. His uncle had spoken that name to him once before, only days ago, and had chosen every word of it then as if it might break in his hand. He did not choose them that way now. He spoke of Grayson as a man speaks of an old friend, and that was harder to hear.

"He talked often about the yellow substance," Jeon went on. "He used to say that this state would never be free until we were the ones who made it. I don't know whether you're aware of this, but that compound was never ours. The Duke of Berghof gave it to us, and he stopped giving it. We've been running short for decades. What was stored on Helm Island, even before it was taken, would have lasted us perhaps another three years. Now it's gone, and we have no time at all, and no way to make more. We don't have the knowledge. Your father saw that, twenty years ago or near it, and he was determined to change it. Unfortunately, the great alchemists of this part of the world all owe their loyalty to Keoland."

"Or to the Duke of Berghof," Paul said.

"Or to the Duke of Berghof." Jeon nodded slowly. "Do you understand what I'm asking?"

"That I go to one of them. Try to find something."

"More than that. Go to the alchemists, yes; you'll need them. But I don't want you carrying questions to other men and bringing their answers back to me. I want you to understand the magic in it yourself, well enough to know when they're telling you the truth. I'm asking you because I believe you're capable of it. Would you study the making of this substance? Would you take it on, as a quest of your own, and find a way to do it?"

Paul did not answer at once. He knew better than most men in the palace what his uncle was asking of him. The homunculus had taken him only a day of study, but Paul knew it would have been beyond the abilities of most men. And that had been one small creature of clay, made from one book, by following a path another man had already walked. What Jeon wanted had no book. It would take years, and it would take the place of most of the other things he might have done with them. But he turned it over and saw the other side of it just as plainly. A prince's nephew was one more lord in a city full of lords. A prince's nephew who could make the yellow powder, or who understood the magic the Toli were turning against Monmurg's ships, would be the one man in the principalities his uncle could not do without.

"Yes," he said. He said it quietly, without hurry and without any of the eagerness his uncle might have expected, the way a man sets his name to a contract he has read to the last line. It was a vow, and he meant Jeon to hear it as one.

"It would take you off the road you're on now. You'd have to go into the arcane arts properly."

"Yes."

"Most men of our blood are reluctant to go there," Jeon said. "The Oeridian houses have always left magic to other people and bought it when we needed it. But I see now that our neglect of it has brought us here. We need wizards of our own."

"We do."

"Which brings me to my second point. Do you feel that Jude is a man you'd be willing to have teach you?"

Paul thought about the wall, and the dark, and the feeling of being lifted out of black water by nothing he could see. But he thought, too, of the way Jude had gone at every trouble on that island: the wall, the sanctum, the prisoner on the plank bridge. He had never once seemed hurried, or afraid, or sorry. He had looked at each thing as a sum to be worked, set down what it would cost against what it would gain, done it, and gone on to the next without looking back. Paul did not admire all of it. But he had watched it bring them off the Helm alive, and he suspected that a man who learned to think that way would have learned something worth more than spells. "Yes. He's a powerful wizard. He could teach me."

"Excellent." Jeon allowed himself something close to a smile. "I've already asked Lord Jamis to put it to him. From what I understand, Jude is a man who deals in bargains. But at present we have things he wants. If he teaches you what he knows, it will be of great advantage to this family. And frankly, a prince of Monmurg with a wizard's abilities would be of great advantage in his own right."

He let that settle, and Paul let it settle in him. Jeon had no wife and no son. He had never spoken of that either.

"So I charge you with this," Jeon said. "Look into the arcane arts, as your father did, and your mother before you. I'll give you full leeway in it. Do whatever you need to do, so long as it brings no harm to this city and no stain on our name. There are magics outlawed in Keoland, and in the Duke of Berghof's lands. Necromancy comes first to mind. But so long as no one learns of it, I won't restrict what you study, or where you go to study it. You have my leave to use the Royal Library as you see fit. And if there are things you need to acquire — I understand wizards require some very strange components — I'll do what I can. The Grand Market still draws wizards from every corner of the world. It's been a very hard year, but I expect that for now, at least, we can find what you'll need somewhere in this city."

"Yes," Paul said.

"Jamis was to speak with Jude about teaching you last night, and I'd like to see it under way without delay. And I'm charging the two of you, together, with this yellow powder. How it's made. Where it comes from. Everything we need to know about it. We have months before our supply runs out entirely."

"Months."

"Months. And there are other places to look. If Fairwind did betray us, he has a great deal of it stored somewhere on Fairwind Island. But I do not wish to go openly to war with Fairwind. Not now."

"Why?" Paul said. "He's a traitor."

"He's a prince of the principalities. He has a great many men and a great many ships."

"So do we."

"That is true," Jeon said patiently. "But we're at war with Gradsul. If we show Gradsul our weakness — if we show him the princes divided against each other — it will be a disaster."

"So we let Fairwind get away with it."

"I didn't say that." Jeon's voice sharpened, and then eased again. "I did not say that. The first thing I want from you and Jude is to find where Fairwind has put his share. Then we strike. He can play his games. We can play ours." He looked down at the chart again, at the long reach of water between Monmurg and the outer isles. "But there is a clock on us, Paul. It will be only so long before we run out. And the truth is that Monmurg is more exposed to the sahaugin than any other holding in the principalities."

"That makes sense." Paul followed his uncle's eyes to the chart, and the reason lay plain on it. The Strait of Monmurg was no shallow sound but a deep channel, running from the Bay of Salinmoor through into Jerlea Bay, its soundings marked deeper than any other water in the principalities. Anything that rose out of the depths of the Azure Sea could pass through it into Jerlea Bay without ever showing itself, and Monmurg sat on its shore like a gatekeeper with no gate to close. "How long does one barrel last, once it goes into the water?"

"Not long. A matter of days. The salting ships used to go out every other day with a barrel. But one barrel covers a great deal of water — miles of it. The whole of the Strait of Monmurg was kept clear, most days, by a single barrel. They take it up toward the head of the strait, tip it over the side, and let the current carry it down past the city." He paused. "We've done it that way for a hundred and fifty years."

"I'll start looking into it."

"There is a political side to this as well," Jeon said. "Which is why I don't want you crying Fairwind's treason from the spires. Not yet. What I hope is that we can bring him to terms — get the supply back from him, or at least some part of it."

"Then you might want to talk to those Marines," Paul said. "The ones who came back with us."

"I will. I'll have Lord Jamis see to it."

"I don't think they'll talk. They're loyal men."

"Good," Jeon said. "Good. Because if I can avoid a war with Fairwind, I will. I don't want Keoland to see us weak in that way. We look weak enough to that country already. Any open quarrel among the princes would bring a direct attack down on us, and I won't see that."

He was silent for a moment, and when he spoke again it was more quietly.

"There is another thing. We've looked very weak at sea, and Lord Jamis believes it's because of some talisman Cain Toli carries with him. I confess I find that hard to credit. A trinket that sinks fleets is the stuff of sailors' tales." He paused. "But we've lost ships we should not have lost, and I can't account for it any better than he can. Until we understand what is happening out there, I will not risk an attack on Keoland's fleet. Jamis can give you his reasons himself, when the time comes. And I think that your gifts, if you have them, might help us undo whatever advantage the Toli have found. Any knowledge you gain of the arcane will serve us here, and I want you to be my interpreter into that world." He met Paul's eyes. "I don't trust anyone else to do it. I don't trust Jude."

"Why?" Paul said. "Has he given you any reason not to?"

"No. Of course not. But he isn't part of this family. I have no reason not to use him as an advisor. I would rather have you be the expert."

Paul considered that. "That's fair." And then, turning it over further: "I think that would be the strongest thing, actually. A wizard who was also a spy."

"Yes," Jeon said. "That is what these times require. Ship's captains and good fighters have their uses, and I'd not do without them. But we have them in plenty. What we lack are men who can work unseen, and men who understand magic." He sat back. "That will do for now, Paul. Thank you. You've had a long road home, and I've kept you from your breakfast long enough."

Paul set his hands on the arms of the low chair to rise, and then did not. He sat a moment longer, looking at the chart rather than at his uncle.

Jeon watched him. "There's something else," he said. "What is it you want to talk about?"

"Let me keep those four under my command," Paul said. "Merrick, Gouge, the other two. As a group of my own."

"I'll consider it. If you want to stay in contact with them, I understand that. But Merrick is of high rank, and Lord Jamis has plans for him — though I'm sure he can be drawn on when there's need. Gouge is another matter entirely. Gouge is something Lord Jamis allocates as required. But if you have a mission that calls for a man of that skill, I'll see him put at your disposal. Come to me when you have an actual mission, and those four are yours."

"I will."

"In the meantime, I suggest that you and Jude go on with what you were doing before, and put your research into where and how this powder is made. And into where Fairwind may have taken his. That will mean sending spies to Fairwind Island, likely. Hiring informants. You'll have the money for it." Jeon tapped the table once with one finger. "A good spymaster knows how to delegate, Paul. I suggest you learn it. Let your agents bring word to you. Cultivate a web."

"I don't really have any agents."

"You can get them. Jamis tells me Jude is very adroit at it."

"We have one man," Paul said. "But that was three days ago."

"Find more. Find more. Jamis says Jude is particularly skilled at bending men to his will, by one means or another. Learn that from him, as well. Use him as your cutout. Your front man." Jeon's gaze did not waver. "But tell me what he does. Tell me what agents you have. And report to me what you learn."

"Yes."

"Good." His uncle rose, and so Paul rose. "Jude will be coming up to the palace this morning. Meet with him and discuss your plans."

###

Paul went back to his own rooms. The coat still lay folded by the door, where he had told the servants to leave it, and the fire had been laid again, though no one had lit it. He sat on the edge of the bed with his hands between his knees and thought about his uncle saying his father's name, and about a clock, and about how many months there were in a year, until a servant came to tell him that the wizard was at the postern.
