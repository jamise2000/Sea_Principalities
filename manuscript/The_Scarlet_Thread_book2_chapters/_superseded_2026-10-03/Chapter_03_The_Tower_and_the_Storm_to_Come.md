# Chapter Three — The Tower and the Storm to Come

Jamis refilled Jude's glass without asking and did not touch his own.

"What might help me," Jude said, "in any work I do for you from here on — and I don't know whether this is a thing you'd be willing to give me — is a list. Men you want dead on sight." He turned the stem of the glass between two fingers. "It would have been useful to know if one existed. On the Helm I was acting on my own judgment. I thought we needed to bring Fairwind back here alive. If there's ever anyone—"

"At this point," Jamis said, "Fairwind is not someone to be killed on sight, Jude. I do not want him assassinated."

"All right."

"The fact that he doesn't fully understand the political situation means that, in time, he may come to understand it. He may realize that his loyalty belongs to the principalities. That the Prince of Toli is a great deal more dangerous to him than Jeon ever was. And that this game he's playing only weakens him. I don't think any of the princes will survive what the Toli mean to bring."

"On that we agree," Jude said.

Jude drank. The wine had opened in the glass and was better than it had been. "How is Monmurg holding? The embargo. Food. Are we losing men to desertion? Is the city able to—"

"The situation is dire." Jamis said it without emphasis, as he might have given the state of the tide. "Our stores are going down fast. We get some food from the smugglers who run to Redshore — that's where most of the merchant shipping has gone now. You saw it yourself. You brought me word of it, and my own people have since confirmed everything you told me." He tilted his head slightly. "I find it interesting that the Earl of Redshore approached you, and talked with you as freely as he did. I may have more to say about that in time. There may be an opening there. I may send you back to him as an envoy."

Jude filed that away and said nothing.

"And I'll say again," Jamis went on, "that I don't believe it's only Fairwind and Jeon being played by the Toli. I have a feeling that the principalities, and Keoland itself, are being played by other forces as well. It's dangerous. I don't know how much you know of what's happening in Keoland at present, but it looks as though civil war there is very close."

"Between whom?"

"The dukes. And the great Suel houses of that nation." Jamis's fingers rested lightly on the arm of his chair. "The Duke of Gradsul ought, by any reasonable measure, to have been crowned king some twelve years ago. He was denied it, and he has been trying to become king ever since. The man they chose instead was an Oeridian of no particular account from a back province called Greyhill. Kimbertos Skotti. He died recently. There is no one on the Lion Throne now."

"Is there any suspicion he was murdered?"

"The royal alchemist died a month before the king did."

Jude raised his eyebrows.

"So yes," Jamis said. "It is very suspicious, what's happening there."

"And is Gradsul the greater danger to the Sea Princes? Or to Jeon?"

"When I say the Sea Princes, I'm speaking only of Jeon. For now." Jamis looked at him steadily. "Because we're close to civil war with the Toli ourselves, depending on where Fairwind falls."

"I think it's already started, if I'm honest," Jude said.

Jamis did not dispute it.

"When you speak with Prince Jeon," Jude said, "let him know this."

"The last time I spoke with Fairwind, before any of this began," Jamis said, "he remarked that he thought Monmurg would fall without the Helm and the support of the Outer Islands." He let that sit. "So he understands the gravity of it. He understands exactly how grave it is. And he is playing a very dangerous game anyway. To be honest with you, I don't know why. He's always been jealous of Monmurg's prosperity. The money, the trade. He's been outspoken that the negotiations over slavery with the Toli and with Berghof are unnecessary. He calls it idealism, trying to end a thing that's older than our country. But those are small grievances. Why he has gone to such lengths now is beyond me, and it's very dangerous. That is one of the things I'd like you to try to find out, if you can."

"One of them?"

"There are several. The second is this. The salting ships did salt the sea around the Helm before it fell. Why didn't it take? Was some magic worked to keep the substance from doing what it's meant to do?"

Jude had been turning the same question over since the grotto. "Could Fairwind have been replacing the barrels as he took them?"

"I need to know that. If he took the true barrels and put a substitute in their place that did nothing, that is a deeper treason still. More evidence of what he's been about." Jamis's voice had grown quieter and more precise. "Or is the substance itself losing its strength? Have the sahaugin learned to overcome it? Or is there some counter-agent that can defeat it? I want to know, and I think you're the one who should be looking."

"Is the tower equipped with anything," Jude asked, "or is it only lodging?"

"I'll have whatever you want brought to it. And you may carry books from the library there and back for your own work."

"Good."

"There is one other matter." For the first time that evening Jamis hesitated, and Jude, who had known him some years, noted it. "I confess I'm reluctant to raise it. But Prince Jeon has insisted. He believes that Paul Revero will make an excellent wizard, and he wants you to teach him."

Jude sat back. "Is Paul interested?"

"The prince means to push him in that direction."

"I can see it, if he has a natural talent for it." Jude thought of the small black clay lizard running along the ceiling of the Helm's inner corridor, and of the way Paul's head had tilted, a half-second behind the creature, as though he were listening through its ears. "Did he really make that homunculus himself?"

"I couldn't tell you." Jamis turned his glass a quarter turn on the table. "I've never seen the thing, and I didn't ask. But the boy comes of a line that has never been short of mages. Nor of sorcerers, for that matter. It's quite possible."

Jude was silent a moment. He had spent a good part of his life believing homunculi to be a conjurer's fraud, clay dolls and pickled toads sold to credulous lords, and he had been wrong about it in front of Paul, which he disliked. A man who could build one unaided out of what he had found in a book was either very lucky or very gifted, and Jude had not seen much in Paul that relied on luck.

"All right," he said. "I have some ideas about where I could begin, at least, with the material." He paused, and then, because it seemed wiser to ask now than to be asked later, "Am I allowed to keep a sample of it without hanging for it?"

"Yes. You've been trusted with the secret now. What you know of it, you know by my leave." Jamis made a small gesture, as though setting a piece on a board. "I want you to research how to make it. I want you to research what may have undone it in the strait."

"Certainly."

"And bring Paul into it. That is what Jeon wants. He wants someone in his own family to have knowledge of the arcane in this." Jamis looked at the shuttered window again, as though he could see through it to the water. "The yellow substance you've seen was not made by any of the Oeridian princes. The Duke of Berghof supplied it to us."

Jude let out a breath. "Then you need me to take it apart and work out how he made it."

"More or less. We have no idea how to produce it. The Duke of Berghof stopped supplying it to us—" Jamis stopped, and seemed to reconsider the figure before he gave it. "Longer ago than most men in this city would believe. Forty years, near enough. It isn't common knowledge, and I'd prefer it stay that way."

"Forty years." Jude did the sum without meaning to. "And the stores—the Helm was the largest? The only one?"

"We have a few barrels here in Monmurg. The Helm was built for storage; historically that's where the bulk of it was kept. We keep some here, but it's been drawn on heavily. There is another place it was stored. A significant store lies in Salinmoor Bay, between here and Saltmarsh."

"Is that still secure, as far as you know?"

"The Toli have had possession of it."

Jude looked at him. "Had possession of it — so it's theirs now? If we needed to raid it — or did they only just take it?"

"It has rotated between houses over the years. The princes trade custody of all these stores among themselves; it's an old arrangement. At present the custody is theirs."

"Do you want us to move to seize it? Because once they know we know—"

"Jude, at some point we'll need to do exactly that." Jamis raised one hand slightly. "But before we do, we need to understand how the Toli are able to destroy our ships. That is the other thing I want you to look into. That artifact, and whether it actually exists."

"I've heard of it," Jude said. "You told me the night I came home — the thing Phranck tore out of the dying Toli's mind, and the turtle dragon it commands." He turned the stem of his glass between two fingers. "Forgive me, but that's a great deal to hang on the last thoughts of a dying man. Men say anything at the end. They see anything. And it came to us by way of an Inquisitor out of Gradsul, reading those thoughts for himself, with no one to check what he found there against what he chose to tell you. Can anyone truly believe it?"

"A fair question," Jamis said, and turned his glass a quarter turn on the table. "I asked it myself. But since we last spoke of it I've grown surer of one thing. The ships we've lost these past months did not founder by chance, and it wasn't the Keoland fleet that sank them. Was it that artifact, then, and some beast Cain Toli holds with it? Is that why, every time we put out in force, something is waiting for us? If it is, we will never meet Keoland at sea until we understand what it is and how it can be broken. And if it isn't—" He stopped, and for a moment the careful voice was not careful at all. "I need to know, Jude. Whatever the truth of it is, I need to know."

Jude sat with that. He had heard it once already, over this same wine, and it sat heavier the second time. There had been a good deal of talk in the taverns this past week that he had dismissed: fogs that came up out of a clear sky, hulls that went down in calm water, crews that did not come home and left no wreckage.

"Are we able to get as far as Redshore, at least?" he asked. "Or into Jerlea Bay, without being attacked? Is it only when shipping goes out into open water that it's at risk?"

"The Dragon Isles lie very near both Redshore and Monmurg. So anything north of here—"

"Runs the risk."

"Runs the risk of Cain Toli and his dragons, if my worst fears are true. Jerlea Bay seems to be untouched. But that's because the Toli control Jerlea Bay. It's their own road. Their access to the Amedio jungle hasn't been troubled in the least."

"Do you believe the Toli could attack Monmurg directly?"

"Absolutely not. Our defenses are proof against any army they could field. Not even Keoland's armies, which are a great deal larger, could take this city."

"With the help of the sahaugin? If we have none of the material left to keep them off?"

"There is no help from the sahaugin," Jamis said. "I don't believe the sahaugin serve anyone. They go where they can feed. They're sharks after chum. They add to the chaos, nothing more. The Toli are doing this to break us. But you may be sure they have no wish to see the sahaugin loose in their own waters, either." He paused, and added in a slightly different voice, "Though I wonder whether they need to fear it as much as we do. It's possible they can lay hands on the yellow substance when they want it. We cannot."

"So," Jude said, gathering it together, "I research the substance. I find out whether it's weakened, or been thwarted, or—"

"Find out why it failed."

"And Fairwind — say again what you wanted of him?"

"Where he has stored his barrels. If it can be learned."

"And help Paul in this."

"Because that is the charge Jeon is giving him," Jamis said.

"And teach him. Or at least take his measure in the rudiments."

"In the arcane, yes. Exactly." Jamis was silent a moment. "Few people know who Paul's father was."

"I've been mildly curious," Jude said. "But if it's a matter of state, I don't need it."

"His father was brilliant." Jamis spoke to the shutters rather than to Jude. "And from what Jeon has told me, and from what I've seen of the boy myself, he has a very capable mind. His father's mother is a woman of great intelligence, and has some magic of her own."

Jude noted the tense, and noted where Jamis was looking while he used it, and did not remark on either. "Was he disfigured?" he asked instead. "Some injury? Or is he only shy? I don't need to know. I'm only curious whether I'll always be speaking to him through a mask."

"You should expect to," Jamis said. "And to be plain with you, his disfigurement has nothing to do with disease. He was burned. Badly. As a small child, in one of his father's experiments."

"Magic?"

"Magical."

Jude said nothing for a moment. He thought of the way Paul stood near fires, which was to say at an angle to them, never quite facing them, and of the scorched coat he had been wearing when they came off the ship.

"All right," he said. "So. The artifact. I look into what it is they're using to command those things, or at least what kind of thing it is. The yellow substance. And where Fairwind hid his barrels."

"Help Paul build a network of informants. Help him with his studies, see what he is capable of, and report to me." Jamis turned his eyes back from the window. "And do not be blinded by Prince Jeon's loyalties."

"His loyalties aren't mine. If you mean his friendship with Fairwind—"

"I mean his relationship with everyone."

Jude paused. "Oh."

"These are dangerous times," Jamis said. "And to be plain with you, I do not fully trust Prince Jeon's judgment."

"Suspect everyone."

"Exactly. That ship would not have been taken by the sahaugin if I'd had my way. There would have been a barrel of our own aboard her." For the first time something showed in Jamis's face, briefly, and was put away. "I think the prince's eyes are opening. Slowly. But if we're going to survive this, they need to open much faster than they are. And you will not let on to Paul about any of this."

"Of course." Jude set his glass down. "There's one thing I have in mind. Paul had command of the twenty Marines who came ashore with us — the only men off that ship who lived. Could you give me their names? And let me know if they're brought back to Monmurg, or reassigned here? If they stay on the Helm, fine — I only mean it in the sense of the web you were talking about. They'd know both me and Paul. And I'd think some of them feel they owe us their lives."

"I can give you the name of their commander," Jamis said. "He can speak to the men, if you wish."

"I'd rather Paul spoke to them. But yes. If you want him to build a network, that's where he starts. Getting men alive out of a sahaugin attack is a better foundation than most."

"And of course there's money for bribes, and the like. That, in fact, is about the only thing we have in quantity just now."

"That's what I was going to say," Jude said. "Those men, if they have families — they'd be grateful to be able to—"

"Food," Jamis said, "is the thing we have least of."

Neither of them said anything more about it.

A knock came at the inner door, and the attendant put his head in and murmured something. Jamis nodded.

"I'm told Gouge will be in to see me shortly," he said. "I'm sure you have things for him and his crew to be doing."

"I do."

"My man will take you back up the way you came. Use my residence as your road to the foreign district tonight, if you'd like to go and collect your things."

Jude rose, and felt the weight of the key in his pocket as he did. "When will the tower be ready for me?"

"Tomorrow. Come back to my residence tomorrow and I'll have someone take you there and see it furnished."

"Thank you for the wine," Jude said.

###

The stair was longer going up than it had been coming down. Jude climbed it behind the attendant's lamp with his hand on the cold wall and counted the landings again, and on the third, where the unused side passage went off into the dark, he felt, rather than heard, a draught come out of it smelling of the sea.

He was thinking about forty years. About a city that had been living on a dwindling store of something it could not make, and had not told itself so, for longer than most of its people had been alive. About a prince who could not believe his oldest friend would sell him, and a spymaster who did not trust the prince. About a young man in a mask who had made clay walk and see by reading a dead man's book, and had been burned as a child by that man's work, and was now to be handed to Jude to be taught the same art.

At the top of the stair the attendant opened the pantry door on light and the smell of bread, and Jude stepped through it into Jamis's house as if he belonged there. Tomorrow, apparently, he would have a tower.
