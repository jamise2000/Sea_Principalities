# Chapter Two — A Vintage from Keoland

Jude's escort took him away from the palace stair and down toward the water, and Jude went with his hands loose at his sides and his attention on every doorway they passed.

It had not escaped him, somewhere between the Helm and the harbor, that Lord Fairwind knew exactly whose man he was. Fairwind had looked at him across the inner sanctum with the bound, patient contempt of a lord who has recognized a servant of his enemy, and whether Fairwind was now at the bottom of a chasm or on the deck of a Toli ship, the knowledge had not necessarily died with him. Jude had been back in Monmurg barely a week before Jamis sent him out to the Helm. A year abroad had worn the city's faces out of his memory, and he had not yet learned the new ones. He did not care to take a knife from a side street on his first night home, delivered by a man he would not have known to look at.

So he walked as if the evening itself might try something.

It did not. The attendant led him to one of Lord Jamis's residences on the palace hill, where the guards passed them through without a word, and then not out again by the front but down: through a pantry, through a door that looked like a cupboard, and onto a narrow stair that dropped in long switchbacks through the rock of the hill. It was lit at intervals by shuttered lamps, and it smelled of damp stone and the lamp oil and, faintly, of the sea. Jude paid attention to the turns. He counted landings. He noted a side passage on the third that the attendant did not take and a barred door on the fifth that the attendant did not look at, and by the time the stair leveled out and ended in a plain wooden door he had a fair picture of where he was: under the Harbor District, somewhere near the naval offices, having crossed half the city without once showing his face in its streets.

It was the office he had always been brought to. He had simply never come to it this way.

This was, he understood, Jamis's own road between his house and his desk. Jamis had let him walk it. That was either a mark of trust or a demonstration that Jamis did not need to trust him, and with Jamis it was usually both. Either way, the point was made. Nobody in Monmurg would be able to say they had seen the elf go in to the admiral tonight.

On the last landing Jude asked, lightly, what had become of the barrels. The four that had come off the Helm with them.

"Taken in hand, sir," the attendant said. "By those whose business it is."

Jude had watched the men who took them, on the quay: not Marines, not the ship's crew, men in plain coats who had known what they were lifting without needing to be told. He had hoped, a little, that one of those barrels might go astray in the confusion and find its way to him. It had not been a serious hope. He still had the fold of oilcloth in his inner pocket, with its pinch of pale yellow grit that he had scraped out of a split stave in the grotto, and that would have to do. He found that he minded less than he expected. The men on the quay had handled the barrels correctly. That meant somebody in this city still knew what they were.

The attendant knocked, opened the door, and stood aside.

Lord Jamis was at his desk, and rose when Jude came in, which he did not always do.

He was a man it was easy to underestimate in a room: of middle height, neatly made, grey at the temples, dressed with a plainness that cost more than finery. He had a sailor's weathered hands and a clerk's stillness, and he did not waste motion. The office was as Jude remembered it — charts rolled in racks along one wall, a single window shuttered against the harbor, a low table with two chairs set on either side of it, and on a sideboard a row of bottles that ran from the very fine to the very bad.

"Jude. Please, take a seat."

"I need a drink," Jude said, and took the seat.

"I assume you're not partial to Gouge's red eye."

Jude made a face. He had tasted Gouge's red eye once, across a table at Ferd's, and had lost the feeling in his lips for an hour.

"No. I thought not." Jamis moved to the sideboard, passed over the row of bottles standing on it, and opened the cabinet beneath. "I have something better for you. Wine? Or would you like something harder?"

"Just wine."

The bottle Jamis brought out was one Jude knew. He had drunk from it on his first night back in Monmurg, in this very office, and had known within a swallow that the crate of Celene red he had carried across an ocean as a gift was not going to be given. What was left of that crate sat under his bed at Ferd's now, going out a cup at a time to loosen the right tongues.

Jamis poured it himself and brought it over, setting the glass on the low table at Jude's elbow before he took the other chair. "You'll remember this one," he said. "My brother-in-law's vintage, out of Keoland. I poured you a glass of it the night you came home, at this same table. You didn't say much about it then, but you finished the glass."

"I remember it." Jude lifted the glass and looked at the color against the lamp: a deep, clear red that smelled of dark fruit and something like cedar. "You told me it's near all you drink."

"It is." Jamis poured a second glass for himself. "Zafar sends it across when he can. I have never once asked him how it reaches me."

Jude turned the stem between his fingers. Zafar Azane sat in Gradsul at the Duke's right hand, and kept the Office that told Keoland's wizards what they might and might not know, and his wine came into a city his own Duke was starving. It went through a blockade that stopped grain and did not stop this. Jude found that he admired the bottle a little more for that, and trusted it a little less.

"Keoland," he said, tipping the glass toward the shuttered window and the starving city beyond it, "is blockading us, I'm told. Terribly strict about it. A sip of this and that's almost forgotten."

"Keoland is." Jamis did not smile. "And the Duke's flotilla stops a great many things. A crate that sails under the seal of the Duke's own right hand is not one of them. No captain on that line cares to be the man who opened Zafar Azane's wine." He lifted his glass an inch in Jude's direction, and drank.

Jude drank. It was very good. He let it sit on his tongue a moment and let the stair and the dark and the sea go out of his shoulders, and then set the glass down and looked at the man across from him.

Jamis had not drunk again since he spoke of the seal. His glass sat untouched at his elbow, and his eyes had gone to the shutters and stayed there, as a man's do when he has said a little more than he meant and would rather not be asked about it. Jude had no wish to be the one who asked. Not tonight, and not over the man's own wine.

"Have you had a chance to speak with Paul?" he said, as if the thought had only just come to him.

"I won't be speaking with Paul," Jamis said. "The prince is debriefing him himself. Perhaps I'll talk with him later. I wanted to speak with you before I heard from Gouge or from Merrick."

"Shall I tell you what happened from the beginning?"

"No." Jamis settled back in his chair. "Merrick and Gouge will be in to see me later tonight, and I'll have the particulars from them — the landings, the order of march, who stood where. I won't waste your time or mine making you recite it. Tell me what you think is important. What a wizard noticed that a soldier wouldn't have."

Merrick and Gouge were the only names Jamis offered, and Jude did not ask for others. If Jamis had had any other eyes on the Helm, Jude would learn of it when Jamis wished him to, and not before.

"Then I'll start with the company we kept," he said. "There's a bard. One of the men in Gouge's party. I can't remember his name." It irritated him more than it should have. He could have told Jamis how many steps there were on the cliff stair under the Helm, and the bard's name had slid away from him like a coin through a grate. "It's annoying me."

"I see." Jamis was quiet for a moment. "I assumed he was simply a Marine who went in with you. He is not one of mine. I'll look into him, however."

"The bard and Merrick were the ones who sent for the corsairs," Jude said. "When we got clear of the Helm. The word went out from the two of them that we'd be leaving by skiff and wanted picking up. It seemed to me—"

"That these are men Merrick trusts."

"Perhaps that's it. Perhaps they're his."

"That would be understandable," Jamis said. "Merrick did ask whether he might bring some of his own people, men he knew. I assume that's what this is. I'll speak with him and satisfy myself of their loyalty." He paused. "And the other one?"

"Tyrus?"

"Tyrus. What did you make of him?"

Jude thought about it. "A big fighter," he said. "Young, and tall, and heavy through the shoulders, and he fights like a door coming off its hinges. But he's loyal. Loyal to the principalities, to the prince, to Monmurg, in a way that's almost embarrassing to watch. Very enthusiastic. I never saw him do anything out of the ordinary. If I had, I'd tell you."

Jamis nodded slowly and let it go. "Did Fairwind confess to any crimes?"

"Not in so many words." Jude settled back in the chair. "We were inside the citadel, and we hadn't been discovered. Paul sent his homunculus ahead, and when it came back to him he was agitated. He said, 'We've got him.' So we went in. Before I ever saw Fairwind we had to fight our way through two Toli. They knew at least some magic — some counter to it. I put fire on one of them and he shrugged it off. Either they had a counterspell ready or they carried something that turned it. They had enchanted blades, rapiers and long daggers, as far as I could tell. Both of them are dead now. We had to go through them to reach him, and when we did, Fairwind was tied to a chair."

"Tied?"

"Bound to a chair in his own sanctum. Whether they'd bound him or he'd let himself be bound, I can't tell you." Jude turned the wine glass. "We meant to bring him back here. Let you and the prince treat with him, or question him, as you liked. On the way down, in the talk we had, he admitted to siphoning off the barrels. For months. I had a look into the barrel room in the keep — it was empty. What we found were in the grotto at the bottom of the cliff. Twenty-five of them, all told. The four we brought home are out of that twenty-five. Everything else had been shipped off already, and we learned where. Onto Cain Toli's ship. The Sea Ghost."

"Go on."

"So it sounds to me as though the Toli had taken some of those barrels, and were treating with Fairwind about the rest. And he was playing it off as business as usual. You know — princes jockeying for position among themselves, the way they always are." Jude frowned. "I did hear him say he'd been siphoning. And he wasn't surprised to find the Toli there. Either he was keeping his face very well, or he wasn't surprised at all. But they were there. I think Fairwind is out for his own advantage, and nobody else's. I think he believes that if he sides with the Toli against Jeon, it gets him leverage. More power. More say."

"So for him," Jamis said, "it is a political play."

"I think it was. And that was one reason I wanted to bring him back to you. I thought you might still be able to treat with him. Negotiate. Ransom him back to his own people, if nothing else." He hesitated. "My sense of the Toli, in the short time I've been back — after a year away — is that if they're involved with this man—"

"That," Jamis said, "is what I'm afraid of."

Jude waited.

"I'm glad to hear from you that Fairwind all but admitted he was playing politics and looking for leverage," Jamis said. "You should know something. Fairwind and Jeon are childhood friends. They've known each other their entire lives. And I think the prince's mind will be changed now, especially with Paul the one to accuse him. I'm glad it fell out that way." He looked at the shuttered window. "The truth is, you were sent because Prince Jeon could not imagine that his friend would betray him so."

"He did say outright that he'd been skimming those barrels," Jude said. "And that he may well be sitting on the largest stock of them still left."

"Which is treason against the principalities. Though not necessarily directly against Jeon." Jamis's voice was quite dry. "For him to be talking with the Toli, in and of itself, would not ordinarily be considered untoward. They're a house of the principalities, whatever else they are. But removing barrels of that substance is certainly treason. In fact, merely knowing of it, for any man who is not of the Admiralty, is treason."

Jude took that in and did not remark on how many men had seen those barrels in the last three days. "Then the Toli agents should never have been allowed anywhere near that part of the Helm."

"No."

"They'd have had to come up from below. Through the caves." Jude set his glass down. "We didn't come in through the citadel. We climbed one of the outer walls, close to the water on the seaward side. I lifted the others up it, and Gouge climbed on his own. We did it in the dark. Fairwind's troops had no idea we were there. A good part of his garrison had pulled back to their barracks by then — they'd been driven out of the keep a few nights before, under cover of that fog that came in, when the hobgoblins came. And the hobgoblins were dressed as Marines. Principality Marine kits, but in colors belonging to none of the princes."

"Interesting."

"I thought so. When I talked with Fairwind's commander at the barracks, he had no idea what had happened, other than that the hobgoblins held the inner citadel. He put on a show of force at the gate that gave us the distraction we needed to go up the wall. But when we made Fairwind show us the way out — we were trying to save any barrels that were left — he took us from the sanctum straight down. Through a door behind his own rooms and down into the cliff. And the sanctum was where we'd found the Toli. They'd most likely come up from the Sea Ghost the same way."

"So they know," Jamis said slowly, "not only about the barrels."

"They know about the entrance. Yes."

"And you think the hobgoblins took the Helm because Fairwind led them up through the smugglers' caves and into the keep."

"I'm not certain of that." Jude considered it honestly. "Fairwind said he didn't want to see any of his own men killed, so he made sure they were disarmed and shut up in other parts of the keep. That's his account. We didn't stay long enough to find out whether it was true. I'd meant to stay with Paul, and go on, but Gouge thought it would be wiser — and Merrick agreed with him — if we got clear and came straight back. That's why they called for help from the corsairs you sent. Or that Jeon sent." He shook his head. "I'm not sure whether the hobgoblins simply came in under the fog, as the sahaugin did. You know there were sahaugin there. Which is, I take it, exactly what the barrels were meant to prevent."

"Yes."

"We lost most of the complement," Jude said. "Two hundred Marines on the ship. I was able to keep twenty alive — the twenty who were ashore with Paul and me. And the thirty we'd put off at the Southern Beacon with Gouge came through as well. We fell back into the barracks alongside Fairwind's men. To my knowledge, those men had no idea of his duplicity. The ones in the outer garrison, at least. I never spoke to anyone who'd been inside the keep. But it didn't sound as if there'd been any massacre of his people."

Jamis was silent for a while.

"I have a feeling," he said at last, "that what Lord Fairwind was trying to do was keep up a pretense under which his theft of the barrels would go unnoticed. And afterward, he could lay it at the door of the Toli."

"That would make sense."

"It would." Jamis leaned forward and set his glass on the table between them. "Now. There are one or two things you ought to know, and I'll tell you. But you're not to speak to anyone of them. Not even Gouge."

"Or Paul?"

"Not even Paul."

"All right."

"No," Jamis said, as if Jude had argued. "The truth is that Prince Jeon's love for Fairwind has blinded him once already, and I'm afraid his feelings in this matter will blind him again. So when we act, we must act without those blinders. What I tell you stays between you and me." He held Jude's eyes. "Understand, this is not because I trust you so greatly, Jude. It's because I think you understand what patronage is. And times are desperate. And my patronage will pay you very well."

Jude smiled slightly. "Have you got another magic ring in that drawer?"

Jamis looked at him for a moment. Then, unexpectedly, something that was nearly humor moved at the corner of his mouth. "In fact," he said, "I have something rather better than that. Something I think you'll like. Real estate is very cheap in Monmurg at present."

Jude waited.

"I know where you are staying in the foreign district," Jamis said. "And you may have your reasons for living there — various disreputable reasons. I do not judge. I think what you're doing is keeping your thumb on the pulse of the foreign district, and I understand that. However." He turned his glass a quarter turn on the table. "There is a tower that overlooks the sea, which I think would suit you very well. And of course I understand that access to the Royal Library would be something you'd most definitely appreciate."

"Of course."

"It isn't the archive of the Arcane Order in Port Toli, naturally. But it is not a bad library."

"It's more than I had in Idee," Jude said. Lord Anlauf Ogmotton had given him a tower there, but it was half destroyed by the time it came into Jude's possession.

Jamis leaned across to his desk, opened the top drawer, and took out a single iron key, long and heavy, its bow worn smooth by other hands. He held it out across the table and did not let go of it at once when Jude took hold.

"The tower is yours," he said, "so long as you are willing to act as my agent in this."

"Of course."

Jamis nodded, as if a small thing had been settled, and sat back again.

"I don't think Fairwind fully understands what the Toli are about," he said. "I don't think he understands what the Scarlet Brotherhood actually is. He doesn't understand the danger in it. He—"

"He's playing politics," Jude said. "With people who are playing for keeps."

"Yes." Jamis looked at him with something like approval. "And he believes that the Toli want, in the end, to see the principalities made one nation. He believes their quarrel with Prince Jeon is about the slave trade — about Jeon's opposition to it, and the Duke of Berghof's stake in it, and Westkeep's, and the interior's. He thinks that Jeon's objection to that trade is the only thing the Toli are interested in. That is not true." He paused. "And unfortunately, I don't believe Prince Jeon himself fully understands what the Toli are doing either. I have information that the Scarlet Brotherhood is not only working its way into the principalities. It is working its way into Keoland. And the present quarrel between Keoland and the principalities is being stoked by the Brotherhood for its own ends."

Jude sat very still in the chair. He had last seen the inside of a Brotherhood temple by torchlight, with Karmirg at his shoulder and something with a red-painted face screaming words at him in a tongue older than anything in this city, and he had not been entirely himself for a season afterward. He did not say any of that. He picked up the wine instead and found that his hand was steady, which pleased him.

"Do you remember," he said, "when I spoke with the Earl of Redshore?"

"I remember your report."

"I told him Prince Jeon was against slavery. He seemed surprised. As though he'd been given to believe that Gradsul had taken up that cause, and the principalities were on the other side of it."

"That comes straight from the Duke of Gradsul," Jamis said. "The Duke has always said that the Sea Princes are morally debased because of the slave trade. That trade has gone on in Berghof since before there were Sea Princes at all. It's been there since the fall of the Suel Empire. We had nothing to do with its beginning." He was quiet a moment, and when he went on his voice was flatter. "Well. Slaves are sold in our markets. That's so. Jeon would see it ended. But it is older than our nation, and it will not die because one prince dislikes it."

The lamp on the desk guttered and steadied. Somewhere above them, through a great deal of stone, a bell in the Harbor District rang the hour, and Jude counted the strokes without meaning to.
