## PART SEVEN: DEBRIEFING IN MONMURG

# Chapter Forty-Three — What Gouge and Merrick Told Jamis

Evening found the corsair docked, and the party scattered to separate summonses before they’d so much as washed the salt from their skin. Paul and Jude were led up toward the Royal Palace and Prince Jeon’s own offices; Tyrus and Folsom answered a summons of another sort entirely — the Marine barracks in the Harbor District, and the draw of comradery and revelry that follows a victory. Gouge and Merrick were taken instead to the chambers where Jamis conducted the quieter half of his business, and, for once, admitted together.

Gouge knew the room well enough to help himself without asking — crossed to the sideboard, poured from the cheapest bottle in reach, and dropped into the chair across from Jamis’s desk. Merrick took the other, and took nothing to drink. For all the ease Gouge wore, there was a burr of shame under it yet, for the one job he’d left half-done: Fairwind, loose and alive somewhere, when the man should have died on his blade.

“Report,” Jamis said, without preamble.

“Paul made it back safely,” Gouge said — the one clean thing he had, set down first. At his shoulder Merrick inclined his head: the same.

“Thank you for that.” Something in Jamis’s bearing eased, fractionally.

“Barely,” Gouge admitted. “But safely.”

“Was his performance in question?”

Gouge turned the glass in his hand, and Merrick let him take it, content to hold his tongue; he owed Prince Jeon too much deference to be heard weighing the man’s own nephew aloud — not that he disagreed with a word of Gouge’s read, only that his own was not an opinion he cared to have known. “He’s got a proud streak to him — fair enough, given who he is. Overall he did well. Interesting young man, gaining experience fast. Whether that chip on his shoulder wears down with more time in the field, I couldn’t say for certain. I didn’t push him too hard, this time, given you had me watching him as much as fighting alongside him. I expect I can work him harder next.”

“I think it will wear,” Jamis said. He glanced to Merrick, who only nodded — it tallied with what he’d seen, and he had nothing to add to it.

“Understand, though,” Gouge went on, “everything we have of Fairwind dealing with the Toli rests on Paul’s word alone. He watched the whole of it through that clay creature of his, from another room; no other eye was on it. Witchcraft or no, I believed every word he gave me: Fairwind was dealing with the Toli, and gave up the island.”

Jamis sat with that a moment, saying nothing — a suspicion he’d carried a long while come home at last as fact, and little comfort in the confirming of it.

“I’ll not vouch for the conversation myself,” Merrick said, “not having seen it. But once we were in the room, Fairwind gave himself away well enough — defensive, going back and forth with Paul, and no innocent man in the way he fought us through the keep after.”

Between the two of them, then, they gave Jamis the whole of it, each taking up where the other left off. Merrick laid out the approach: the landing near the southern beacon, the fight for the guard tower, the sahaugin rising out of the bay and the corsair overrun where she lay — two hundred Marines dragged under, and nothing come up since. Gouge took the keep: the crossing to the garrison, the diversion at the front gate, the climb over the seaward wall — he first, Jude carrying the rest up on the air behind him. The creep to the inner sanctum, Fairwind found through the homunculus’s eyes bargaining with the Toli under no duress Paul could see. The fight that followed when they forced the door: one of the Toli killed in the sanctum, the other beaten down and taken alive; Fairwind himself trussed to a chair and none too pleased about it.

“He’d bound himself, we think,” Gouge said, “or had it done, once he heard boots in the corridor — so we’d take him for a hostage worth sparing rather than the man selling the island out from under it. It didn’t hold. He fought us the whole way down after — Folsom had walked him down under a charm, the two of them talking low the while, till the lord shook it off, shoved Folsom aside, tripped Paul into the water, and finished grappling me at the lip of the drop.” A flat pause. “I threw him over. The Toli we’d taken alive, Jude finished on the plank bridge, when Fairwind’s shout gave us away — put him down and tipped him into the water.”

“And no body,” Jamis said.

“None. We searched what we could. A shark took something down in the dark, and there were footprints later, in a cove on the Jetsom side — Jude scouted them under invisibility. Whether Fairwind drowned or swam, I couldn’t swear to you.” Gouge’s mouth thinned. “I’d sooner have known.”

That much squared between the two accounts — though where the others took Fairwind for drowned, Jamis did not. No body had surfaced, and there were the footprints; a man like that did not oblige you by dying when a swim would serve him better.

Merrick carried it the rest of the way home: the barrels down in the smuggling caves, the ballista turned on them, one cracked into the water and the sahaugin dying by the score, the great shark with them. “Five and twenty in all. We rafted four home and left one-and-twenty on the coast — enough to hold that stretch of shoreline a while. I could carry no more; we knocked a raft together from what the shore gave up and got Paul home fast, per your orders. Staying would only have left us sitting for the next thing to come.” Fairwind, he added, had admitted the theft outright — a personal stash moved to his own island, the rest handed to the Toli and gone aboard the *Sea Ghost*, which had slipped her anchorage by the time Jude looked for her.

Jamis put a few sharp questions to the pair and let them answer between them — the barrel count again, whether Fairwind had struck at Paul directly (“tripped him in the struggle,” Gouge said; “I’d not call it a direct attack — he was cordial enough, arrogant as ever, and admitted the barrels without a blush”), and whether the outer garrison had known their lord for a traitor (they hadn’t, by every sign; only the handful he’d kept closest).

“Good,” Jamis said at last, and folded his hands. “Commendable work, the both of you — everything I’d expect, and more. Now hear me. Say nothing of Fairwind’s part in this yet — to anyone, least of all his own men. I must speak with Paul, and with Prince Jeon, before any accusation is made; Jeon never believed the man capable of it. This is a matter between princes now, and I’ll not have it—”

“Before you seal our mouths,” Gouge said, “you ought to know what’s already behind them.” He reached into the pouch at his belt and set a book on the desk between them — worn, salt-swollen, older than either of them. Merrick recognized it: Admiral Flotsom’s history of the sahaugin wars, which he had taken from the inner sanctum, the account of what the yellow powder truly was and why it mattered. “Merrick handed it to me on the crossing. We’ve both read it now, cover to cover. So spare us the half-story you’d give men who don’t know better. We know what that powder is. We know what losing it means. If it’s that dire — and I read that it is — then tell us the whole of it, and trust us to carry it.”

Jamis looked from the book to the two men, and something in him settled, having the choice made for him. He nodded once, and rose, and paced a slow turn behind his desk — and when he spoke it was less a briefing than a man thinking aloud in the one room where he could afford to.

“Then I’ll not tell you what Flotsom already has. A hundred and fifty years past this nation was made — an alliance of the Duke of Berghof and the pirates of the outer isles — and it was made against the sahaugin, whose kingdoms ruled these waters and would rule them again tomorrow given the chance. The powder was the weapon that broke them, the only thing that ever did. You’ve read how thoroughly. What Flotsom could not tell you is where the thing stands now.” He stopped at the window. “Berghof stopped selling it to us long ago — claimed it was no longer needed, with our stores full and the shark-folk broken. The great reserve sat in a fortress in the middle bay, and the lease on that island lapsed, and Cain Toli took it. This past winter he cut us off cold — told us Berghof had ordered it, that the outer isles were to have no more. The same winter Gradsul’s fleet closed the blockade on Monmurg. And scarce five-and-twenty barrels answer for the whole nation now — the number you rafted home and left on that beach. If our enemies knew that figure, they’d know precisely how thin we’ve worn.”

“And no one else can make it,” Merrick said.

“No one but Berghof, who holds the manner of it and always has. Gradsul keeps a grand academy full of transmuters and not one of them comes near it.” Jamis turned back. “Here is the part that keeps me from sleep. The Duke of Berghof and the Prince of Port Toli have had nothing of consequence to do with one another in a hundred years — Berghof a famously secretive man, Toli beneath his notice. And now there is word passing directly between them. Tell me that’s chance.”

“Doesn’t sound like chance,” Gouge said.

“No. It doesn’t.” He let it sit. “Fairwind I take for a fool in all this more than a mastermind — reaching for gold and a fatter share of Jeon’s trade, jealous of everything Monmurg has become, and blind to the size of the game he’s joined. But a fool can pull the roof down as surely as a traitor. What the Toli truly want is not the powder for themselves; they want us without it. And under that sits the older thing — the slave trade out of the Amedio, the bulk of Toli wealth, which Jeon means to end. Take that from them and you take everything they are. Berghof takes his cut of it too. They’ll gamble the whole alliance to keep it, and court Gradsul to do it.” His face hardened. “There is more. A wizard came to our docks a month and a half gone — Lord Phranck, you’ll recall, whose man hired you and Karmirg to stand a watch. From him and others I’ve pieced that Cain Toli holds some artifact that commands one of the turtle dragons out of the Dragon Isles. That is what rises against our fleets when they sally — the storms, the ships that simply aren’t there afterward. It has to be recovered, if there’s any way to it. And the hobgoblins that took the Helm are Berghof’s own, hired out of his mountains by the shipload — which keeps his hand hidden behind creatures no one will trace to him. Everything on that side of the water runs back to Berghof, one road or another.” He made a short, dismissive gesture. “The Duke of Gradsul be damned — his fleet is the fist at our throat, and a fist you can see. It is the arm behind it I cannot yet make out.”

He was quiet a moment, and what came next came slower, as though he weighed whether he had the right to say it even here.

“And yet I don’t think Berghof is the head of it. Nor the Toli, nor even the Duke of Gradsul.” He turned the empty air over with one hand. “Berghof, Toli, Gradsul — three powers who’ve no love and little history between them, all leaning the same way at the same hour, toward the same end. That is not how such men move on their own. There is a shape behind the shape, I think — a hand setting them each to a task none of them would choose alone, patient, and very well hidden.” His eyes went from Gouge to Merrick and back. “I’ll not put a name to it. Not yet — not aloud, not even in this room, not until I’m surer than I am. It’s enough that you know to watch for it: when a thing makes no sense from any one prince’s greed, look for the hand you can’t see.”

“That’s a great deal to carry quiet,” Gouge said.

“It is. Carry it anyway.” Jamis gathered himself back from wherever the talking had taken him, and the room came down out of it. “Enough for tonight. Gouge — your armor’s cleaned and waiting in the back; take it and take your rest, you’ve earned the both. I’ll want you again before the week’s out.”

Gouge finished his glass, set it aside, and rose. “Aye.” He left the book where it lay on the desk, and himself did not look back at it.

Merrick made to rise with him, but Jamis lifted a hand. “A moment yet, Merrick.” He crossed the room himself and drew the door shut behind Gouge before he spoke again.

Merrick had expected it. Jamis did not keep two men to say what he meant for one.

“Your read on Gouge,” Jamis said.

“Did what he was paid to do, and did it well. Followed your orders. I’ll not turn my back to him — but on the water I trusted him.”

“Trust him exactly that far. You can rely on Gouge so long as you never cross him — he’ll knife an enemy from behind, but an ally he warns first, which is more courtesy than most in his trade can manage. Keep clear of him in his cups.” Jamis let that settle. “And the elf?”

Merrick took his time. “A great asset — I don’t think we’d have come out alive without him. His arts, a quick and clever mind. Hotheaded, though, and a law unto himself. When we were spotted and badly exposed he did what he judged he had to and asked leave of no one. And he holds life cheap — that Toli on the bridge, bound and helpless, he put down without a second thought.”

“Then you’ve taken his measure. Jude is entirely amoral — effective as you say, but he holds no loyalty to you, nor to anyone, only to himself and his own advancement. Favors are the leash I keep him on, favors and the promise of more. Let him think he stands to gain by it at your expense, and he’ll take the advantage without a blink. I don’t trust him, and I’d not have you do so either.”

“Then why let him so near Paul?”

“Jeon’s doing. The boy has a genuine taste for the arcane, and Jude’s among the ablest wizards in the principalities; Jeon means him to tutor the lad, and that homunculus may prove him right. I argued against it and lost.” His look sharpened. “This stays between us. I tell you only so you know where the ground is soft, should you find yourself among these men in the dark.”

“Knowing what they are, while they don’t know what I am to you,” Merrick said. “That’s an edge.”

“It is.” Jamis moved on before it could sit too long. “Two things more. You both marked it in your account — Folsom had the man under a charm near the end, the two of them talking low before he broke loose. Find out what passed between them. Quietly. Whatever it was, I want it.”

“And the other?”

“You’ll not go back to the Helm; I’ll send corsairs, and with your report and the barrels we’ll likely have it back inside a day or two, perhaps by parley. Your cousin Owen’s returned from Redshore — ran goods past the blockade, cider and the like, and turned a profit doing it. Find him in the Harbor District and ask what he’s seen. I believe Redshore is more than the funnel Gradsul runs the seized traders through — I want to know whether the Toli — or Fairwind — keep agents there, and Owen’s the man to draw it out of. Probe him for it, easy, without letting him feel the hook; he’ll have sailed straight through the middle of whatever’s moving on that island.”

“And my own neck, in the meantime? If Fairwind still lives, do you think he’ll try to have me killed? He knows my face, and my name.”

“He knows you, and respects you — you served near him, never under him. It wouldn’t serve a man pleading innocence to move against you. Do your duty, hold your tongue, and you’ll be well enough.”

Merrick rose, and thanked him, and went out turning over the thought of Owen Black — his own cousin, once Navy, always drawn to profit, who had smuggled into Keoland before the blockade and smuggled food out of Redshore into a starving Monmurg since. Whether the man’s loyalty ran deeper than his purse, Merrick couldn’t yet say. Jamis seemed unbothered. *Gradsul be damned*, he had all but said — and Merrick found he couldn’t disagree.

The door closed on Merrick, and Jamis did not rise.

He sat with the two empty glasses and the salt-swollen book between them, and let the room settle, and let the face he wore for his agents come off. Alone, there was no use for it.

He made himself go over the doubts one at a time, the way he made his own people account for a night’s work. Nearly all of it — the shape he had carried into that conversation and handed down to two tired men as though it were his own — had come to him from across the water, from Zafar. His wife’s brother. Zafar Azane, who sat in Gradsul at the Duke’s right hand and kept the Office of Forbidden Magic, and who had been, this year past, the one voice in the dark Jamis had let himself lean on. It was Zafar who had named the Toli’s moves before they made them — the blockade, the squeeze on Jeon, the courting of Gradsul — each foretelling coming true in its turn, until Jamis had stopped weighing them and started simply believing. Zafar who had first said the collusion plainly: Berghof and the Toli and the Duke, powers that should have had nothing to say to one another, all leaning the same way. Zafar who had breathed the name Jamis would not give Gouge and Merrick tonight — the Scarlet Brotherhood — and set it under everything, the hand behind the hands. Zafar who had marked Lord Phranck not as an enemy to be put down but as an edge that might, with care, be turned.

Every piece of it, from his wife’s brother, in the enemy’s own city.

And there was the splinter he could not work loose. For all Zafar had foreseen, he had never once named Fairwind. Not a warning, not a whisper — the betrayal nearest home, the one that had drowned two hundred Marines and lost the Helm, and Zafar’s long sight had slid past it as though it were not there. Jamis had caught Fairwind himself, watching the man’s small envies harden season on season, while Zafar’s letters spoke only of dukes and princes and secret brotherhoods.

His finger rose to his lips, shaping words that made no sound. Which was the worse thing — that Zafar, who saw so much, had truly not seen this? Or that he had seen it, and chosen not to say?

He thought, unwillingly, of the touch he had felt once upon his own thoughts — the cold, unhurried feel of another mind moving through his, never quite scrubbed away since — and found he could no longer be certain, at this hour, how much of what he believed he had reasoned for himself, and how much had been laid there gently by a hand he trusted. He had built a war on Zafar’s word. He no longer knew whether the word was Zafar’s own, or something wearing Zafar’s voice, or Zafar himself, playing a game longer than Jamis had the wit to follow.

He put the fear back where he kept such things; a spymaster who let it show was no longer any use to the men who leaned on him. But he sat a long while in the dark before he rose.

When he rose at last it was to the cabinet, and to the bottle his brother-in-law had sent — the better vintage, near all he drank these days. He took down a clean glass for it, and then stood a moment holding the two, and set the wine back unopened. He had done too much of his thinking of late through Zafar’s gifts, and through Zafar’s eyes. He filled the glass instead from the rough bottle he kept for Gouge, the red-eye the old pirate crews had sworn by under Captain Cross, and made himself drink it down — and made himself look at the whole tangled business the way a plain Oeridian of the sea principalities might, and not the way a Suel courtier in Gradsul had taught him to. It scalded going down — a hard, raw whiskey the harbor distilled to strip a throat and steady a hand, and a crude, eye-watering shock after the smooth vintage Zafar had sent. The worse drink by far, past any question. He was no longer certain it was the worse counsel.
