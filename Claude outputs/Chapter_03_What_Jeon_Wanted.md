## PART TWO: THE FOURTH DAY

# Chapter Three — What Jeon Wanted

Jude came up to the palace on the morning of the fourth of Wealsun looking entirely unhurried, as though he had slept the night through and had nowhere in particular to be. He smelled faintly of fish.

He had come down from the tower on the headland as the sun cleared the city and gone by the back lanes to Ferd's, where Gouge and the Marines lay snoring among the benches and no one looked up as he passed. In his room he collected what he wanted. He had not tranced, and did not yet feel the lack of it. He came up the hill to the palace before the markets opened, with a satchel over one shoulder and a damp bundle of rag inside his robe. The guards at the palace postern had his name. A servant brought him through a series of rooms each quieter than the last and left him at a door in the east wing, and when it opened it was Paul himself who opened it, masked, dressed, and plainly awake for some hours.

The room behind him was a sitting room with a window over the palace gardens and a good many more books in it than furniture. The mask was not the one Paul had worn on the Helm. This one was silver, smooth and close-fitted, with narrow eye-slits and nothing at all in the way of expression. A palace mask. Jude found he disliked it slightly less than the black steel one Paul had been wearing when they met.

"You're early," Paul said.

"I'm an elf. We don't sleep. We brood." Jude came in and took the chair by the window without being asked. The morning light through the glass was thin and clean, and he sat with his back to it. "I've been thinking about Fairwind."

Paul closed the door and sat across from him. "What about him?"

"I hope we didn't kill him," Jude said. "I'd rather he were alive. I don't know whether Prince Jeon and Fairwind could ever have mended it between them in any case, but if they could, it's likely the best thing that could happen to the principalities." He looked at Paul. "Did you want him dead?"

"I wanted him in custody."

"So did I. I still think it would have been for the best. That's why I tried to bring him out. But I was never sure it was what Jamis wanted." Jude turned a hand. "Jamis doesn't send Gouge along on a mission unless he wants someone dead. And Gouge had more than enough proof to justify putting an end to Fairwind, before Fairwind ever went for him on that stair. When we lost him over the edge, I couldn't have told you whether I'd watched an accident or an order carried out. And if we'd got him home, I'm not sure what Jamis would have had me do with him."

"Arrest him," Paul said. "He's a traitor. What he has done is treason. But he's a prince of the principalities, Jude, whatever else he is. He deserves better than a knife on a stair or a hot iron in some cellar. If he's to die for it, that's for the council of princes to decide. Not Jamis, and not Gouge."

"A trial before the council? Think what that would do. Fairwind on his feet before the other princes, swearing he was the loyal one and Monmurg the liar, and every prince who's ever envied Jeon choosing his side. It would split the principalities down the middle, with Keoland watching from the other side of the blockade. Jamis would never allow that. Not with the country at war." He shook his head. "And we never had time to think about it in any case. Not going down through the keep, not on the raft, not on the corsair." Jude leaned forward and put his elbows on his knees. "You said, 'We've got him.' Dead to rights, you said. I believe you. But I've never actually heard from you what you heard. What did he say?"

"I already told you."

"You told me he was dealing with the Toli. Plotting. I'm asking what you heard, exactly."

Paul was quiet a moment, and Jude watched him go back for it.

"He was talking with them about moving it," Paul said. "Getting rid of it — selling it on. The powder. And one of the Toli said, 'Well, we noticed you took most of it anyway.' He wasn't pleased about it, either. You could hear it in his voice."

"Most of it." Jude leaned forward. "Of the powder? You're certain that's what they meant?"

"Of the powder. There was nothing else worth arguing over."

"Then he wasn't simply handing it over to them." Jude nodded slowly. "He was keeping the greater part back for himself. He wanted to be the one holding the reins, and let the Toli have whatever he chose to give them."

"That's how it sounded." Paul was quiet a moment, turning something else over. "But the hobgoblins tell a different story. One of closer cooperation between Fairwind and the Toli. They could never have taken the Helm on their own. Not a fortress like that, not with a garrison inside it. Not without leaving half their number dead on the walls, and as far as we saw, they'd lost hardly anyone. Someone let them in, Jude. It had to be Fairwind."

Jude considered it. "My guess is the Toli brought them in under that fog, and up through the caves the same way the powder was going down. As for Fairwind—" He turned a hand. "He told us he had his own men give up their arms and shut them away so none of them would be killed. If that's true, he left the door open for whoever came up those stairs. Whether he knew who it would be, I can't tell you. I don't think he brought them himself."

"Don't you think he was working with the Toli?" Paul said.

"I don't disagree that he was dealing with them. But I think Fairwind was working for himself. I don't think he's working for the Toli."

"He had his own reasons, it seems." Paul was silent a moment, and then went on as though arguing against himself. "Although. He had Toli agents in the inner sanctum of the Helm, Jude. Not his house. A fortress of the principalities, held in the principalities' name. And they were standing there talking with him as if they'd been doing it for months. The powder was going down the Helm's own stairs to Cain Toli's ship. And he let the hobgoblins walk into the Helm. That isn't a man keeping the Toli at arm's length."

"No," Jude allowed. "He also knew me for one of Jamis's men the moment he saw me. He looked at me across that room as if Jamis himself were standing in it, and he didn't care. A lord who's afraid of Monmurg doesn't do that."

"Or a lord who's sure of his new friends."

"Or that." Jude turned it over. "But the Toli told him to his face that he'd taken most of it for himself, and they weren't pleased about it. Men don't scold a creature that belongs to them. They scold a partner who's been cheating the count."

"And he was tied to a chair."

"Or he'd let himself be tied to one. So that if anyone came through the door, he'd be the prisoner and not the host."

"Which is what a man does when he's playing more than one side," Paul said. "Or what a man does when he's given himself to the Toli entirely and was only haggling over his price." His fingers drummed once on the arm of his chair. "I can make it come out either way."

"So can I." Jude sat back. "Either he's theirs, body and soul, and the barrels he skimmed were his wages. Or he's playing them for all he can get, with one eye on the door back to Jeon. Everything we saw on that island fits both."

"The one thing I keep coming back to," Paul said, "is the signals."

Jude looked up. "Oh? What of them?"

"Before we sailed. They were saying it at the Admiralty, and on the corsair. The last day or two, the lamp signals from the Helm weren't right. They were sending, but the codes didn't answer. The countersigns were wrong."

"I'd thought the hobgoblins took the citadel, and then the signals went wrong because it was hobgoblins sending them." Jude said it slowly, working it through as he went. "They came in under that fog. They took the citadel. And by taking the citadel they took the salting, and the signal tower with it." He stopped. "And that's why the signals didn't match."

"But," Paul said.

"But." Jude sat forward again. "If Fairwind had really been working as closely with the Toli as all that — hand in glove — he'd have known the right countersign. He could have given it to them. Or sent it himself. The Toli would have wanted the right code going out. Nothing to alarm anyone. Everything quiet on the Helm, while the Toli took whatever they wanted and were gone over the horizon long before anyone in Monmurg thought to send a ship."

"And instead it was wrong."

They looked at each other.

It came to both of them at nearly the same moment, and it was Paul who said it, very quietly.

"He was playing the Toli and Monmurg against each other."

"He wanted Jeon to see the signals were wrong," Jude said. "At the same time, the Toli very likely thought he was sending exactly what they wanted sent. They'd not know our codes."

"He was saying, 'Warning. Something's wrong. Come out and look.'"

"When Jeon's ships arrived, he'd be the loyal lord who raised the alarm." Jude laughed shortly. "And he was, too. He did raise it."

"And it would have worked," Paul said. "If I hadn't sent the homunculus ahead. Jeon's ships come, the hobgoblins are driven off, and Fairwind is found tied to a chair with Toli agents standing over him. A loyal lord, betrayed and rescued. Nobody would ever have known he'd been talking with them as a partner. And the empty storerooms? He'd have laid every missing barrel at the Toli's door, and no one would ever have thought to ask whether he'd taken them himself." His voice was very even.

Jude sat back. "He's out for himself. Mostly for himself. This proves it." Jude turned it over again and liked it better each time. "In a way, it's as if he's saying to Jeon, 'Respect me.' Lord Jamis told me Fairwind has always been jealous of Monmurg — of Prince Jeon. So it's not that he isn't working with the Toli. Obviously he is, to some degree. But he's not in their pocket. I had the same sense of him when we had him tied. He's still his own man. If he weren't, we'd never have had that signal. We'd never have had the wrong code."

"So."

"So I think he may still be willing to deal. If what he wants is respect — and power's how he measures respect — then perhaps we take the plain road for once. Instead of creeping round behind him, we go and talk to him." Jude spread his hands. "We didn't kill him. Whatever happened to him in that chasm, it wasn't us. We only killed the two Toli."

"You killed the Toli," Paul said. "Both of them, as it happens. I didn't. I was in the water."

"I think," Jude said, "I've more Toli to kill before I'm done."

"I can see it."

"So will you, I expect, before this is finished. You'd do well to acquire a taste for it." He said it lightly, and it did not come out as lightly as he had meant, and he let it go. "In any case. It's a thought. I'd like to know what you make of it — the idea that we don't creep around Fairwind. That we try dealing with him."

"We could put it to Lord Jamis," Paul said. "And to my uncle. We could go to Jamis now."

"Not yet." Jude shook his head, and did not say why. He did not yet know which side of this Jamis stood on, and he had a growing suspicion that the spymaster would rather see Fairwind at the bottom of the sea than across a table from Jeon. That was not something to lay before Paul. Not yet. "We've a guess about Fairwind, and a good one, but it's still only a guess, and Jamis has a great many people bringing him guesses. Let's bring him something more than that. We make some headway on what we were actually set to do, the powder and where Fairwind's put his share, and then we take him our thoughts on Fairwind with something to stand beside them."

He sat back and let the silence go on for a moment. Somewhere below the window a gardener was raking gravel, a long patient scraping that came and went.

"I've never been to Fairwind's holdings," Jude said. "His island, his ports. Have you?"

"I've been to the main harbor. There was a feast there, a few years ago. I've seen what they show visitors." Paul shrugged. "I didn't go poking about. I've never toured the island."

"So if we have to go and find where he's hidden his stock, you'd have no more idea than I would where to begin."

"Not of where."

"Then let me ask you something else. What sort of man is he? Is Fairwind the kind who keeps what he values close — physically close, where he can put his hand on it? Or the kind who hides things away somewhere else entirely? A different island, somewhere far off?"

Paul considered it, and Jude watched him do it and was pleased by how he did it: not reaching for the first answer, but going back through what he had seen of the man, at the feast, in the sanctum, bound in a chair and still behaving as though the room belonged to him.

"The people of the outer islands are old pirate stock," Paul said at last. "All of them. Fairwind's house more than most. They made their fortunes on hidden coves and buried chests and cargo that never touched a customs dock. He's that school through and through. He wouldn't put it all in one place."

"Divided?"

"Divided. Several places. And if he's true to type, he'll have one place that's well defended and dangerous to get into — somewhere obvious, somewhere anyone looking would look first. That's the one he wants found. And then the others, that are simply secret."

"A decoy and a scatter."

"Yes."

"How big are the outer islands? I've never seen them charted properly. Are we speaking of a handful of rocks within a day's sail of his hall, or—"

"Hundreds of square miles," Paul said. "Not just his island. Islets, reefs, sea caves. You couldn't search it. Not with a fleet."

Jude swore quietly. "Then we don't search it. We find someone who knows."

He thought about how to put the next thing and decided to put it straight.

"You're of Jeon's family," he said.

"Yes."

"Which might mean your knowledge of some things is more limited than you'd think — because you're young, and because of how the family keeps its business. I'd not expect you to know much in particular about the Toli. Or about Fairwind. His habits. His ventures. Where he's sailed and why."

"I probably know more than most people would."

"I'm sure you do. I'm not asking for anything definite now. Only give it some thought." Jude weighed how much to say, and said a little. "For instance. I've had dealings with the Scarlet Brotherhood, in the past. If I have reason to suspect there's a close association between the Brotherhood and the Toli — and I do — it colors how I look at the Toli. A great deal."

"I don't know anything about that."

"No. That's plain enough. But you'd better start learning about them, and soon. I'm only saying it's the kind of thing I mean." Jude opened a hand. "Things you'd have heard at your uncle's table when you were growing up. Talk over dinner that went past you at the time. If we're going to find where Fairwind has hidden his barrels, we need to know where he's been. Raids he's led. Places he's put in. Waters he knows."

"Then we should go to the library," Paul said. "The Admiralty sends copies of its logs and returns there. Every prince's ship that sails under the council's commission reports where she's been. We'd find his voyages. Where he's watered, where he's careened. That would tell us more than anything I remember."

Jude looked at him with real interest. "I'll certainly be going to the library."

"You'd have to go with me to get into that part of it."

"Then I'll go with you. I appreciate it."

Paul inclined his head a quarter of an inch.

"One more thing," Paul said. "On the stair in the cave. Before I went into the water. He was talking to Folsom about something. What was he saying?"

"Folsom?" Jude frowned.

"The bard."

"Folsom." Jude repeated it once, to fix it. The name had been annoying him since last night. "Thank you." He shook his head. "I didn't hear what passed between them. Fairwind had dropped back beside the bard and had his head down, murmuring to him, from the moment we started down. The moment you went off the edge he went for Gouge, and that is when Gouge threw him over."

Paul's voice did not change, but Jude saw his hands go still on the arms of his chair. "I'd give a good deal to know what he told him."

"So would I." Jude filed it with the other things he would like to know. "Have you spoken with Prince Jeon about the Helm? Or with Lord Jamis? What did they make of it?"

"I haven't spoken to Lord Jamis," Paul said. "Only my uncle. This morning, at first light, for a little while."

"And? Did he ask you much about Fairwind?"

"He wanted to know what happened. All of it."

"And how did he take it? When you told him what Fairwind had done?"

"He was upset." Paul said it without inflection. "He didn't want to go to war with him."

"But he believed you."

"Yes. He believed me."

"You didn't have to argue with him? He wasn't making excuses for Fairwind?"

"No. Not really. He doesn't want a war. But he wants Fairwind found, and he wants him to answer for it."

"Good," Jude said. "It sounds as though he'd still like to mend things with Fairwind, if it can be done."

"He wants you and me to find him," Paul said. "Bring him to justice, if we can. Alive, not dead. But first he wants us to find the powder and work out how to make it. Find where Fairwind took his barrels, and bring them back." He paused. "And he wants you to teach me to be a wizard."

"Prince Jeon said that?" Jude knew perfectly well that he had. Jamis had put it to him the night before, across the wine, and it had been part of the price of the tower key in his pocket. But the way a man answered a question was often worth more than the answer itself, and Jude had never lost the habit of asking what he already knew.

"Yes."

Jude studied the silver mask. One flat word, and the mask gave him nothing to go with it. That would not do, and he kept probing. "And how do you feel about that?"

"I feel fine about it."

"Is it something you want?"

"Yes." Paul's hands moved very slightly on the chair arms. "It benefits the family. We aren't spellcasters, my uncle's house. We never have been. We buy magical services when we need them. One of our own who could do it would be worth a great deal to them."

"So he's trying to mend a weakness he sees in the family."

"Yes."

"And your homunculus," Jude said. "You did make that yourself? You are sure?"

"I did," Paul said. "From a book. I'm an intelligent man, I think."

"You are, but it takes more than an intelligent man to make a wizard. It wants a particular kind of mind, and very few men have it."

"I've watched you for some days now," Paul said. "On the wall, in the sanctum, on that stair. From what I've seen, I'd say I am your intellectual equal."

Jude laughed, once, in spite of himself. "I doubt that."

"I don't see you making homunculi," Paul said.

It was a fair hit and Jude let it land. He had told Paul to his face, not long ago, that the creatures were a fairground swindle, and had been shown a black clay lizard running across a ceiling for his trouble. "No," he said. "You don't. Your creation of that automaton tells me you have a bent for magic, which is a good place to start. And you're curious. That's better." He sat back. "I'll take you on as a student, then. On trial, for a season, until we both see what you're made of."

"So." Jude counted it off. "Jeon wants Fairwind found, and brought to justice alive. He wants Fairwind's barrels back. He wants the powder understood, and made, and that first, I'd think, since there's only so much of it left. And he wants you made a wizard." He looked at the silver mask a moment longer. "Very well. Where do we begin?"

"With the powder," Paul said. "My uncle told me we have a couple of months' supply left. No more than that. Everything else can wait on it."

"A couple of months. Perhaps more, if there's more to be had, and if the twenty-one barrels are still in the grotto on the Helm. They should be secured by now. Or soon." Jude frowned. "Do we need to go back to the Helm? To help drive out the rest of the hobgoblins?"

"Nothing was said about us going back," Paul said. "He asked me whether it was safe for ships to go in. He means to send them."

"With troops."

"With troops, and with barrels aboard. Jamis is to see to it."

"Then he has it in hand." Jude let out a breath. "Good. I'd go if we were needed. But I'd rather stay here and show you how to go about this kind of research. Alchemical research. It's slower than fighting hobgoblins, and it matters more." He was quiet for a moment. "Do you remember — you may not have seen it — when I was cutting the gills out of that dead sahaugin in the grotto?"

"I saw you doing something to it."

"I'd meant to use them for a water-breathing draught. It seemed a waste to leave them. And then on the way home, on the corsair, it came to me that we ought to study the way the powder had killed the creature. It went into the gills and killed it. It's almost like an acid to them."

"Right."

"So we've seen the strength of it with our own eyes. It killed that shark — and that shark was forty feet long — and it killed dozens of the sahaugin in the cove, and it did it fast. So the potency isn't in question."

"One barrel keeps the whole strait," Paul said.

"So I'm told. But what Lord Jamis has asked me to find out is whether there was something else. A counter-agent. Something that was used to undo it." Jude reached into his robe and drew out the damp bundle of rag, and the smell of it came into the room — a thick, rank reek of fish gone past its day, strong enough that Paul drew back a little behind the mask, with brine and old blood under it and something sharper and more chemical beneath that. He did not unwrap it. "So. We take these. We examine them. They'll show us what the powder does to a gill. Whether it swells them, or burns them, or congeals the blood in them. But there may also be traces left in them — if there was some counter-agent that had been used to push back an earlier salting. Remember, the salting around the Helm has to be renewed every week or so. If something had been working against it before the last barrel went in, there may be some sign of it still in the tissue."

"So we start with the gills."

"We start with the gills." Jude put the bundle away again. "Do you know the alchemist's shop, just off the square in the foreign district?"

"Of course I do."

Jude raised an eyebrow and said nothing, and let the silence say what he thought of that.

Paul's mask turned toward him and held there.

"There's a good alchemist there," Jude said, "and I've been trying to get to know him. This is one more reason to call on him. We can buy some materials. And ask whether he knows of any reagents we could use to find out if something else is present in these — something that would counter the powder, still in the flesh, even though the thing died of the powder in the end."

"But why would anyone make a counter to it?" Paul said. "Who'd want that?"

"No one in the principalities who lives by the sea would want to counteract it." Jude shook his head. "What worried Lord Jamis is this. The waters around the Helm were salted with the powder shortly before that fog came in. A salting lasts a week to ten days. So it should have kept the sahaugin off. It didn't."

"So it could have been false," Paul said. "It could have been a barrel of sand. Yellow sand, instead of the powder. Fairwind may have been taking the true barrels and putting a counterfeit in their place for months, and no one the wiser until the sahaugin came."

Jude patted his robe, where the rag bundle sat. "That's what I mean to find out. Whether Fairwind was swapping the barrels, or whether someone has found a counter-agent to the real thing. Which is why I'm going to get started on these. They won't keep."

"And we need more—" Paul stopped. "We need agents. People. Just — people to start looking."

"We do." Jude looked at him a moment, weighing something, and then set it down in front of him. "May I give you some advice?"

"Yes."

"Do you like to take it straight? Or dressed up?"

Paul's mask considered him. "Straight."

"Good," Jude said. "Then listen."
